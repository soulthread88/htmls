Scene 1 / 8

The new leader did not come as a voice. He came through someone else’s mouth.

Dr. Leila Shirazi was watching Iranian state television. An anchor in black was reading from a sheet of paper. It was the first statement of the Supreme Leader, Mojtaba Khamenei. The leader’s face was not on the screen. There was only one old photograph. The son, half hidden behind his father.

On March 12, the first statement issued in the name of the new Supreme Leader, Mojtaba Khamenei, was made public on Iranian state media, read aloud by an announcer. The statement said the Strait of Hormuz would remain closed and warned the countries of the region to stop hosting U.S. military bases. A day earlier, on March 11, the U.N. Security Council had adopted Resolution 2817, condemning Iran’s attacks on its Gulf neighbors and Jordan, by a vote of 13 in favor with 2 abstentions (China and Russia). A Russian draft that also took up the U.S. and Israeli attacks drew only four votes in favor and failed.

She listened to the Persian and took it down in English. The translation team’s draft would come in thirty minutes. She could not wait. The sentences sounded like his father’s. The same words, the same breath. One thing was different. His father’s speeches always had places to pause. Places for the crowd to shout. This statement had none. Writing done without a crowd. Writing done in a bunker. In 1989 the father had taken his seat in front of the cameras. The son took his on paper.

“Is he even alive?” Sam Farrell asked.

“A living man wrote it,” Leila said. “Who wrote it is another question.”

She moved her eyes to the right-hand screen. Fordow. 5.7 seconds. The same pulse for twelve days. The bombing had still gone around that mountain. Natanz had been quiet since its entrance buildings were blown away on March 2. Only Fordow turned. Under a mountain nobody hit.

One sentence of the statement caught in her ear. The strait will stay closed. There was no Fordow. Nothing nuclear. The new leader had put one of the cards he held on the table. The other he kept buried under the mountain.

On the desk was a figure from the UN refugee agency that had come in this morning. People who had left their homes inside Iran: three million. Was her aunt Fereshte one of the three million? Where would a seventy-year-old woman go with three cats? Leila bet her aunt had stayed. Her aunt was not a woman who left her roses. The numbers count the people who leave. They do not count the ones who stay.

She wrote the first line of the report. New Supreme Leader’s first statement—presents closure of the strait as the regime’s first commitment. No mention of the nuclear program. Note the absence.

Some days what goes unsaid is the loudest thing. The analyst measures the frequency of that silence.

March 12, 2026 (Thu.) — War Day 13
Scene 2 / 8

The enemy did not come as a missile. It came up a dryer exhaust duct.

Captain Marcus Webb heard the fire alarm on the bridge. At first he thought it was a drill. Then the announcement came. Not a drill. Laundry spaces. Smoke was spreading through the ventilation.

On March 12, fire broke out in a dryer exhaust duct in the laundry spaces of the USS Gerald R. Ford, then operating in the Red Sea. The automatic fire-suppression system reportedly failed to activate, and the crew put the fire out by hand. Two were injured. Berthing compartments burned, and more than 600 crew members lost their bunks and personal belongings. Fighting the fire and cleaning up after it took thirty hours. The Ford would later leave its operating area for repairs, bound for Souda Bay on Crete and then for Split, Croatia. The same day, in western Iraq, a U.S. Air Force KC-135 aerial refueling tanker crashed, killing all six crew members.

He went down to Damage Control Central. The red compartments on the plan went from one to two to four. Fire did not care about people or rank. On a hundred-thousand-ton ship fire is an older enemy than missiles. The Navy shows every recruit the film of the Forrestal’s flight deck in 1967. For twenty-five years he had learned how to fight that enemy. Close the bulkheads. Set the boundaries. Take back one compartment at a time.

The men on the hoses were cooks, deckhands, yeomen. In front of a fire everyone is a firefighter. That was the law of a ship. Webb watched the faces coming out of the smoke. Soot. Red eyes. Twenty, twenty-one. Kids who had been launching aircraft eighteen hours a day for thirteen days were now putting out the fire in their own bunks.

After it was out he walked the burned compartments. Bunk frames melted together. Wet ash. Locker doors frozen open. On the deck someone’s family photograph was stuck down, half burned. He reached to peel it up and pulled his hand back. If he peeled it, it would crumble.

“Six hundred with no place to sleep, Captain,” the XO said.

“Put cots in the hangar bay. Give them my cabin too,” Webb said. “Write the families first. Start with the sentence that everybody’s alive.”

He went back to the bridge and touched the paper in his breast pocket once. The days your hands are still are the ones that scare me most, Captain. Today his hands were not still. They had soot on them, and they shook a little. Strangely, that was a relief.

It is not always the enemy that pulls a ship back. Sometimes a dryer that has run for nine months does it.

Tehran — an alley off Enqelab Street
March 13, 2026 (Fri.) · 11:30 a.m. (Tehran local time) — War Day 14 · Quds Day
Scene 3 / 8

It was Friday. The last Friday of Ramadan.

Soraya Tahir leaned on the shutter of a closed bookstore at the mouth of the alley and watched the avenue. People went by. Flags. Pictures of the dead leader. Pictures of the new one. A loudspeaker threw out the slogans and the marchers shouted them back. Quds Day. The march held every year. This year it was under bombing. Enghelab, the street named for revolution. In the winter of 1979 this street had been full of people carrying pictures too.

On March 13, while the Quds Day rallies were under way, explosions were reported in Tehran, and one person was killed by shrapnel. The same day, U.S. forces bombed Kharg Island, the hub of Iran’s crude oil exports. President Trump said every military facility on the island had been destroyed. The U.S. State Department’s Rewards for Justice program posted rewards of up to $10 million on ten members of Iran’s leadership, Mojtaba Khamenei among them.

Soraya tried to hate the march. It was the march of the system that had locked her up in 2022. But she could see faces. A woman pushing a stroller. An old man on a cane. A man with a bandage around one arm. Who had come because they believed and who had come because they were told to, she could not tell. They probably did not know exactly themselves. In a country where bombs fall, belief and fear and anger clot into one lump.

There was an explosion in the distance. The march faltered. A few people looked up at the sky. Then they walked on. The slogans got louder. At that moment Soraya understood. Those people were doing the same thing she did. Raising their voices in front of something frightening. Only the songs were different.

Her friend came up and stood beside her.

“The firewood?”

“In the basement. Four days to go,” Soraya said.

“You think we can do it in front of those people? The bonfires?”

Soraya did not answer. The march of slogans went down the avenue, and the people of the spring song were hiding in the alleys. The two marches lived in the same city. They were hit by the same bombs. And they feared each other most.

Bombing does not make a country one. It only pushes two countries under one roof.

Bahrain — Manama, a hotel room serving as the Associated Press’s temporary bureau
March 13, 2026 (Fri.) · 11:20 p.m. (Bahrain local time) — night of War Day 14
Scene 4 / 8

Sarah Callahan was sitting in the bathtub, writing her story.

The safest place in this hotel was the bathroom with no window. She spread a folded towel and put the laptop on her knees. Two nights ago a fuel tank had burned at the airport on Muharraq Island. The light had turned the curtains in her room orange. The Gulf had not burned like this since the winter of 1991, when Kuwait’s oil wells burned. Every night now the cities of the Gulf waited to see which tank would burn.

The satellite phone rang. It was the call she had been waiting for. A number she had through Lieutenant Kim Junho. The second mate of a tanker anchored deep inside the Gulf. From Kerala, India, twenty-nine years old. They had agreed she would not use his name.

“What can you see today?” she asked.

“The northern horizon’s red.” The voice on the phone broke up and came back. “That’s toward Kharg. Since this afternoon. We’re two hundred kilometers off and we can see the sky.”

“What do you have left on board?”

“Eleven days of water. More rice than that. Vegetables ran out last week. The captain cut us to two meals a day. That’s not the problem.”

“What is the problem?”

“We’re sitting on two million barrels of oil, ma’am.” He laughed. It was not a laugh. “Every night you hear drones. Nobody knows whose. Every time one goes over, twenty-three men stop breathing. Every day I write my mother a text. I can’t send it. I just write it.”

Sarah took it down. This time she took out her notebook. This man wanted to be written down. He wanted someone to know they were there.

“People write that the strait is blocked,” he said. “We are not blocked. We are forgotten. Please write it that way.”

The line went dead. Sarah deleted her first sentence and wrote a new one. Inside the Gulf, on hundreds of ships, thousands of sailors are waiting for the war on land to end. They are on no side. They have no side. They have water and rice and texts they cannot send.

On the map of a war only the warships are drawn. The ships floating between them, on nobody’s side, are not.

Scene 5 / 8

The cable from Seoul was one line. Report on-scene assessment. By 1800 today (Seoul).

Lieutenant Kim Junho knew what that one line was asking. Last night the American president had told a number of countries to send warships to Hormuz. Britain, France, China, Japan. And South Korea. Seoul would have been up all night. Somewhere in the chain of approval somebody would have said it. Don’t we have an officer of ours in Bahrain?

On March 14, President Trump called on a number of countries to send warships to help secure the Strait of Hormuz amid Iran’s closure, saying he expected Britain, France, China, South Korea, and Japan to take part. The same day, the Indian tankers Shivalik and Nanda Devi passed safely through the strait carrying 92,700 tons of LNG. Their passage followed consultations between India’s external affairs minister, Jaishankar, and Iran’s foreign minister, Araghchi. On March 16, Iran would say the strait was open to everyone except the United States and its allies.

A twenty-nine-year-old lieutenant had no business judging whether his country should send ships. But he was a man who counted ships every day, and the numbers were talking to him.

He stood at the whiteboard. On the left, the ships hit near the strait in the last two weeks. Skylight. MKD Vyom. Prima. Mayuree Naree. Star Gwyneth. One Majesty. On the right, the two ships that went through yesterday. Shivalik. Nanda Devi. Indian flags.

The strait was not closed. It had a doorman. Ships from countries that could talk to Tehran went through; ships from countries that could not got hit. Which line would a country that sent warships stand in? And what kind of target would that make the Korean sailors trapped inside the Gulf?

He wrote the report. Three paragraphs. The first, facts. The second, the Indian case. In the third his hand stopped. In 2011 in the Gulf of Aden the navy had taken back one hijacked ship. This time the other side was not pirates. It was a whole sea. It was not a sentence for a lieutenant to write. But there was what his father had said. That a soldier who loses the strength to think is no longer a soldier.

In this officer’s judgment, apart from the question of whether to dispatch naval vessels, the first priority is to secure a channel of consultation with Iran for the safe transit of our vessels and seafarers now inside the strait. A warship can escort a ship. It cannot bring out a ship that is already trapped.

He read the paragraph three times. He did not delete it. He sent it.

Only after it was sent did he notice his hands were shaking. It was not the missiles. It was the tremor of a man who, for the first time, had put his own name behind an opinion.

War asks a soldier for courage not only in front of a gun but in the third paragraph of a report.

Tehran — Imam Khomeini Hospital, nurses’ dormitory, third floor
March 15, 2026 (Sun.) · 9:30 a.m. (Tehran local time) — morning of War Day 16
Scene 6 / 8

Nariman was seven years old.

The child was sitting by the window. The sun drawn on the glass had long since been wiped away. The black smoke had thinned but the sky was not its own color yet. Now and then the sound of a plane, now and then a far-off boom. Between them the hospital generators hummed.

For the first time in sixteen days Maryam was taking a day off. Last night: a nine-hour shift, two transfusions, one amputation. The head nurse had pushed her out the door. If you collapse you’re no use to anyone. She sat on the bed and looked at the back of her daughter’s head. It had been four days since she washed her hair.

“Maman,” Nariman said. “When is Baba coming?”

Maryam was quiet for a moment. Her husband, Davood, was a sergeant in the Revolutionary Guards. He had been called up before dawn on the first day of the war and gone out. Twelve days ago, one phone call. He said he was alive. When she asked where he was, instead of answering he asked her to put Nariman on. The child was asleep. Every day Maryam regretted not waking her.

“Soon,” she said.

The child accepted that. At seven you do not know yet when soon is. It could be this afternoon, or tomorrow, or a time the grown-ups have not told you about yet. In the 1980s, when they were fighting Iraq, the children of this city heard the same word in basements. Those children were the grown-ups in this hospital now.

There were two days of milk left in the dormitory’s shared refrigerator. Bread came from the hospital cafeteria, but it got thinner every day. The shop across the street had had its shutter down for five days. The pharmacy did not have Nariman’s allergy medicine. The pharmacist said next week. Maryam knew. Next week was one kind of soon too.

On March 12, UNHCR announced that the war had driven three million people from their homes inside Iran. Hospitals were not spared, either. The records of the human rights group HRANA list, as facilities damaged in airstrikes, Besat Hospital and a surgical center in Tehran on March 4, Persian Gulf Martyrs Hospital in Bushehr on March 11, and Bustan Hospital in Ahvaz on March 12.

Nariman turned her head.

“Maman, when the war is over, can we have ice cream?”

Maryam laughed. It did not seem strange that a laugh came out. Between bombs a child asks about ice cream. That was life. That was why all this had to end. Ice cream. Nothing at all. So, everything.

“Of course,” Maryam said. “The biggest one they have. The three of us, with Baba.”

She would have done better not to say the last part. But it was already said. Nariman held up three fingers and looked out the window again. Three fingers. That promise was hers now.

Alborz Mountains, Iran — north of Tehran, Iranian Air Defense Force command post
March 15, 2026 (Sun.) · 2:00 p.m. (Tehran local time) — War Day 16
Scene 7 / 8

Colonel Darius Sepahvand read the damage tally three times.

Thirty-one years of service. Two wars. Seven decorations. The decorations would be somewhere in a drawer in the Tehran apartment. Decorations go where service piles up. But what is at the end of all that service, the decorations do not say.

On March 14, the Israeli military announced it had killed the two acting heads of the intelligence directorate of Khatam al-Anbiya Central Headquarters. They were the men who had taken over the post after their predecessor, Saleh Asadi, was killed. On March 15, the Revolutionary Guards said they had launched what they called their “50th wave of attacks” at U.S. bases in the UAE, Bahrain, and Kuwait. The same day, joint U.S.–Israeli airstrikes killed 15 people in Isfahan, security-force deployments increased across the country, and pro-government rallies continued in Tehran’s big squares.

The count was simple. Two search radars left in his sector. Eleven interceptors he could use. The commander who took over the post above him was killed in a few days, and the one who took over after him was killed too. An army where the higher you went the shorter you lived. He was a colonel, and colonels were still near the bottom of the target list. It was no comfort.

Yesterday he had talked to Narges. Forty seconds. A twenty-four-year-old software engineer. A daughter who had been dragged off twice by the morality police in 2022 and come back. He told her to get out. North, to her grandmother’s.

“I’m not going,” his daughter said. “Who’ll carry water up for the old lady downstairs? Father, don’t tell me to run. Just answer me one thing. Who are you guarding right now? Us, or them?”

He could not answer. The line went dead. Whether it had dropped or he had hung up, he could not tell.

In thirty-one years Darius had never once been asked that question. Guarding the sky meant not asking whom you guarded. Bring down what flies in and everyone under it lives. He had loved that simplicity. In the winter of 2020, on the day his own country’s air-defense missile brought down an airliner in the sky over Tehran, that simplicity cracked for the first time. Now his daughter had split it in two. Us, and them.

For the first time he thought his daughter was right. The moment he thought it he pushed it away. Thirty-one years of service had built the muscle for pushing such thoughts away. But pushing a thought away does not make it go. It only backs off. Until next time.

He sent up his report. Radars remaining: 2. Interceptors: 11. Cumulative personnel losses: 14. Resupply requested.

That was the report he could write. What he wanted to write was something else. That—he could not write yet.

When duty and conscience look in different directions, what is a soldier? He had thought thirty-one years would let him dodge that question, and on day sixteen he met it. Next time he would have to answer.

Tehran — University of Tehran, Department of Philosophy lecture hall, Building 16, Room 204
March 15, 2026 (Sun.) · 4:00 p.m. (Tehran local time) — War Day 16 · five days before Nowruz
Scene 8 / 8 — Final

Sunday, four in the afternoon. The hour he would normally have taught. Professor Azad Moradi came to the lecture hall.

The university had stopped all classes on the third day of the war. He knew that. That there were no students. That there was no lecture. He came anyway. When the Cultural Revolution of 1980 shut the doors of the universities, the lecture halls had been empty like this too.

Room 204 was empty. Sixty chairs. Last semester’s chalk was still on the board. A line from Heraclitus. Panta rhei—everything flows. Nobody had erased it. Soot had settled on the windowsill.

Azad stood at the lectern. He looked out at the empty chairs. And — he began the lecture.

“In the Republic Plato asked: can a just man live in an unjust state? That is today’s subject. Because all of us are living inside that question now.”

He spoke to the empty chairs. He did not lower his voice. He spoke at his usual pace. There were things that had to be said even if the students somewhere in this city—at home, or gone from the city, or on rooftops, or standing at checkpoints—were not listening.

Nowruz was five days off. March 20. He had seen seventy-two of them. Even the year of the revolution, even in the eight years of war with Iraq, spring had made people set the table. Apples, garlic, vinegar, sprouted wheat. He began to count how many houses in this city would be left to set that table this year, and stopped.

“Plato did not give an answer. Socrates drank the hemlock in prison. The man who taught justice died at the hands of his own state. But the dialogues remained. The words remained. If that is Plato’s answer, it is an answer.”

Beside the panta rhei on the blackboard he wrote something new.

Logos menei — the word remains.

As he put down the chalk there was a sound by the back door. The custodian. A man of about sixty stood in the doorway with a broom. There was no telling how long he had been listening.

“Professor,” the custodian said. “Will you be teaching next week as well?”

“I will,” Azad said.

The custodian nodded. He straightened one chair in the back row and went out.

War brings down buildings. But a sentence on a blackboard—stays, unless somebody comes and erases it.

That same evening, Seoul. Kang Yujin watched the debate over sending ships on the lab television. The panel talked about Hormuz and not about the sailors trapped in the Gulf. She turned the sound down and went back to her own screen. The answer the model had given three days ago—Not moving—had been wrong yesterday. Two dots flying Indian flags had slipped out through the strait. The model knew about warships and missiles. It did not know about phone calls between foreign ministers. At the bottom of the list of input variables she added one more line. Negotiation. How do you turn a phone call into a number?

War Situation
The Weight on the Ground — What a War in the Sky Leaves on the Earth

For sixteen days this war was fought in the sky. The human rights group HRANA recorded hundreds of airstrikes a day: 312 on March 12, 285 on March 14, 494 on March 15. But the weight of the war piled up on the ground. According to UNHCR, three million people had left their homes inside Iran, and seven hundred thousand in Lebanon.

In Lebanon a ground war was already under way. Israeli ground forces crossed the border on March 4, and the fighting with Hezbollah went on around Khiam and Houla. Lebanon’s Health Ministry counted 886 people killed and 2,141 wounded in Israeli attacks between March 2 and 16.

At sea, too, the center of gravity was shifting. On March 13, U.S. forces bombed Kharg Island, through which most of Iran’s crude exports pass. On March 14, the United States called on its allies, and even on China, to send warships to the strait. The same day, two Indian tankers slipped out through the strait after diplomatic consultations. A road opened with bombs and a road opened with a phone call lay side by side on the same day.

Author’s Note
Mojtaba Khamenei’s First StatementReal event / March 12
The first statement issued in the new Supreme Leader’s name was read not by the man himself but by a state television announcer. It said the Strait of Hormuz would remain closed and warned the countries of the region to stop hosting U.S. military bases. Leila’s analysis document is the author’s invention.
Security Council Resolution 2817Real event / March 11
Led by Bahrain, the resolution condemned Iran’s attacks on its Gulf neighbors and Jordan. It was adopted by a vote of 13 in favor and none against, with 2 abstentions (China and Russia). A separate draft submitted by Russia failed, with 4 in favor (Russia, China, Pakistan, Somalia), 2 against (the United States, Latvia), and 9 abstentions.
The Fire Aboard the FordReal event / March 12
The fire, which began in a dryer exhaust duct in the laundry spaces, injured two and cost more than 600 crew members their bunks. Bringing it under control and cleaning up took about 30 hours. The Ford was later repaired at Souda Bay, Crete, and at Split, Croatia. The ship left port in June 2025, and its deployment ultimately ran 326 days, the second longest in the history of U.S. aircraft carriers. The commanding officer Marcus Webb and the details of the fire scene are the author’s invention.
Kharg IslandReal place / Iran
An island in the northern Persian Gulf; most of Iran’s crude oil exports pass through its terminal. U.S. forces bombed it on March 13.
Quds DayReal observance / Iran
A march in solidarity with the Palestinians, held on the last Friday of Ramadan. Khomeini established it in 1979. In 2026 it fell on March 13.
The Gatekeeper of HormuzReal developments
On March 14, two Indian tankers passed through the strait after consultations between the Indian and Iranian foreign ministers, and on March 16 Iran said the strait was open to everyone except the United States and its allies. That same March 14, President Trump called on a number of countries, South Korea among them, to send warships. Kim Junho’s report and its contents are the author’s invention and do not depict the actual judgment of the South Korean government.
The Seafarers of the GulfReal situation · fictional character
Since the start of the war, a great many merchant ships have been stranded inside the Gulf. The Indian second mate Sarah speaks with is a character invented by the author.
DavoodFictional character
Maryam’s husband and Nariman’s father. A sergeant in the Revolutionary Guards. He was called up before dawn on the first day of the war; a single phone call on March 3 is the last word from him.
Panta Rhei · Logos MeneiPhilosophical terms
Panta rhei (πάντα ῥεῖ) means “everything flows” and is the phrase that sums up the thought of Heraclitus. “Logos menei” (the word remains) is Professor Azad’s own coinage, set against it; it is not a quotation from the classics.
