Asaluyeh, Bushehr Province, Iran — South Pars gas processing complex
March 18, 2026 (Wed.) · 2:10 p.m. Tehran time — War Day 19
Scene 1 / 9

It was midday. The sun was so bright that at first you could not see the fire.

Reza Karimi. Forty-one. Control-room engineer at Processing Plant 4. Sixteen years in this complex. Along forty kilometers of coast stood the silver towers and the pipes and the flare stacks. Gas came up from three thousand meters under the sea, and here it was washed and split and sent out to heat half the country. He did not call the place a plant. He called it the heart.

The lunch shift was over. He poured a glass of tea. The surface of the tea shook first. Then the floor. Then the sound came.

At 2:10 p.m. on March 18 the Israeli Air Force bombed South Pars, the largest gas field in the world, and the refining and petrochemical facilities at Asaluyeh. Storage tanks, gas installations, and pipelines were hit. Plant 4 was almost completely destroyed and Plant 7 badly damaged. The two plants, with a combined capacity of 100 million cubic meters a day, stopped working; 12 percent of Iran’s total gas production was hit, and Iran cut off the gas it had been sending to Iraq. No deaths were immediately reported. It was confirmed that Israel and the United States had planned the attack together and that the United States had coordinated and approved it.

A coworker pulled him by the arm into the evacuation truck. Somebody shouted not to look back. He looked back. The plant whose every valve he had learned in sixteen years stood inside an orange column. It was brighter than the midday sun.

This was not a military site. He knew that better than anyone. The gas that left here went to the boilers of Tehran’s hospitals, to the bread ovens of Tabriz, to his mother’s stove in Shiraz. The side that dropped the bombs called this place the Revolutionary Guards’ money. That was not all wrong. But in the fall of 1980, when the Abadan refinery burned under Iraqi shells, the ones who shivered through that winter were not the Guards. They were the people standing in the fuel-ration lines. A country’s heart sends blood everywhere. To the good and to the bad.

The man who aims at the heart says he is aiming at the fist. But when the heart stops, it is not only the fist that goes cold.

Energy War Background · Energy War Context
South Pars — Why the World Held Its Breath

South Pars is a single huge gas field lying under the seabed in the middle of the Persian Gulf. The Iranian side is called South Pars, the Qatari side North Dome. It is the largest natural gas field in the world. A large share of the gas Iran uses, and nearly all the LNG Qatar sells to the world, comes from this one reservoir.

Hitting this field crossed one of the taboos of the war. The market reacted at once. On March 18 alone oil jumped from $103 to $108 a barrel, and European gas prices rose 7 percent. Qatar called the attack dangerous and irresponsible. Oman and the UAE condemned it too. Iran’s President Pezeshkian warned that the uncontrolled consequences would engulf the whole world.

And a few hours later Iran fired missiles at the other side of the same field—Qatar’s Ras Laffan.

Qatar — Ras Laffan Industrial City
March 18, 2026 (Wed.) · night, Qatar time — War Day 19
Scene 2 / 9

Park Jeongwoo. An engineer a Korean engineering company had sent to this desert.

This was his third year living here. Ras Laffan. A city of LNG plants rising at the edge of the desert. One in every five barrels of the liquefied gas the world used went out from these docks. At night the flames on the flare stacks stood up like lighthouses. He liked those flames. They meant somebody’s winter on the other side of the earth was warm. Among those somebodies were the apartment blocks of Seoul.

On March 2 the complex had already been hit once. Production had stopped since. The people who stayed guarded the equipment and waited for the day they could light it again. This afternoon the news came that Asaluyeh was burning. The Filipino head cook in the canteen crossed himself. Nobody spoke. They all knew. Where the answer would come.

The alarm went off. It was not a drill.

On March 18 Iranian missiles fell on Qatar’s Ras Laffan Industrial City, causing fires and heavy damage. QatarEnergy said it had suffered extensive damage. Qatar declared the Iranian embassy’s military and security attachés persona non grata and gave them twenty-four hours to leave. The same day Saudi Arabia intercepted six ballistic missiles aimed at Riyadh and the Eastern Province, and the UAE responded to 13 ballistic missiles and 27 drones and shut down a gas facility in Abu Dhabi.

Park Jeongwoo sat on a bench in the shelter and opened his phone. Messages from headquarters in Seoul came pouring in. Messenger, email, texts. They all said one thing. Status report. Damage assessment. Headcount. Whether the people were all right was on the last line.

Through a crack in the door he could see outside. Flames were standing up. At first it was hard to tell whether it was a stack flame or a bomb fire. In a city of energy, flames were everyday. But a stack flame does not spread like that. Three years of watching told him so.

“What do we report to Seoul?” asked Lee Minjun, the junior engineer. His voice was cracking.

Park Jeongwoo thought for a moment. In the winter of 1997 Seoul had once collapsed over a few lines of numbers in a report.

“All personnel evacuated. Fire visually confirmed in the direction of Zone 3. Equipment damage unconfirmed. Further report after site control is lifted.”

“That’s it? Headquarters is asking when we restart.”

“What you don’t know, write that you don’t know. Write only what you saw. Right now Seoul sets the price of gas from one line we write.”

What a battlefield needs is not courage. It needs a hand that writes down fear and fact separately.

Tehran — Professor Azad Moradi’s apartment
March 18, 2026 (Wed.) · 3:14 p.m. Tehran time — War Day 19
Scene 3 / 9

Professor Azad Moradi was cooking a late lunch.

Another explosion. He was used to them now. The getting used to them was what was strange. That a man could be trained to explosions. That was the quietest violence war did to people.

He turned on the radio. State radio. The announcer’s voice shook. It was the fourth time in this war he had heard that shaking. The first was the leader’s death. The second and third were the two names yesterday at dawn. The fourth was now.

Israel said it had killed Iran’s Intelligence Minister Esmail Khatib in a night strike on Tehran before dawn on March 18, and President Pezeshkian confirmed his death. The same day thousands of people in Tehran joined Ali Larijani’s funeral procession. Israel also hit at least five Iranian navy vessels in the Caspian Sea. The target was the arms route between Russia and Iran.

Azad took the pot off the flame. He could not eat. It was not grief. He was not a man to grieve for an intelligence minister. That ministry had taken two of his students, and one had not come back. What he felt was vertigo at the speed. The man of yesterday was gone today, and the man of today would be gone tomorrow. The country was being peeled from the top, one layer at a time. It had been like this in 1979 too. Then people believed they knew what would come up from underneath. Now nobody could see underneath.

Outside the window a procession went by. Black clothes following Larijani’s coffin. He stood at the window and watched. The two of them had written their doctoral dissertations in the same year. One read Kant and went to the lecture hall. The other read Kant and went to power. They had never liked each other. But there was one fewer person in this country to argue the Critique of Pure Reason in Persian. To him that was not a small thing.

“Khatib too,” he said. He was talking to himself.

He was a philosopher. All his life he had practiced thinking alone. Today the alone was heavy.

People dying in Tehran is not news anymore. News is what you did not expect. What you expected is just reality. This city has too much reality now.

Langley, Virginia — CIA Iran Operations Center
March 18, 2026 (Wed.) · 4:30 p.m. EDT / midnight March 19 in Tehran — War Day 19
Scene 4 / 9

On Dr. Leila Shirazi’s screen, things one man had said on the same day were lined up side by side.

Right after the South Pars strike, President Trump wrote on Truth Social that Iran was the world’s leading state sponsor of terror and that the United States was rapidly bringing it down. Afterward he said he had known nothing about the attack in advance, said Israel had lashed out in anger and slammed into a major facility, and said there would be no more attacks on Iran’s energy facilities. Later he changed his account, saying he had known beforehand but had told Israel not to do it.

She read the three statements from top to bottom. Then from bottom to top. Whichever way she read them, they did not add up to one man’s day. When the enemy’s words contradict each other, an analyst looks for the truth in the gap. What you look for when your own president’s words contradict each other, nobody had ever taught her.

In the morning she had sent up a memo.

The South Pars strike succeeded as a short-term disruption but has created a strategic burden. (1) Iranian retaliation against Qatar is already under way. (2) The IRGC is assessed to hold contingency plans against Saudi and UAE energy facilities—likely to be executed on escalation. (3) Recommendation: establish an immediate mutual red line on energy infrastructure. Once an energy war starts it cannot be contained.

No answer came. The president’s post came instead. Maybe that was the answer.

She remembered three months ago. A target review meeting. When the Asaluyeh coordinates came up on the screen she had raised her hand. It’s dual-use. The civilian dependence far outweighs the military contribution. Hit it and the Iranian people don’t get through the winter. The minutes said “Analyst’s view heard.” That one line was where the view was buried. In 1953 this agency had replaced Tehran’s prime minister. Then too the decision was made in a conference room, and the winter after was lived through by the people of Tehran.

On one side of the screen the satellite image of the Evin district was still up. Aunt Fereshte. For nineteen days, since the war began, the phone had not gotten through. The building was standing. Every day she counted that roof. That the roof was standing and that the person under it was alive. The satellite did not close the distance between the two.

What an intelligence officer fears most is not the enemy’s silence. It is her family’s. The satellite cannot break that silence.

Bahrain — Manama, a café outside U.S. Fifth Fleet headquarters
March 19, 2026 (Thu.) · 5:40 p.m. Bahrain time — War Day 20
Scene 5 / 9

There was a number now.

Sarah Callahan turned her phone screen toward Lieutenant Kim Junho. Breaking news out of London. The International Maritime Organization had held an extraordinary session. About thirty-two hundred ships trapped west of the strait. About twenty thousand seafarers.

On March 18 the International Maritime Organization (IMO) held an extraordinary session to discuss humanitarian passage for about 3,200 ships and about 20,000 seafarers stranded west of the Strait of Hormuz. On March 19 U.S. forces began air operations against Iranian vessels and drones in the strait. The same day Kuwait’s Mina Al-Ahmadi refinery caught fire after an Iranian drone strike, and Israel’s Bazan refinery in Haifa was also hit by missiles. One U.S. F-35, reportedly hit over Iran, made an emergency landing at a U.S. base in the Middle East.

“Twenty thousand.” Kim Junho read the number out loud. “The ones I count aren’t even one percent of that.”

“The ones you count have names,” Sarah said. “Twenty thousand don’t have names. The bigger the number gets, the less people feel. The curse of my profession.”

On the café television the Haifa refinery was burning. Yesterday Asaluyeh, that night Ras Laffan, this morning the refinery in Kuwait. The fire was going around the Gulf. In the tanker war of 1987, ships got out of this sea only by borrowing another country’s flag.

“As of today the U.S. is hunting over the strait,” Kim Junho said, lowering his voice. “Drones and fast attack craft. But Ms. Callahan, catching fast attack craft from the sky doesn’t get ships out. It isn’t the fast attack craft the shipowners are afraid of. It’s the number on the insurance policy.”

“Then what gets the ships out?”

Instead of answering he turned his teacup. He knew the answer. He had already written it up and sent it once. What came back was silence.

“A phone call,” he said finally. “The way India did it. Somebody calling Tehran.”

“In Tehran there are fewer people every day to take that call.”

The two of them watched the television. A crawl ran along the bottom of the screen. Intelligence minister’s death confirmed. Kim Junho did the arithmetic. Every time a bomb fell, the captains whose names he knew got one day further from home. How many people in this war were doing that arithmetic?

The side that makes war counts the enemy. The side trapped in war counts the days. The two numbers have never been written in the same ledger.

The Mediterranean — the U.S. aircraft carrier USS Gerald R. Ford (CVN-78), bound for Souda Bay, Crete
March 19, 2026 (Thu.) · night — War Day 20
Scene 6 / 9

Captain Marcus Webb was moving away from the war.

On the hangar deck six hundred cots stood in rows. The smell of the burned compartments still rode the vents through the whole ship. Wet ash and melted plastic. The ship was on its way to be repaired. The orders said “temporary departure from the area of operations.” One sheet of paper had turned the ship’s head.

He read the evening intelligence summary. South Pars. Ras Laffan. Haifa. Kuwait. And one F-35 hit over Iran. The pilot had brought the aircraft back and landed it, they said. He read that sentence twice. A stealth aircraft had been hit. It meant the sky in this war no longer belonged to one side.

How much of this war do we actually have in our hands. He wrote it in his notebook. It was not for anyone to see. Our ally hit something we told them not to, they say. Or hit something we approved, they say. Which one, I find out from the news. The enemy doesn’t hit us, it hits our friend’s gas plant. Our friend throws out the enemy’s diplomats. Everyone is firing and no one has a hand on the stick. On the bridge of the Vincennes in 1988 everyone trusted the screen too. And an airliner came down.

And right now I am taking my ship out of the middle of it. Because of a laundry room.

He drew a line through the last sentence. Wrote again. There are forty-five hundred people on my ship. Kids who haven’t been home in nine months. Bringing them back whole is the mission too. Maybe the only mission I have left that is clear.

In his breast pocket were two pieces of paper. Sarah’s note. And her short cable from Bahrain yesterday. An interview request. The sixth one. He had said no five times. This time he did not write an answer. He folded the paper and put it away. It was the first time he had not said no.

Nowhere is the war easier to see than from the bridge of a ship pulling back. You have to be far away to see the whole. And the whole is more frightening than any piece you saw up close.

Tehran — Imam Khomeini Hospital, nurses’ dormitory, third floor
March 20, 2026 (Fri.) · 7:30 a.m. Tehran time — War Day 21 · the morning of Nowruz
Scene 7 / 9

Maryam came up to the room after the night shift.

When she opened the door Nariman was awake. A seven-year-old knew how to wait for the New Year. The moment the year turned was 6:16 this evening. Yesterday Maman had pointed to it on the calendar. The child knew the table had to be set by then. Last year Baba had set it.

Maryam was worn out. Still she saw it. On the small table by the window, the things her daughter had laid out by herself.

One apple. From the hospital canteen, saved. One candle. Put away in a drawer for the power cuts. Two coins. And on wet cotton in a paper cup, lentils with sprouts the size of a fingernail. Maryam did not know where the lentils had come from. Later she found out. For ten days the child had been taking a spoonful of the lentils from her meals and soaking them in water.

“Maman, I set the table,” Nariman said. “I couldn’t do seven. It’s four.”

Haft-sin is the table set for Nowruz with seven things whose Persian names begin with the letter sin (س): sprouted grain (sabzeh), apple (sib), garlic (sir), vinegar (serkeh), sumac (somaq), sweet wheat pudding (samanu), and oleaster fruit (senjed). Coins, candles, a mirror, goldfish, and painted eggs are set out with them. The sprouts stand for new life, the apple for health, the candle for light. In 2026 the spring equinox fell at 6:16 p.m. Tehran time on March 20.

Maryam took a long breath so as not to cry. She was a nurse and she knew what edge she was standing on. In twenty days she had not cried once. If she started she would not know how to stop.

“Four is enough, joonam,” she said. “All the most important things are here. You have sprouts.”

“This is Baba’s.” Nariman pointed to an empty place at one end of the table. “When Baba comes he’ll put the garlic. Baba’s in charge of garlic.”

Maryam held the child. Outside the window was a city at war. And yet there was spring light. Through eight years of fighting Iraq the mothers of this city had set the table in bomb shelters. Somewhere a bird sang. Birds do not know about war. They only follow spring.

One apple, one candle, two coins, bean sprouts in a paper cup. The table a seven-year-old set was the most beautiful thing in Tehran on the twenty-first day of the war.

Tehran — Professor Azad Moradi’s apartment
March 20, 2026 (Fri.) · 6:16 p.m. Tehran time — War Day 21 · Nowruz, the moment the year turns
Scene 8 / 9

Spring came.

The Iranian New Year is set not by the calendar but by the sky. The moment the sun crosses the celestial equator from south to north. The moment day and night are the same length. For three thousand years the people of this land have taken that moment as the New Year. The Achaemenid kings could not change that hour, nor the shahs, nor the ayatollahs. Power changed. The sky did not.

Azad Moradi lit a candle. He was alone in the room. On the table, one dish of sprouted wheat. Wheat he had soaked three weeks ago, the morning the war began. Since that day he had forgotten it. The wheat had not forgotten. Under the bombing it had grown as long as a finger, right on its date.

He wrote on the back of his lecture notes.

Spring does not wait for the war.
Spring always comes on time.
Whether people are ready to see it or not.

This year on the tables of this city,
in the place where the sprouts should be,
sit nerves and fear.

Still the children ask for new clothes.
Still the mothers cook the rice.
That is how people beat the war.

The war did not rest for Nowruz in 2026. On March 20 alone, the rights group HRANA recorded 640 airstrikes in 17 provinces. Revolutionary Guards spokesman Ali Mohammad Naeini was killed in an Israeli strike, and the historic Falak-ol-Aflak castle in Khorramabad was hit. Iran fired nine ballistic missiles at Jerusalem, and debris from an intercepted missile fell about 400 meters from the Western Wall in the Old City. In Iran, Eid al-Fitr fell the next day, March 21. The new Supreme Leader had still not appeared on screen since the statement read for him on March 12.

New Year’s programs were on television. An announcer read the New Year’s greeting. There was no leader’s face. Every year at the moment the year turned, the old leader came on the screen and gave the year its name. Azad had never once listened to that speech all the way through. But without it the room was strangely empty. Even a voice you hated leaves a place when it is gone. Nobody said who would sit in that place.

He turned off the television. Only the candle was left. He looked out the window. There was a light in the window across the way too. And the window next to it. They were not lamps. They were candles. In a blacked-out city people were setting candles in their windows. Small fires that, from the sky, could be targets. Nobody had told them to.

To meet a New Year in the middle of a war. To declare that spring has come when nothing has changed. It is the oldest form of resistance.

Langley, Virginia — CIA Iran Operations Center
March 20, 2026 (Fri.) · 11:20 a.m. EDT / 6:50 p.m. in Tehran — War Day 21
Scene 9 / 9 — Last

The phone rang. Not the work phone on the desk. The personal phone in the drawer.

Leila Shirazi heard the buzz and for a moment could not move. In twenty-one days that phone had not once rung from that number.

In her head a scene from yesterday’s lunch was still hanging. The cafeteria. A liaison officer on loan from the National Security Agency sat down next to her. Across the table somebody brought up the words Persian Code, like a joke. The liaison officer set down his tray and said it as if it were nothing. “Persian Code? That’s a canceled project.” That was all. The talk moved on to something else. Leila put the words away in a drawer somewhere in her head. She did not know what she would use them for. Only that for a canceled project, his answer had come too fast.

The phone kept ringing. A Tehran number.

“Leila jan.”

It was Aunt Fereshte’s voice. Leila started to say something and could not. Her throat closed.

“Auntie.”

“Nowruz Mobarak, azizam. The year just turned. I thought of you and called twenty times. On the twenty-first it went through.”

Then Leila cried. She thought she should not. This was Langley, and Sam was on the other side of the partition. But the moment she heard her aunt’s voice after twenty-one days, that thought had no strength at all.

“Auntie, are you all right? Why couldn’t I get through?”

“The line dies and comes back. Our street is still all right. The sounds are far away. The sky was black for a few days, but the roses lived. The cats are a little thin. And you? Are you eating?”

“I’m eating. I’m all right too.”

It was a lie. But inside this phone call it was not a lie. Now that she had heard her aunt’s voice, she really was all right.

“Leila jan,” her aunt said. Her voice dropped a little. “I don’t ask what you do over there. I never asked once. I won’t ask today either. Only—I set a place for you at the table. Next to the apple.”

The line went dead.

Leila sat a long time holding the phone. On the screen was the satellite image of the Evin district. Roofs. Under one of them there was a table, and on the table an apple, and next to the apple an empty place. The satellite did not show it. But now she could see it. In this building, not many people looked at those roofs as dinner tables instead of targets.

There is something war teaches you. The weight of the smallest things. A phone call after twenty-one days. One “I’m all right.” Before the war you did not know how light they were.

Historical Note
Nowruz — The Spring War Could Not Erase

Nowruz (نوروز) means “new day.” The Persian New Year, with roots in Zoroastrianism, is more than three thousand years old and is celebrated by more than 300 million people, not only in Iran but in Afghanistan, Tajikistan, Azerbaijan, Kurdish regions, and beyond. In 2009 UNESCO added it to the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

After the revolution the Islamic Republic for a time frowned on this pre-Islamic holiday, but could not get rid of it. Through the eight years of the Iran-Iraq War people set the haft-sin in bomb shelters. In 2026, in the spring of the twenty-first day of bombing, they did it again.

Author’s Note
South Pars strikeReal events / March 18
At 2:10 p.m. Tehran time on March 18 the Israeli Air Force bombed the South Pars gas field and the refining and petrochemical facilities at Asaluyeh. It was confirmed that the United States had coordinated and approved it, but President Trump first said he had not known in advance and later said he had known but tried to stop it. Reza Karimi is a fictional character.
Ras LaffanReal events / Qatar
The largest LNG production and export hub in the world. Production stopped after it was hit on March 2; QatarEnergy declared force majeure on March 4; on March 18 Iranian missiles caused extensive damage. Park Jeongwoo and his company are the author’s invention.
Esmail KhatibReal person / Iran
Iran’s intelligence minister from 2021. Killed in an Israeli night strike on Tehran on March 18; President Pezeshkian confirmed his death.
The trapped seafarersReal events / March 18
At an extraordinary session the International Maritime Organization (IMO) said about 3,200 ships and about 20,000 seafarers were stranded west of the strait.
The F-35 hitReal events / March 19
A U.S. F-35, reportedly hit during a combat mission over Iran, made an emergency landing at a U.S. base in the Middle East. The Revolutionary Guards released video of the attempt to shoot it down. It is considered the first time an F-35 was directly hit in combat.
Nowruz and Haft-sinReal holiday / Iranian culture
The 2026 spring equinox fell at 6:16 p.m. Tehran time on March 20. Haft-sin is the New Year’s table set with seven things beginning with the letter sin (س). In Iran, Eid al-Fitr fell the next day, March 21.
“Persian Code”Fictional
The words Leila heard in the cafeteria. An invention of this novel; they return in later episodes.
The Ford’s departureReal events
After the March 12 fire the Ford left the area of operations, received initial repairs and resupply at Souda Bay, Crete, then five days of further repairs at Split, Croatia, at the end of March before returning. The sailing dates in this episode and the captain’s notebook are the author’s invention.
