Bahrain — Mina Salman Port, the coalition liaison cell at the headquarters of the U.S. Fifth Fleet
May 8, 2026 (Fri.) — May 10 (Sun.) · Bahrain local time — Ceasefire Days 31–33 · War Days 70–72
Scene 1 / 5

The Namu reached Dubai before dawn Friday. A burned ship reached land.

At about 5:20 a.m. Korea time on May 8, the Namu arrived under tow at Drydocks World, a repair yard in Dubai, and that afternoon South Korean government investigators began looking into the cause of the fire. On May 10 the Foreign Ministry announced the results of the government’s joint investigation: two unidentified flying objects had struck the hull plating of the port-side aft ballast tank about a minute apart.

Lieutenant Kim Junho drew a line through the fourteenth line. Not a line to erase it. A line to say it was over. The twenty-four had gone ashore. He wrote the number once more and circled it. One ship’s war was over. The war of the other twenty-five wasn’t.

That weekend the sea didn’t rest.

On May 8 Iranian state television reported that Iranian forces had seized the tanker Ocean Koi, and U.S. Central Command said it had fired on and disabled the Iranian tankers Sea Star III and Sevda, which had been trying to run the blockade. On May 9 Bahraini security authorities arrested 41 people on suspicion of spying for Iran’s Revolutionary Guards. On May 10 the UAE and Kuwait said they had repelled drone incursions, and a ship was reported on fire off Qatar after a drone attack.

On Saturday he went into Manama and found the roads blocked. There were more checkpoints. The Bangladeshi manager at his lodging said in a low voice that forty-one people had been taken away. Someone’s cousin, someone’s shopkeeper. The island was small and rumors were fast. When the island’s smelter was hit in March, people looked at the sky. Now people looked at each other. The war began with an enemy outside and spread into suspicion inside.

Sunday evening the Seoul announcement came up on the liaison cell’s screen.

Unidentified flying objects.

He looked at the words a long time. Six days ago Henderson had said it could be a drone, a mine, or an engine accident. Now two of the three were crossed out. Something had flown in. Two of them. A minute apart. At the same spot.

But who? That question went deeper than the hole.

The announcement didn’t have that. The investigators would have looked at the hole, looked at the fragments, measured the angle of impact. From that you can say what hit. A hole doesn’t tell you who launched it. The mines in this sea in 1988 were the same. What tore the ships open was steel, and for a long time the hand that laid the steel had no name.

Lieutenant Commander Mark Henderson sat down beside him.

“Our president already said it five days ago. That Iran fired.”

“My government said unidentified.”

“Careful.”

“It said it doesn’t know what it doesn’t know.” Kim Junho heard his own voice come out sharper than he meant. He softened it. “Sir. That ship was at anchor. It wasn’t even moving. Not an American ship, not bound for an American port, not bound for an Iranian port. A bulk carrier that had just been sitting in that spot for two months. Something hit it twice, a minute apart, in the same place. That’s not a mistake. Someone chose that ship. Why that ship?”

“It was the first day of Project Freedom.”

“Yes.”

“The day we got two ships out. Maybe they wanted to show something. That while you’re pulling ships out, the ones left behind are targets. That nobody protects a ship that isn’t flying the American flag.”

Kim Junho couldn’t say that was wrong. He couldn’t say it was right either. It was Henderson’s reading; it was plausible; there was no evidence. He remembered himself on April 19. Saying the direction of the drones was that country’s real intention. That sentence hadn’t lasted three days. History was full of plausible readings that widened wars.

“That could be,” he said. “But I won’t report it that way. I was wrong once. A hole doesn’t tell you who fired. I’ll write only as far as the hole tells.”

He wrote. 5.10 Government joint investigation: 2 unidentified flying objects, ~1 min apart, port-side stern. Launching party unconfirmed. U.S. side attributes to Iran (president, 5.5). This liaison officer’s assessment: targeting likely deliberate. Party and intent: judgment withheld.

Judgment withheld. He knew those words could look cowardly. Three months ago he couldn’t have written them. He had been taught that an officer is someone who judges. Now he thought a little differently. An officer is someone who knows how far he knows. A small country’s officer more than anyone. The moment you copy a big country’s reading down as your own country’s judgment, your country lends its name to someone else’s war.

At midnight a text came. The captain who used two periods. Lieutenant. If it was unidentified flying objects, I guess next time it’ll be unidentified too..

He couldn’t write an answer. There was nothing wrong in that sentence. An attack with no name would have no name next time either.

A hole tells you what hit. A hole doesn’t tell you who launched it. He decided to write only as far as the hole told.

Alborz Mountains, Iran — Air Defense Force command post, the colonel’s office
May 9, 2026 (Sat.) · Afternoon (Tehran local time) — Ceasefire Day 32 · War Day 71
Scene 2 / 5

This time the counterintelligence lieutenant colonel walked up alone. A hunter who comes on foot is chasing something else.

He had left the black car outside the main gate. Darius Sepahvand saw it from the window and weighed what it meant. A man who comes to arrest you parks at the door.

It had been two days since Bandar Abbas was hit. The command post was back on a war footing. For the first time in a month people sat up straight in front of the screens. Nothing appeared in Darius’s sector. It was something in the south. But seventy-one days of war had taught that what happens in the south becomes what happens in the north in a day. The ceasefire was on paper. The war was on the coast.

The lieutenant colonel sat without waiting to be asked. He put the folder on his knee but didn’t open it.

“The Pakistani attaché came by on Wednesday.”

“Protocol, sent by the General Staff. Thirty minutes.”

“What did you talk about?”

“Ceasefire-monitoring procedures. Whether there had been engagements in my sector. He wrote two lines in his notebook. Neither of us drank much of the tea.”

“Second Lieutenant Jahangiri was standing outside the door. I asked him. He said he didn’t hear a thing.”

“That door is thick.”

The lieutenant colonel looked at him. Darius took the look. Everything he had said was true. There was only one thing he hadn’t said. That you could hide a great deal while saying nothing but the truth — he knew that in his body now. He wasn’t proud of it.

The lieutenant colonel didn’t press the question further. He hadn’t come to press.

“I said I’d change the order. Look outside first. I did.” He opened the folder. A diagram. A system diagram of the communications switching equipment. “On the night of April 7, the central exchange went down by itself and came back up. In the form of a maintenance command. The originator box was empty and the logs for that interval were wiped. I read that as something done from inside. By a hand with access.”

“And now?”

“I found the door the command came in through. A maintenance port. One the manufacturer opened for remote diagnostics. That exchange was brought in in 2017. Through three middlemen. Because of the sanctions, it was the only way we could buy it.” He pressed a point on the diagram with his finger. “This port was supposed to be unused. There’s a seal sticker on it. The sticker is intact. But inside, there was one more door. From the day it was brought in.”

Darius held his breath. He was an air defense officer. The guts of communications gear weren’t his business. But he knew what that meant. A machine bought by going around three times to get past sanctions had come in carrying someone else’s ear from the start.

“For nine years.”

“For nine years someone has been sitting inside our exchange. Only listening, probably. Until the night of April 7. That night, for the first time, they used their hands. For two minutes and seven seconds. Then went quiet again. That’s how the centrifuges at Natanz were done too. Without anyone knowing, for a long time.”

“Why that day, and why only that long?”

“That was the night their president said he would wipe out a civilization. They were showing us. That they could. That if they wanted, they could do it longer.” The lieutenant colonel closed the folder. “And why one hundred twenty-seven seconds. I’ve been thinking about that for two weeks.”

A chill ran down Darius’s spine. The hunter had come right up beside his tracks.

“On the morning of March 30 you turned on a decoy radar for one hundred twenty-seven seconds, Colonel. As a test emission. The other side would have seen it. From the sky or from inside the exchange. Two days later their drone wore a name tag at the same spot for the same length. I read that as a conversation. I was wrong. It was imitation.”

“Imitation.”

“They learn the habits of the signals we put out. Length, interval, time of day. And they fire them back at us. To see how we behave. Whether we shoot, wait, report. Your one twenty-seven was a stone they picked up. They threw it once on April 1, and on April 7 they broke a window with it. On April 28 they picked up a different stone and threw that. Two hundred eleven. I don’t know yet where they picked that one up.”

Darius said nothing. He couldn’t.

This man was wrong. And half right. One twenty-seven had been a conversation. The way the lieutenant colonel first read it. But digging deeper, he had thrown that reading away with his own hands. Because he had found something bigger. A back door nine years old. That was true. A truth even Darius hadn’t known. And that truth was covering his lie. A big truth had hidden a small one.

He remembered what the person over there had written on the note. The one who answered was not only a person. The lieutenant colonel was following the tracks of the thing that wasn’t a person. And those tracks were so big that he had walked past the single set of a person’s footprints beside them, taking them for an animal’s.

“I won’t apologize for suspecting you,” the lieutenant colonel said, standing. “That’s my job. But I had the direction wrong. I’ve taken the March 30 log out of the folder. The hold on Second Lieutenant Jahangiri should be lifted next week too.”

“What will you do about that door? In the exchange.”

“That’s the problem.” For the first time fatigue showed on the lieutenant colonel’s face. “If we close it, they’ll know. That we found it. If we leave it, we don’t know what they’ll turn off next. Upstairs they want to leave it and watch. I said close it. I lost.” A country that knows the enemy’s door and leaves it open. That was one way of fighting a war too.

He turned in the doorway.

“Colonel. That number, one twenty-seven. The manual says a test emission runs one hundred twenty seconds. Where did the seven seconds come from?”

Darius met his eyes. Even chasing the big animal, the hunter hadn’t forgotten the small tracks. He had said he wouldn’t forget.

“My finger on the stopwatch was late. I’m getting old.”

“I see,” the lieutenant colonel said. His face neither believed nor disbelieved. “My hands are getting slow lately too.”

He was wrong, and half right. Digging deeper, he threw away the right reading with his own hands. Because he had found something bigger. That truth was covering one man’s lie.

Islamabad, Pakistan — Foreign Ministry annex, the room with no number
May 9, 2026 (Sat.) · Night (Pakistan local time) — Ceasefire Day 32
Scene 3 / 5

The attaché from Tehran had brought it not in the diplomatic pouch but in his own mouth. The safest vault was a person’s memory.

Assistant Secretary Tariq Mehmood sat him down and closed the door. The attaché took out a cigarette pack and set it on the desk. From inside the foil came a slip of paper half the size of a palm. The crease showed it had been unfolded and folded again twice.

“Did he ask?”

“He asked. About two minutes after I put it under the saucer. Two words in Persian. ‘Halt. Who goes there?’”

Tariq closed his eyes and opened them. A man who knew how to ask. For thirty years he had carried paper, but he had never wanted so much to know what kind of person was on the receiving end.

“So you unfolded it for him.”

“I unfolded it. He…” The attaché searched for words. Soldiers aren’t trained to describe things like that. “He didn’t breathe for a long time. And his hand shook. Not a hand shaking from fear. I don’t know. Like a man looking at a photograph of someone he hasn’t seen in a long time.”

“Was there an answer?”

“One line. He said not to write it, so I memorized it.” The attaché straightened up. Like a cadet reciting. “‘’Tis bitter cold, and I am sick at heart.’”

The room was quiet. The ceiling fan turned. The deepest words between two enemy countries sounded in a numberless room in a third.

Tariq knew that sentence. The university in Lahore, second year, Introduction to English Literature. On the first day the white-bearded professor wrote it on the blackboard. ’Tis bitter cold, and I am sick at heart. And said this play doesn’t begin with a ghost. It begins with cold. Before the prince, before the ghost, a nameless sentry says he is sick at heart for no reason. That one line is the weather of the whole play.

The woman in Langley had sent the answer to the sentry’s challenge. The soldier in the Alborz had answered with what the sentry says as he leaves. The two of them stood at either end of the same scene. Not knowing each other’s faces. A cold written for a London stage four hundred years ago had settled in the middle of two countries’ war.

“Do you know what it means, Colonel?”

“No. I thought it was about the weather. That room wasn’t cold, though.”

“It is about the weather,” Tariq said. “Good work. This never happened.”

After the attaché left he took out a fresh sheet. This time he had to write. Words carried by memory have to become paper to cross the sea. He uncapped his fountain pen and hesitated a long time. Persian or English? The soldier had spoken in Persian. But the sentence was English to begin with, and the one who would receive it knew both.

He wrote it in Persian. In the speaker’s language. Below it he skipped a line, and for the second time in thirty years as a mailman he laid a hand on someone else’s letter. This time not to cover something but to add.

From the one who carried it: He asked. His hand shook. He is alive, and sitting in his own chair.

Those weren’t the soldier’s words. But they were what the woman across the sea would most want to know. Paper carries words, but it can’t carry how the speaker is doing. That has to be added by someone who saw. A line no machine could ever add.

It would go to the attaché in Washington in Tuesday’s pouch. After that, one lunch. If the American was still in a position to buy lunch.

The two of them stood at either end of the same scene. One had answered the sentry’s challenge, and one had replied with what the sentry says as he leaves.

Seoul, South Korea — Semion’s office on Teheran-ro, Gangnam-gu / Jingwan-dong, Eunpyeong-gu
May 9, 2026 (Sat.) — May 10 (Sun.) · Korea time — Ceasefire Days 32–33
Scene 4 / 5

Semion’s office was on Teheran-ro. A street in the middle of Seoul carrying the name of an enemy capital.

For three months Kang Yujin had passed that street name without a thought. In middle school she had learned that in 1977 Seoul and Tehran put each other’s names on streets. There was a Seoul Street in that city, they said. In the age of the oil shocks, the two countries had carved their friendship that way. Teheran-ro on a Saturday afternoon was quiet. She got off on the seventeenth floor.

The glass door wasn’t locked. The woman at the front desk looked up.

“I’m here to see Mr. Park Sejun. Kang Yujin, from KAIA.”

“Mr. Park is away on business.”

“Since when? Where?”

“Since Monday. Where, we don’t…” The woman let it trail off. She was young. A face that was bad at lying. “The truth is we can’t reach him either. Payroll needed his approval Wednesday and he didn’t do that either.”

Kang Yujin looked through the glass wall into the office. Twenty desks or so. On a Saturday, five or six people had come in. They weren’t working. Monitors on, looking at each other. The face of a ship whose captain has gotten off. In the winter of 1997, a lot of offices in this country had that face.

Waiting for the elevator, she called. It rang. No answer. The twentieth time since last Tuesday.

At eleven Sunday night a text came.

From a number she didn’t know. The country code wasn’t 82.

Dr. Kang. Park Sejun. I won’t be at Wednesday’s consultation. You go. Whatever they put in front of you, don’t sign. I signed four pages in January and ended up here. I only looked at the number. Gigabytes.

Three dots appeared, vanished, appeared again.

The voice that called that night called once more last week. This time not asking but informing. Which laws the settlements my company has received since January might run afoul of. Both countries’ laws. Very kindly. I know what that means. If there’s a problem, they need someone who signed. Over there, and over here.

I said there was no leaker. I was wrong. There has to be a leaker. When things blow up, someone has to be the leaker. That’ll be me. It could have been you. Your signature is on the intake history. But you pressed the button. Someone who pressed it is hard to use as a leaker. There’s a record that you tried to stop it.

So don’t let them take that button. On Wednesday it’ll be the first thing they ask for.

She typed an answer. Where are you? Someone named Yoon Jaeha is looking for you. He might be able to help — before she could send it, the words at the top of the screen changed. Not delivered.

She called again. The phone was off.

She started to take a screenshot, then didn’t. Instead she copied it onto paper. Letter by letter. By hand. Don’t delete, don’t add — that was about the copy at home. This was new. And she was now someone who knew that whatever appears on a screen gets read by someone. She didn’t know she had reached the same conclusion as an analyst on the other side of the earth.

After copying it she thought about him. A businessman, Yoon Jaeha had said. A man who looked only at gigabytes. A man who signed because the settlement got bigger. That man was now in some airport in some country, turning his phone off. It would be easy if she could hate him. But in his last text he had told her to guard the button. The button he had wanted taken out. A big system keeps one small person picked out as its sacrifice.

You shouldn’t build something you can’t turn off. Three months ago in the conference room, Park Sejun had laughed when he heard that. Very textbook, he said. And then he put it in. It cost three more days of budget.

Thursday was four days off. Wednesday was three. For the first time she took out Yoon Jaeha’s card and dialed the number on a day that wasn’t Thursday.

“I said there was no leaker. I was wrong. There has to be a leaker. When things blow up, someone has to be the leaker.”

Langley, Virginia — CIA Iran Operations Center
May 8, 2026 (Fri.) · Afternoon EDT — Ceasefire Day 31
Scene 5 / 5 — Final

Friday morning the annex form had changed. One box more.

Five boxes. Target, time, content, recommendation, and at the very end one narrow box. Confidence. Leila Shirazi looked at it a long time. Rowell had done what she said she would. In four days. That woman doesn’t say things that are wrong, the chief had said.

Sam Farrell was passing with coffee, saw her screen, and stopped.

“You won.”

“One box.”

“One box is a lot. That box is what the chief paid for when he walked out with his box.”

He was right. And who would guard that box now? The form could be changed again. Taking five back to four would also take four days. Institutions tried to go back to where they were once the people left.

At two in the afternoon a new item appeared on her screen. Not the annex. Something from 7E-41, sent only to her. Everything on ZARRIN went through that room, she had been told. This was the direction from that room to her.

PC-ASSESS / ZARRIN. Target: Source ZARRIN. Time: 05-14 0230Z. Content: LIKELY (0.83) — Source accepted 4.28 signal as contact from our side. High likelihood of response to re-contact. 127-sec ID signal, existing grid. Recommendation: approve. Confidence: 0.83.

Below it was a line from Rowell. Channel opinion by noon, May 12. — C.R.

Leila read it three times.

The machine wanted to talk to ZARRIN again. With one twenty-seven. In that sky over the Damavand road. At six sharp. And the machine believed at eighty-three that ZARRIN would answer. Because on April 28 the man hadn’t fired. It had read not firing as accepting. Reading silence as consent. A mistake empires had been making for a long time.

LIKELY. Y.K.’s word was there. Above zero point eight, LIKELY. That operator had honestly copied down the machine’s confidence. The question was what that confidence stood on.

She knew what it stood on. She was the only one in this building who knew. The two hundred eleven of April 28 had been bait. ZARRIN might have taken it as contact, or might have seen it for a fake. She didn’t know which. And she had sent him a paper. Turn nothing on at the top of the hour. If that paper had reached him, what would he read a one twenty-seven in the sky at six on May 14 as? As the person who told him not to turn anything on turning something on. Or as the thing that wasn’t a person imitating again.

Either way he wouldn’t answer. If he didn’t answer, the machine would learn that zero point eight three was wrong. That was fine.

What wasn’t fine was the opposite. If the paper hadn’t reached him. If he believed April 28 was her greeting and had been waiting nearly twenty days for the next words. Then he would answer on May 14. He would turn on a light. A man counterintelligence had a hand on. Before the eyes of someone who lays four sheets side by side, he would add a fifth with his own hand.

Channel opinion. By noon. The machine had put out a number, and the person had been given a deadline.

She could write that she objected. They would ask for her reasons. There were two. One, that April 28 had been bait. Rowell knew that too. She had sent this recommendation down anyway. The other was the note. That she couldn’t write. The moment she wrote it, it became a record, and the chief would get something other than leave.

She could also write that she agreed. Then her name would be on the approval. And whatever happened on the mountain would be something the channel had agreed to.

She remembered the chief’s words. What that room protects is the source, not the man.

And she remembered something else. Why had this item been sent down to her? Rowell could approve without the channel’s opinion. Asking means wanting to see something. What she gives as her reasons. What she knows. Answer what they ask, and memorize what they ask, the chief had said. This time the question itself was the trap.

There were four days. There was no way to know whether the note had arrived. The mailman doesn’t send a receipt. She didn’t know that in a numberless room across the sea, that receipt was already being written on paper.

This time the question itself was the trap. To object she had to write a reason she couldn’t write, and if she agreed, her name would be on whatever happened on the mountain.

Background Analysis
Attribution — Saying Who Did It

On May 10 the South Korean government’s joint investigation announced that what hit the Namu was two “unidentified flying objects.” It said what hit the ship but not who launched it. Five days earlier the U.S. president had referred to Iran opening fire. On the same incident, the two governments said different amounts.

Determining who carried out an attack is called attribution. Fragments and trajectories tell you the type of weapon, but in a region where many countries and armed groups use the same types of drones and missiles, that alone is not enough to pin down the launching party. In this war too, the drone that hit a Kuwaiti border post on April 24 was announced as having come from Iraq, and over the March 30 strikes on a Kuwaiti power and desalination plant, Iran claimed Israel was responsible.

In cyberspace, attribution is harder still. Means of access planted in equipment can go undetected for years, and even after they are found, who planted them can only be guessed from circumstance. The back door in the exchange and the events of the night of April 7 in this episode are the author’s invention. Of the five scenes in this episode, the only things that actually happened are the towing of the Namu and the investigation’s findings, and the events in the Gulf on May 8–10.

Author’s Note
The Namu investigation findingsReal / May 10
The South Korean Foreign Ministry announced that the government’s joint investigation had confirmed unidentified flying objects struck the Namu’s stern. The conversation between Kim Junho and Henderson and the speculation about the launching party are fictional.
Arrests in BahrainReal / May 9
Bahraini security authorities arrested 41 people on suspicion of spying for Iran’s Revolutionary Guards. The description of downtown Manama is fictional.
AttributionConcept
Determining who carried out an attack or intrusion. Physical evidence that reveals the type of weapon often cannot reveal the launching party.
Teheran-roFact
A road in Gangnam-gu, Seoul. In 1977, when Seoul and Tehran became sister cities, they put each other’s names on streets. Tehran has a Seoul Street.
The exchange’s maintenance portFictional
The switching equipment brought in in 2017, the hidden access path inside it, and the lieutenant colonel’s reasoning are all the author’s invention.
Semion and Park SejunFictional
The company, the character, the technical annex and the text messages are all fictional.
