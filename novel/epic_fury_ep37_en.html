In two weeks the deal died once and came back to life. On paper.

On June 1, Iran said it was ending all peace talks with the United States, citing an increase in American attacks and in Israeli airstrikes on Lebanon. On June 3 an Iranian drone hit Kuwait International Airport, killing one person and wounding 63. On June 7, after Israel struck the southern suburbs of Beirut, Iran fired ballistic missiles at Israel, and the next day the Israeli military hit military sites in central and western Iran. Explosions were reported in Tehran, Isfahan, and Tabriz. Two Iranian soldiers were killed. Iran then said it had halted military action against Israel, and Israel announced that it had stopped its attacks at President Trump’s request. On June 9 a U.S. Army Apache helicopter collided with an Iranian Shahed drone off the coast of Oman and went down. The crew was rescued.

Last night Colonel Darius Sepahvand’s sector had fought again, for the first time in sixty-two days. Israeli planes came in from the west. The one battery left fired two missiles. They missed. The battery survived. The sky west of Tehran was red for about an hour, and then it stopped. Word went around that someone in Washington had called someone in Jerusalem.

After that the men in the command post talked less. Nobody said the word ceasefire. As if it were a bowl that would crack if you said it.

The Pakistani attaché came at three in the afternoon. His second courtesy call. The General Staff cable said reconfirmation of ceasefire monitoring following the events of June 8. A thirty-minute visit. Second Lieutenant Sohrab Jahangiri brought the tea. This time he didn’t ask. He set the sugar cubes beside the saucer. Then he left. The door was thick.

The attaché was in more of a hurry than last time. His country went back and forth between the two capitals several times a day now. The pace of a country that carries other people’s words. He asked three questions about monitoring procedures, wrote down the answers, picked up his teacup and set it down.

There was paper under the saucer.

This time Darius didn’t think of a trap. He reached out and pulled it free. A little bigger than the last one, and no fold marks. The same hand. A woman’s Persian, written without hurry.

Friends to this ground. What comes overhead I cannot stop. Only be underground when it comes.

He read it. Then for a long time he didn’t move.

Friends to this ground. He knew that line. It wasn’t in the part they had played on the roof. His brother only liked the first page of the first act. At fifteen, two years after his brother died, he had read the book to the end by himself. The two men who come after the sentry leaves. Halt. Who goes there? Friends to this ground. And liegemen to the Dane.

The second half was missing.

He read the empty space. The person on the other side had not written liegeman to the king. Couldn’t. That person’s king was the man sending bombs over his head. A liegeman can’t choose his king. He can choose his sentences. The person had written I cannot stop it. Cannot. Not will not.

One line was left. Be underground.

It meant something was coming. The person on the other side knew. Either didn’t know the date or couldn’t say it. So this one line was all. The paper would have been written three weeks ago. The last note had taken that long. Three weeks ago was mid-May. Around when the other side’s president wrote that he was putting off the attack scheduled for tomorrow.

That attack hadn’t been called off. Only put off. Last night Israel came. Today the word was that a U.S. Army helicopter had gone down off Oman.

The attaché took the paper back. This time Darius spoke first, before the look that asked for an answer could come.

“Carry one line back for me. ‘O, farewell, honest soldier.’”

The attaché repeated the words under his breath. He didn’t ask what they meant. Darius went over for himself why he had picked that line. It was what the men who stay say to the one going off watch. Something you say to a man you may not see again. And that person was honest. Had written I cannot stop it.

After the attaché left he stood at the window.

Underground. This building had an underground. An alternate command post three floors down. Dug in the 1980s. The years Iraq’s missiles were falling on Tehran. Cramped and damp, and only half the communication lines reached it. They had used it the first week of the war and then come back up. The operations room above had bigger screens and better air. People always climb toward the light.

The person on the other side must have hoped he would go down, even if alone. But a commander doesn’t go down alone and leave his men up top. That person knew that too. So she wrote be. Not an order. A wish.

He couldn’t go down alone. He could go down taking them all.

He called for Sohrab.

“From tonight the night watch runs out of the alternate command post. Switch the essential operations-room lines down below by eight this evening. Put it in the log like this. Survivability measure following the June 8 engagement.”

“Only half the lines reach down there, sir. And there are only three screens.”

“Then we use three.”

The second lieutenant looked at the colonel. There was a question in his face. He didn’t ask it. By now he knew that some of this colonel’s orders were better followed without asking. Three months of war had taught him that.

“I will have them switched over by eight, sir.”

A commander doesn’t go down alone and leave his men up top. He couldn’t go down alone. He could go down taking them all.

That night the liaison cell was underground.

On June 10, President Trump said progress in the talks to end the war was insufficient and that he would hit Iran very hard, and Defense Secretary Hegseth said the United States would bomb key Iranian facilities that day. The U.S. military attacked multiple targets in Iran, firing 49 Tomahawk cruise missiles. The same day, three Indian crew members died aboard the tanker Settebello after it came under U.S. attack. The United States said the crew had not complied with instructions from American forces. In Bemani, near the Strait of Hormuz, a local official said a drinking-water facility was destroyed during the American strikes, leaving about 20,000 people without water. Iran said it had launched attacks on the U.S. Fifth Fleet, on Ali Al Salem Air Base in Kuwait, and on an American base in Jordan.

Lieutenant Kim Junho sat with his back against the shelter wall. The same wall where he had heard the news of the ceasefire before dawn on April 8. That time there had been two hundred people in this room. Someone had cried.

The siren went three times. On the third the ground shook once. Far off. The south of the island.

On June 11, Iran declared the Strait of Hormuz closed with no time limit and said that, in response to the American attacks, it had fired on the Fifth Fleet’s communications antennas and radar installations, on U.S. bases in Kuwait and Bahrain, and on an American command post in Jordan. The radar station on Jabal al-Dukhan, the highest hill in Bahrain, was hit in the Iranian attack. In mid-June a Swiss newspaper confirmed that the installation had been damaged.

Lieutenant Commander Mark Henderson came and sat down beside him. He was wearing body armor. He handed Kim Junho a bottle of water.

“It’s Jabal al-Dukhan. The radar.”

“The people there?”

“Don’t know. Not yet.”

The two of them were quiet a long time. There were about forty people in the shelter. Quieter than two months ago. Back then everyone believed it was over. Now nobody believed the word. Where the belief had gone out, silence filled in.

“Forty-nine,” Kim Junho said. “Yesterday afternoon the President said he’d hit them hard. And at night forty-nine went out. On the sixty-fourth day of the ceasefire.”

“Ceasefire.” Henderson chewed the word. “You still use that word?”

“In reports, yes. In quotation marks.”

“It’s the helicopter. The Apache went down the day before yesterday. Hit a drone. Their side says it wasn’t on purpose. The two crew got picked up. By an unmanned boat, they said. A boat with nobody on it went out to save people.” He laughed low. “That one helicopter cost forty-nine missiles. And three Indians on a tanker. And twenty thousand people who can’t drink the water.”

“Sir.”

“I know. Not something an officer should say. Give me a pass down here.”

Kim Junho drank some water. Then he took out his notebook. In the dim light of the shelter he looked at the list. After the Universal Winner, one more ship had gotten out. Just yesterday, June 10. After the first-week date had slipped once. The ship of the captain whose daughter was taking the Suneung. A ship that carried liquefied natural gas. The moment the captain cleared the strait and reached the wide water of the Gulf of Oman, he texted. We’re out. Two words.

Twenty-three lines were left. Twenty-four if you counted the Namu, up on a dry dock in Dubai. In the winter of 1950 on the pier at Hungnam, someone had counted the ships left like this too. And today Iran said it would close the strait with no time limit.

The consultations would stop. Starting with the thin road that let ships go one at a time. He knew it. Of all the things he had seen in three months, it was the one he was surest of. When big things collide, the small roads close first.

The siren stopped. People got up one by one. Henderson didn’t get up.

“Lieutenant. I’m going home next month. Rotation orders came through.”

“Congratulations, sir.”

“And you?”

“I stay until the ships are all out. My orders were amended to say so. I recommended it.”

Henderson looked at him. Three months ago he had taken this Korean lieutenant for a spectator. A spectator from a country that hadn’t sent a single ship to this war.

“All twenty-four ships.”

“Until the people aboard them are all out.”

When big things collide, the small roads close first. The thin road that let ships go one at a time.

It was cramped underground.

Three screens. Eight chairs. Water dripped from the pipes in the ceiling. The night watch, eleven of them, sat shoulder to shoulder. It was the second night. The first night someone had grumbled. Why are we down here when it’s fine upstairs? Darius Sepahvand didn’t answer. He had come down with them. That was answer enough.

At 0214 the reports from the southern sectors poured in all at once. Cruise missiles. Low altitude. Many. Three screens couldn’t draw them all. Second Lieutenant Sohrab Jahangiri wrote bearings by hand on paper. Where the machine fell short, a hand filled in.

“Eastern Sector 1 command post, communications lost. Sector 2 not responding.”

An air defense command post gets hit in the first hour. He had taught that for thirty-one years. It was going by the manual now.

“Our battery.”

“Standing by. No target lock. They’re too low.”

“Don’t fire. Don’t give away the position.”

0221.

The sound didn’t come first. The floor came first. The chairs lifted and dropped. Then the sound came. More pressure than sound. It came in through the chest, not the ears. The lights went out. All three screens went dark at once. Dirt poured from the ceiling. Someone screamed, and someone prayed.

The emergency light came on. Red.

Darius was face down on the floor. He pushed himself up. A high ringing in his ears. In the red light he counted heads. One, two. The second lieutenant crawled out from under a desk. Blood ran from his forehead. Three. Four.

Eleven. And himself.

“Wounded.”

“It’s a scrape.” Sohrab touched his own forehead as he said it. His hand was shaking. “Colonel. Upstairs.”

Upstairs was gone. He knew. The operations room three floors up. The room with the big screens and the good air. Where these eleven had sat until the night before last.

The second one came. A little farther off. Toward the communications antenna. Then it was quiet.

They were looking at him. Eleven faces in the red light. The sergeant who had grumbled two days ago had his mouth open. They hadn’t finished the arithmetic yet. They would soon. Why the colonel had suddenly sent them all underground the night before last.

Survivability measure following the June 8 engagement. That was what the log said. It was true. Three nights ago Israel came, and the commander prepared for what came next. Whatever counterintelligence officer came and asked, that answer would hold.

Only he himself knew. Without that paper he wouldn’t have come down. To a cramped, damp place with only three screens. He would have been upstairs. And these eleven too.

The person on the other side wrote to save that one man. Twelve lived.

That person would have no way to know it. He thought that, then corrected himself. No. She would know. She was someone who read what this building reported. If you asked how he knew, he couldn’t answer. Only, since some day in May, each time he signed the confirmation box on the morning report, he had felt someone reading it.

He spoke to the lieutenant.

“See whether the backup line survived. If it did, send the morning report on time. Don’t leave out a line. Main building hit. No personnel casualties. Night watch, eleven, all hands, carrying out duties at the alternate command post.”

“I am to write ‘all hands,’ sir?”

“Write all hands. Make sure that word goes in.”

Sohrab felt for paper with a bloody hand. Then his hand stopped and he looked at the colonel. Twenty-three-year-old eyes. Eyes that had believed anything three months ago. Eyes that had spent three weeks in the room underground. Eyes that had counted the drums in the storehouse three times.

“Colonel. This makes the fourth time.”

Darius understood. March 28. March 30. The storehouse. And the night before last. The days the colonel had sent him somewhere.

“This time I didn’t send you out. We came down together.”

“I know.” The second lieutenant’s voice cracked. “That’s why I’m saying it. Thank you.”

The person on the other side wrote to save that one man. Twelve lived.

Leila Shirazi did not go home that night.

She couldn’t see the target list. She wasn’t cleared for it. But in the afternoon Catherine Rowell had called her in and said one thing. It’s tonight. The same list as May. The woman had no obligation to say it. Leila didn’t thank her. It wasn’t something to be grateful for.

Dots came up on the wall screen in the operations center. South to north, slowly. Cruise missiles are slow. They fly for more than an hour. For that hour she tried to sit in her place and not watch the screen. And she watched.

Eastern Sector 3. East of Tehran, on the mountain. On the screen it was a small numbered square. Two lines touched that square. 18:51 EDT. 02:21 Tehran time.

The square changed color. Screens don’t draw people.

Sam Farrell came and stood beside her. He said nothing. He knew what that square was. The window the three of them had watched the lights in, three months ago. The four lines she had read every morning since.

“The note,” he asked quietly. He had never been told about any note. But Sam was no fool. The chief’s lunches, her paper notebook, the way she kept touching her wallet.

“It’s been three weeks. It may have gotten there. It may not.”

“If it did.”

“Even if it did, he’s a commander. He won’t leave his people and go down.”

She said that and shut her mouth. That night in May, in the chief’s kitchen, she had written it knowing that. Still, it stays—that somebody said it. What stays. Back then she had tried not to think that it might be what stayed on that man’s grave.

The morning report came up at ten past six Tehran time. Ten-forty at night on the U.S. East Coast. Four hours to go.

Afterward she couldn’t remember how she spent those four hours. She wrote something in the notebook and tore it out. She drank three cups of coffee. She washed her face with cold water in the restroom. At ten-thirty she opened the raw-collection window and sat in front of it.

Ten-forty. Nothing came up.

Ten-fifty. Nothing.

On May 21 that report had been an hour and a half late. The day after the signals officer died. Then, even late, there had been someone to send it.

Eleven. Eleven-thirty.

That building had a communications antenna. If the antenna was destroyed, the report wouldn’t come. Even if people were alive. She held on to that thought. If people were dead, the report wouldn’t come either. That thought she tried not to hold.

Sam didn’t leave. He stayed at his desk, his back to her.

2356.

A line came up.

The form was different. Not the usual report. Short. A brief cable over the backup line. She read it. In her own language, one word at a time.

Eastern Sector 3. Main building hit June 11, 0221. Operations room destroyed. One communications antenna destroyed. No personnel casualties. Night watch, 11, all hands, carrying out duties at alternate command post. Reporting by backup line. Prepared by: 2nd Lt. Jahangiri. Confirmed: Sector Commander, Col. Sepahvand.

All hands.

She looked at that word. A word you didn’t often see in a form cable. No personnel casualties would be enough. Someone had said to put that word in. Make sure.

Alternate command post. That was underground.

He had been underground. Not alone. With eleven men.

She pressed a hand over her mouth so no sound would get out. Her shoulders shook. Sam turned around. He got up and came behind her chair and looked at the screen. It was Persian. He couldn’t read it. He read her face instead.

“He made it?”

She nodded. No words would come. It was a long time before they did.

“It says all hands. All eleven. They were underground.”

Sam let out a long breath. Then he did something he had never done. He took her shoulders in both hands, squeezed hard, and let go.

After a long while Leila wrote the memo. To 7E-41. Her hands were still shaking and she made typos. She fixed them.

Collected 6.11 0356Z. Eastern Sector 3 main building hit, no personnel casualties. Source alive and in post. Night watch had been relocated to underground alternate command post as of 6.9 (log reason: survivability measure following 6.8 engagement). Assessment: appears to result from source’s command judgment.

She wrote the assessment line and looked at it for a long time. It was true. It was that man’s command judgment. The reason given was the June 8 engagement. Whatever inspector read it, the sentence had no gaps.

Only four people in the world knew what that sentence covered. Her, the chief on leave, the mailman in Islamabad, and the man on the mountain.

She took the paper out of her wallet. ’Tis bitter cold, and I am sick at heart. He would be cold now too. Three floors down. With the building over his head gone. But alive. With eleven men.

For sixteen years she had written reports. Hundreds of them. She had never once known which of them saved anyone. Assessments go up and disappear. In 1953 this agency’s papers brought down a government in Tehran. A piece of paper the size of a palm had walked for twenty-two days and sent twelve men underground.

A word you didn’t often see in a form cable. Someone had said to put it in. Make sure. — All hands.

The news came around the time of evening prayer.

On June 14, President Trump and Iran announced that they had reached an agreement to end the war and reopen the Strait of Hormuz. President Trump also said he had authorized the lifting of the U.S. Navy’s blockade. The U.S. military explained that the blockade would stay in place until the agreement was signed. A draft memorandum of understanding reported the next day provided for a 60-day ceasefire period and the reopening of the strait to merchant shipping, with the unresolved issues, Iran’s nuclear program among them, to be negotiated again during that period. The same day, Israel carried out airstrikes on the Dahiyeh district of Beirut.

Maryam was on the roof. The place where the children had first seen sunlight on the morning of April 8. The children who were twenty-three then were fourteen now. The rest had gone home, to relatives, to other cities. Every evening the fourteen came up to the roof and kicked a ball around.

News was playing from someone’s phone. Agreement. At that word one of the nurses grabbed the railing and started to cry. Another one laughed. Maryam neither cried nor laughed.

Four nights ago Tehran had shaken again. And two nights before that. Before that they had said the ceasefire was indefinite. Before that they had said hostilities had terminated. She had long since forgotten how to believe words. There was one thing she believed. The message that came every morning.

Since the internet came back, Davood had sent one line every day. Quiet today. Hot today. No water today. Wednesday night nothing came. It came at eight on Thursday morning. Alive. We were east again. When she got those two lines she sat down on the stairs and couldn’t get up for ten minutes.

Nariman left the ball and came running.

“Maman! They said there’s an agreement! What’s an agreement?”

“It’s when people who were fighting promise to stop fighting.”

“But they promised last time. On the roof. And Grandpa said it wasn’t over, it just stopped.”

“So it was.”

“Is it over this time?”

Maryam looked at her daughter’s face. In three months the girl had lost two teeth and grown two finger-widths taller. And she had learned to doubt words. At that age. This country’s children always learned that too early.

“I don’t know either. You only know a promise is a promise once it’s kept.”

“Then what about Baba? When’s Baba coming?”

She took out her phone. The one that had come this morning. Quiet today. The company commander says there might be a rotation soon. Might be, he said.

“He might be coming soon.”

“Might be?”

“Might be.”

The girl turned that over carefully. Then she ran to Professor Azad Moradi, who was reading in a chair by the roof door.

“Grandpa! They said there’s an agreement. Is it over this time, or did it just stop?”

The old man closed the book. It was Saadi. The secondhand copy with pencil writing in the margin of the first page. He carried it everywhere these days.

“That is not decided by what they write on the paper.”

“Then what decides it?”

“The day your Baba walks in through that door with a seashell in his hand. That is the day your war ends. Whatever the paper says.”

“What about the other kids? The ones from the west pier.”

Azad didn’t answer. He couldn’t. Those children’s war wouldn’t end. There had been children like that on the summer day in 1988 when the radio announced the ceasefire too. In seventy-two years he had never found the words to tell a child that.

The sun was going down. Over the roofs of the city. More lights were on than three and a half months ago. In the alley below a car horn sounded, long. Somebody had already decided to believe. Maryam rested her arms on the railing and listened to it.

Her phone buzzed.

Did you hear? Everybody here is yelling their heads off. I didn’t yell. Afraid I’d jinx it. The seashell is in my pocket.

She laughed. For the first time in three and a half months, out loud.

“That is not decided by what they write on the paper. It is decided by the day your Baba walks in through that door with a seashell. That is the day your war ends.”

Background Analysis
Two Weeks — May 28 to June 14

The agreement that in late May was said to be in its final stages fell apart and was put back together over the course of two weeks. On May 28, Kuwait intercepted a ballistic missile headed for its territory, and the debris was reported to have come down on Ali Al Salem Air Base, lightly injuring about five U.S. troops and contractors. On June 1, Iran said it was ending all talks with the United States. On June 3, one person was killed and 63 were wounded at Kuwait International Airport. On June 7–8, Iran and Israel struck each other’s territory for the first time since the ceasefire.

On June 9 a U.S. Apache helicopter collided with an Iranian drone off the coast of Oman and went down. The crew was rescued by an unmanned surface vessel, in what is said to be the first time the U.S. Navy has carried out a search and rescue with an unmanned craft. On June 10 the U.S. military fired 49 Tomahawks, and three Indian crew members died aboard the tanker Settebello. On June 11, Iran declared the strait closed indefinitely and attacked U.S. facilities in the Gulf. The Jabal al-Dukhan radar station in Bahrain was hit.

And on June 14 the two sides announced an agreement. It came directly after the fiercest three days. On June 17, President Trump and President Pezeshkian sign the memorandum of understanding, and on June 18 the U.S. Navy lifts the blockade. In this episode, the scene in which the command post in the Alborz is hit by missiles on the night of June 10–11, the underground alternate command post, the second note, and Davood’s messages are all the author’s invention. This novel does not know which facilities were in fact targeted that night.

Author’s Note
The June 10 StrikesReal event
U.S. forces hit multiple targets in Iran in an attack that included 49 Tomahawks. The full target list has not been made public. The scene in which the command post in the Alborz is hit is fictional.
Jabal al-DukhanReal event / June 11
The radar station at the highest point in Bahrain (134 meters above sea level) was hit in an Iranian attack. The scene of Kim Junho in the shelter is fictional.
The Settebello and BemaniReal event / June 10
Three Indian crew members died aboard the tanker Settebello. A local official said the drinking-water facility in Bemani was destroyed, leaving about 20,000 people without water.
The June 14 AgreementReal event
The announcement of an agreement by the two sides. The memorandum of understanding was signed on June 17 and is known as the “Islamabad Memorandum.” It was a framework document; the final agreement was to be negotiated separately within 60 days of it.
“O, farewell, honest soldier”Literature
Hamlet, Act 1, Scene 1. Marcellus’s parting words to Francisco, the sentry going off watch (O, farewell, honest soldier).
The Second Korean Ship to TransitFact and fiction
After the Universal Winner, one LNG carrier belonging to a Korean shipping company transited the strait on June 10 (Ministry of Oceans and Fisheries briefing, July 1). The “captain whose daughter is sitting the college entrance exam” and his text message are fictional.
