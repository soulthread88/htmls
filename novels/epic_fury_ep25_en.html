Islamabad, Pakistan — Foreign Ministry annex, the room with no number
April 25, 2026 (Sat.) · Evening (Pakistan local time) — Ceasefire Day 18 · War Day 57
Scene 1 / 5

The attaché came straight from the airport.

Still in uniform. He shut the door. He took a cigarette pack from his inside pocket. From inside the foil of the pack he took a slip half the size of a palm and set it on the desk. Then he went out without saluting. For twenty years, everyone who left something in this room had gone out like that.

Assistant Secretary Tariq Mehmood unfolded the slip. Persian. A woman’s hand. Written without hurry.

The one who asked was a person, but the one who answered was not only a person. Turn nothing on at the top of the hour. — Long live the king.

He read it three times. His Persian he had learned reading Hafez at the university in Lahore. Not a diplomat’s Persian. The Persian of poems. So even if he didn’t know what the first sentence said, he knew what it was afraid of. In the land where the Mughal court wrote poems in Persian, that language was still alive.

At the last words he took off his glasses.

Long live the king. Someone in Langley must have thought this was clever. A password. A quotation. Something only the man over there would recognize. But in the country this slip was going to, those words were no quotation. They were the slogan of a dynasty thrown out forty-seven years ago. A country where people beyond counting had gone into the streets this winter and not come back. If this slip came out of the wrong pocket, the owner of that pocket would get no chance to explain.

Americans were always like that. Frighteningly careful about machines, and frighteningly casual about people. It was the same in 1953, his teacher used to say. They drew up their plans with great precision, and counted roughly the lives of the people laid under those plans.

After the first round in Islamabad broke down, Pakistan kept up its diplomacy to sit the United States and Iran down again, and began calling it not a single round of talks but the “Islamabad process.” Ahead of the second round, Pakistani intelligence told reporters that Foreign Minister Araghchi would come to Islamabad to meet the American side, but Araghchi did not come. President Trump canceled the delegation’s trip and said that if Iran wanted to talk, it could call.

That week he learned shame. What the host feels when the guest he announced doesn’t come. For thirty years he had carried paper between the two capitals, and the paper always arrived. This time the room it was going to was empty.

But now on his desk was paper meant not for a room but for one man.

He did the arithmetic. A mailman’s arithmetic.

The addressee. He didn’t know the name. What he knew was the end of a line. The far end of the dormant line the Quetta relay had been holding. The air defense command post in the Alborz. In that building, not many people could touch that line.

The road. It could not go through Iran’s official document channels. Envelopes get opened. The herb traders at the border got as far as Zahedan but stopped at the gate of a military installation. That left the attaché at the embassy in Tehran. A mediating country’s attaché could, under the pretext of monitoring the ceasefire, ask to pay his respects to air defense commanders. Shake hands, drink tea, and leave a folded paper under a saucer when he stood up. There was one man in Tehran who knew how to do that.

Time. For a meeting request to go through Iran’s General Staff and be approved would take seven days at the soonest. Maybe ten.

Risk. Those words.

He picked up his fountain pen. A mailman should not touch another man’s letter. In thirty years he had never once done it. But he knew the face of the woman who wrote this slip. In the small meeting room at the Foreign Ministry, the woman who put the one sheet he handed her into her inside pocket. If she were in this room now he would have asked her. Is it all right if this man dies?

He folded the bottom of the slip up to cover the words. He pressed the crease with his thumbnail. He did not cut it. Instead he wrote one line for the attaché on another sheet.

If the recipient asks “Who sent this?”, unfold the crease and show him. If he does not ask, do not unfold it — bring it back.

A man who knows to ask will recognize the answer. To a man who doesn’t ask, those words are only a noose. He left that judgment to the man who would look the colonel in the eye across a teacup. A machine can’t be given a judgment like that. That is why there are still mailmen.

Americans were always like that. Frighteningly careful about machines, and frighteningly casual about people.

Seoul, South Korea, Eunpyeong-gu — KAIA Lab, air-gapped network operations room
April 25, 2026 (Sat.) · 11:50 p.m. (Korea time) — Ceasefire Day 18
Scene 2 / 5

The table came out at exactly that time.

Kang Yujin was alone in the empty lab on Saturday night. She kept to go to work as usual even on Saturday. The truth was she couldn’t stay home. 23:50. The time the table went out on April 6. She wanted to see the screen at that time with her own eyes.

At the end of the log, lines sprouted. Four rows.

expected: interval 211 / window: 04-28 02:30–02:34 UTC / confidence 0.34 / basis: stated intent, single document, draft status.

And under them, before she could reach out, a fifth line was stamped.

output → channel KR-7: sent 23:50:02.

Two seconds.

She pressed her back into the chair. Yoon Jaeha had said: tell me before it goes out. She had nodded. Neither of them knew. That before it goes out meant two seconds. In the time it takes a person to go down the stairs, take the subway and reach the library’s third floor, a table crosses the Pacific ten thousand times.

The giving side ought to know what it’s giving, he had said. The giving side didn’t even know when it gave. This country had lent its ears and didn’t know when those ears spoke.

She read the table again. This time not as a frightened person but as the one who made the model.

Two hundred eleven. A new number. Not one-twenty-seven. The model was saying the beat would change. The basis column was different. On April 6 it was pattern continuation, a pattern carrying on. Today it was stated intent. A document had come in in which someone wrote that they would do it. In draft form.

And the confidence. Point three four.

She looked at that number a long time. For the first time in three months she was proud of what she had made. The model knew. One document, a draft at that, an intention not supported anywhere else. That is weak ground. So it wrote point three four. One in three. More likely not to happen, the model said with its own mouth.

Can your model say it doesn’t know what it doesn’t know?

It can. It is saying it. The problem is the far end. The April 6 table said point eight one, and at that time the thing happened. If whatever was at the far end didn’t read the number and read only the time, point three four would be read exactly like point eight one. April 28, 02:30. Three days away.

She picked up the phone and put it down. No phone, he had said. Thursday at ten. Five days away. The table’s time was three days away.

In a corner of the operations screen was a field she had not used once in three months. operator annotation. A field where a person could attach a line to the model’s output and send it out through the same channel. The design document said “for correction in case of output error.” She was the one who made that field too. Without thinking about who would use it.

The output wasn’t wrong. There was nothing to correct. But there was something to add.

She wrote. In English. Deleted it and wrote again. It had to be short. Whether what read it at the other end was a person or a procedure, it would not read something long.

OPERATOR NOTE re: 04-25 23:50 output. Confidence 0.34 is LOW. Basis is one unverified draft. This is NOT a forecast of an event. Do not schedule against it. — Y.K., system operator.

Over the send button she stopped a moment. Act as usual, he had said. This was not usual. An operator silent for three months was speaking to the other side for the first time. The other side would learn there was a person on this side. That the person was reading the tables.

She pressed it.

operator annotation → channel KR-7: sent 00:21:40.

No answer came. That channel was not a channel answers came back on. Turning off the lights and leaving, she thought. The soldier at the beacon tower had lit the fire and then, for the first time, set up a sign beside it. This fire is not certain. Whether the next mountain could see the sign too, there was no knowing. The soldiers of Joseon had no authority to set up such a sign. She had made that authority with her own hands.

The model was saying it with its own mouth. One in three. The problem was not the machine but the far end, which read the time and not the number.

Northern Tehran, Evin — Professor Azad’s home
April 26, 2026 (Sun.) · Afternoon (Tehran local time) — Ceasefire Day 19 · War Day 58
Scene 3 / 5

All the books were standing.

Two thousand books. Two hundred a day for ten days. Professor Azad Moradi put plastic over the window and fixed the bookcase back to the wall. About thirty books that couldn’t find their places were piled on the desk. Ones whose spines had come off or that had taken water. He did not throw them out.

His nephew had gotten a six-hour pass. Captain Farhan Moradi. A civilian jacket over his uniform. He stood in the study doorway and looked up at the shelves. At ten, in this room, he had lain on the floor drawing pictures while his uncle read.

“You stood them all up.”

“Standing them is easy. Remembering where each book was is the hard part. Fifty years I lived with these books and I can’t recite my own shelves.”

He made tea. There were no sugar cubes, so he put out dates. Farhan held his cup in both hands and said nothing for a long time. Azad waited. Half of teaching is waiting.

“Uncle. I want to ask you something. A philosophy question.”

“When a soldier asks about philosophy, I get scared.”

“A man saw something. He saw it, and decided not to look into what it meant. If he looked into it he would know, and if he knew he would have to tell it or hide it. So he decided to stay not knowing. Is that a lie?”

Azad set a date pit on the plate.

“What does that man answer if someone asks him?”

“That he doesn’t know.”

“Then that’s the truth. He really doesn’t know.”

“But he decided not to know.”

“Yes. That’s the hard part.” He picked up one of the injured books on the desk. A Plato that had taken water. “Socrates said he knew that he didn’t know. That is a not-knowing you arrive at trying to know. The not-knowing of a man who went all the way and put his hand on the wall. What you’re talking about is different. It’s the not-knowing of a man who turned back at the door.”

“So it’s cowardice.”

“Don’t rush. You have to see why he turned back. There’s the man who turns back because he’s afraid of what’s behind the door, and the man who turns back to protect the person behind the door. The first is cowardice, and the second is…” He looked for the word. “Closer to courtesy. The courtesy of not going into someone else’s room. Something we’ve learned for a long time in this country. Not asking, even when there are noises next door. Because asking hurts the neighbor.”

Farhan nodded. But his uncle wasn’t finished.

“Only, that courtesy has a price. A man who turned back at the door can no longer say he bears no responsibility for whatever happens behind it. Deciding not to know was his own decision. The not-knowing may not be his, but deciding not to know is.”

“I said that. A few days ago. Almost word for word.”

“Then you didn’t come to ask. You came to check your answer.”

For the first time Farhan smiled. His ten-year-old face passed over his for a moment.

He put down his cup and took from inside his jacket not an envelope but a slip with one name on it. A lawyer’s name and office address. Enghelab Street.

“This man may bring you an envelope. It would be best if he doesn’t. If he does, open it. One sheet. There are a few numbers and two times on it.”

“What numbers?”

“Don’t ask. What you don’t know now protects you.”

Azad took the slip. What he had just said came back into his own lap. The courtesy of turning back at the door. And its price. All his life the philosopher had explained other people’s lives. Today for the first time his own life asked for an explanation.

“Farhan. All my life I’ve only read other people’s records. I’ve never kept one.”

“That’s why it’s you. Someone who knows how to read should keep it. And for two months you’ve been writing on the backs of prescription slips. Every day.”

“That was children’s names and the weather.”

“That’s a record.”

After his nephew left, Azad thought a long time about where to put the slip. Then he opened the children’s Shahnameh. The part about Rostam and Sohrab. The page where the drawing his nephew made at ten was tucked in. He put the slip behind the drawing. In the drawing two men on horseback faced each other. The boy had drawn a question mark over each one’s head. A thousand-year-old story held one page of record.

“The not-knowing may not be his, but deciding not to know is.” A man who turned back at the door can no longer say he bears no responsibility for what happens behind it.

Virginia, USA — a footpath along the Potomac River
April 26, 2026 (Sun.) · Morning EDT — Ceasefire Day 19
Scene 4 / 5

The chief asked her to walk by the river Sunday morning.

He told her to leave her phone in the car. From that, Leila Shirazi guessed the weight of what she would hear today. The river was high with spring water. The chief wore an old windbreaker and looked ten years older than he did in the office.

“Know what PC stands for?”

“No. I’ve only seen it as an intake account name.”

“Persian Code.” He talked as he walked. “A program that started in 2019. Fort Meade and we each put in half. Pour in every signal Iran’s chain of command gives off, and let a machine learn the grammar by which that country’s decisions come down. Who calls whom when, and what follows. What rhythm the air defense net breathes in.”

“Did it work?”

“Too well. Around 2023 it started calling things before we did. The trouble was nobody could explain why it was right. It couldn’t explain itself either. When an analyst writes a judgment in an assessment, she has to give the basis. That’s what you’ve done for sixteen years. That thing wrote only ‘pattern’ in the basis field.”

Leila knew the term. Analytic judgment (integrated). That blunt phrase in the source field.

“In December 2024 there was a review. Can judgments that can’t be explained go into the assessment chain? I sat in that room. Seven of us. Five said no. I was one of the five.” He stopped walking a moment. “The program was canceled. On paper.”

“On paper.”

“What got canceled was the name. The budget line. The thing itself didn’t go dark. A year later, last November, it came back. Through a different door. The rules kept unexplainable machine judgments out of our assessment chain. But they didn’t stop us from receiving an ally’s analytic product as reference material.”

Leila stopped. The sound of the river was loud.

“So they routed it outside. Gave our data to them, and took back what their machine spat out as an ally’s product.”

“Laundering. Not money — the source. Something banned if it comes out of here becomes reference material once it’s crossed the sea and come back. One intake account, one liaison channel, and people over there to run the machine. The people over there probably don’t know half of what they’re running.”

“Who did it?”

“One of the two in that review who said yes is on seven now.” He walked again. “You’ll find out the name yourself soon. I can’t say it yet. I might be wrong.”

For a long time neither spoke. A jogger went by. A couple with a dog went by. Sunday morning people. People who didn’t know what was reading what on the other side of the earth. The citizens of a democracy walked by the river not knowing what was being run in their name.

“Sir. You said it was right. It got the ceasefire right too. It said give them a door, and when we gave them a door they took it. Fewer people died. Is that wrong?”

“Someone asked the same thing in 2024,” he said. “I answered that being right and being trustworthy are not the same. That something you don’t know why it’s right, you don’t know when it’ll be wrong. And that on the day it’s wrong, we won’t even know it was wrong. Because there’ll be no basis to question.”

“Is that still your answer?”

“I don’t know.” He was honest. “On the night of April 7 I pounded the desk too. Because I was glad. I didn’t know then where that sentence came from. Even if I had, I’d have pounded it. That’s what’s frightening, Leila. As long as it’s doing the right thing, nobody tries to turn it off.”

He took a folded paper from his pocket. Not a printout. His own handwriting.

“Sam looked at it for me Friday evening. Your draft. Saved at 15:20, and at 15:21 that account took it. One minute.”

“It ate it.”

“It ate it. Now we wait for it to spit it out. If it shows up in tomorrow morning’s annex, that confirms it reads your documents and not your head. And if anything at all happens in that sky at 02:30 Z on Tuesday —”

“It means someone moved an aircraft on one unapproved draft.”

“Then I take a piece of paper not to seven but to the Inspector General. A piece of paper that isn’t in the system.” He looked at the river. “In 2024 I thought I raised my hand and shut it off. I lived a year and a half thinking so. This time I mean to see with my own eyes whether it’s off.”

Something banned if it comes out of here becomes reference material once it has crossed the sea and come back. What got laundered was not money but the source.

Alborz Mountains, Iran — Air Defense Force command post, the colonel’s office
April 26, 2026 (Sun.) · Night (Tehran local time) — Ceasefire Day 19
Scene 5 / 5 — Final

This time the counterintelligence lieutenant colonel didn’t come. He sent paper.

An orderly brought a sealed envelope. Colonel Darius Sepahvand signed for it. One sheet. The sentences were polite and left no gaps.

In connection with your post’s technical report of April 7, the following records are requested. One, all operating logs of subordinate deception emitters from March 25 to April 2. Two, the signals room duty roster and access records for the same period. Three, the originals of the weekly checklists for the dormant-line counter. Deadline: noon, April 28.

He put the paper down.

The lieutenant colonel had not backed off. He had gone back and read. The report Darius wrote to get the lieutenant out. A person with a high level of access to the network. Following that line the lieutenant colonel had gone back through the dates, and had not stopped at April 7. He had gone ten more days back. To the week the lieutenant was taken. This country’s counterintelligence did not chase people. It chased dates.

The operating log of the deception emitter. From 06:00 on March 30, one hundred twenty-seven seconds. The decoy radar by the road to Damavand. The log said test emission after maintenance. His signature was in the originator field. That couldn’t be hidden. He had no thought of hiding it. A commander switching on a decoy as a test is no crime.

But if someone set that log next to the April 7 record. One hundred twenty-seven seconds next to one hundred twenty-seven seconds.

The lieutenant colonel would. That was his job.

Less than forty-eight hours left. Darius went to the window. Down the mountain the lights of Tehran were spread out. More lights than two weeks ago. Power was coming back. People were beginning to believe the ceasefire would last.

He took his wife’s old phone out of the drawer. Narges’s last message was six days ago. Istanbul. A small room on the Asian side. You could see the water from the window, she said. With no classes she was learning Turkish. Baba, people here say “olsun” for “it’s okay.” It means let it be. I like that word.

He didn’t write an answer. The fewer letters that went out on this phone, the better.

There were three choices. Hand over the log as it was. Let the lieutenant colonel set the two numbers side by side, and answer when the question came. Coincidence. Not say it was his brother’s service number. Say that when a soldier picks a number, he picks one he knows without meaning to.

Hand over the log altered. He threw that thought out in a second. Farhan had done that once, and that one line was now around three men’s necks.

And the third. Not hand it over. Go to a place where he wouldn’t have to. He looked at that thought a long time. Knowing what it meant.

His father’s prayer beads were on the desk. His brother’s photo was not. On the day the war began he had taken his brother’s photo down and put it in the drawer. He couldn’t bear for a seventeen-year-old face to watch what happened in this room. A boy who died in one war watching his brother in another.

Noon Tuesday. He looked at the calendar. That day was April 28. A month ago on that same date, before dawn, he had spoken to the other side for the first time. He hadn’t known then an answer would come. The answer came. Once as a light, once as a name tag in the sky. In the twenty-seven days since, the other side had been quiet.

What was the person over there doing now? He didn’t know that person. Not the face, not the name, not whether a man or a woman. He only knew the first sentence that person chose. Halt. Who goes there? Show your light. When he got that sentence he had thought he’d heard it somewhere. Where, he never could remember.

There is a man who sets one hundred twenty-seven seconds next to one hundred twenty-seven seconds. That is his job. And that man has said he will wait until noon on Tuesday.

Background Analysis
The Arithmetic of the Blockade — A Date Called April 26

After the ceasefire was extended with no end date, the pressure moved from the sky to the sea. The Pentagon estimated Iran’s lost oil revenue from the blockade at $4.8 billion between April 13 and May 1, and said 31 tankers carrying 53 million barrels of Iranian crude were “trapped” inside the Gulf. On April 22 President Trump put Iran’s losses at $500 million a day.

An analyst at an American research institute estimated that the blockade was costing Iran $400 million a day, and that if it was not lifted by April 26, storage would fill and wells could be permanently damaged or made unusable. Lloyd’s List reported that through April 20 at least 26 ships had slipped through the blockade line in both directions, eleven of them tankers that left the Gulf of Oman carrying Iranian cargo. The blockade was tight, but it was not without gaps.

The five scenes of this episode are all the author’s invention. The “Persian Code” program, the 2024 review and the device of changing the source by routing through an ally do not depict any real agency’s real program. What this novel asks with that device is one thing. May we use a machine that is right without knowing why it is right?
