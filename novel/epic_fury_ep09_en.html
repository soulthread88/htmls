Pakistan — Islamabad, Foreign Ministry building
March 25, 2026 (Wed.) · 9:20 a.m. Pakistan time — War Day 26
Scene 1 / 7

A bundle of paper. Fifteen points.

When Tariq Mehmood got the document he counted the pages first. Seven. Seven pages in hard English. He was the Foreign Ministry’s additional secretary for West Asia. Three weeks ago, in the reception room of a villa with a broken air conditioner, he had memorized a number that belonged to no one. Seventy-two. That number had evaporated before it reached Washington. This time there was paper. Paper does not evaporate.

He looked out the window. The Margalla Hills were sunk in morning fog. Islamabad was a quiet city. Too well planned, the roads too wide. A city that did not go with war. In the summer of 1971 Kissinger had claimed a stomachache, disappeared near here, and flown to Beijing. Then too this city had handed over someone else’s secret. Now it stood in the middle of the most complicated war in the world.

On March 25 Pakistan delivered the U.S. fifteen-point ceasefire plan to Iran. According to Pakistani officials, it included sanctions relief, a rollback of Iran’s nuclear program, limits on missiles, and the reopening of the Strait of Hormuz. The White House declined to comment on the plan or on whom in Iran it was negotiating with. Pakistan shares a long border with Iran, has represented Iran’s interests through an interests section at its embassy in Washington, and is a nuclear-armed state with a defense relationship with Saudi Arabia.

He turned the pages one at a time. It was a document wrapped in the language of diplomacy. Take off the wrapping and the sentences were short. Put it down. Then we will let you go. Thirty years of experience told him: Tehran will not take this paper. But handing over a paper was not useful only when it was accepted. A paper would come back. On it would be the conditions Tehran had written with its own hand for the first time. The distance between two sheets of paper. Measuring it was his job.

The delivery was done by ten in the morning. In his notebook he wrote down only the time. Not the contents. Not writing it down. That was still the rule of this work.

A diplomat is a man who hands over the impossible as if it were possible. There is one difference from an actor. When this performance fails, people die.

Diplomatic Analysis · Diplomatic Analysis
Fifteen Points and Five Conditions — Two Sheets of Paper

The U.S. fifteen points (outline as described by Pakistani officials) — rollback of Iran’s nuclear program · limits on missile capabilities · reopening of the Strait of Hormuz · sanctions relief in return.

Iran’s five conditions (announced by state media March 25) — an end to attacks and to the assassination of its leaders · a mechanism to make sure the war never happens again · war reparations · an end to hostilities on all fronts · Iran’s exercise of sovereignty over the Strait of Hormuz.

One side told the other to put down what it had. The other told the first to pay for what it had done. Only one word appeared on both papers. Hormuz. And what the two countries meant by that word was exactly opposite.

Langley, Virginia — CIA Iran Operations Center
March 25, 2026 (Wed.) · 1:45 a.m. EDT / 9:15 a.m. in Tehran — War Day 26
Scene 2 / 7

Leila Shirazi had written her resignation letter three times.

The first time the day South Pars burned. The second time at dawn the day after Natanz was hit. The third time now, a little before two in the morning. She wrote it and did not save it. She put the cursor over the save button, held for a beat, and closed the window. All three times the same. It meant, decide tomorrow. When tomorrow came, so did another dawn. She was back in front of the keyboard.

The fifteen-point document had crossed her desk too. The technical annex to the nuclear clauses. Number of centrifuges, enrichment ceiling, inspection intervals. She had written those numbers. Sentences her hand had written were going now through Islamabad to Tehran. To the city where her aunt lived. The document went in the name of a state. The numbers came from one person’s hand.

Tehran would reject it. She knew that. The document was closer to a surrender than to a peace offer. Then why write it? To leave a record. To make proof that a hand had been held out. Or as a procedure: confirm the rejection, move to the next stage.

She read one line of the annex again. The list of facilities subject to inspection. Natanz, Isfahan, Arak. And Fordow. She was the one who had asked to put that word in. The draft that came down from above did not have Fordow. She asked why. The answer was one line. “Facility already inoperable.” That was the answer that came back to the person who, four days before, had reported its pulse speeding up to 5.2 seconds. She wrote the word in herself. Whether it was still there in the final version, after the chain of approval, she did not know.

She wrote the cable.

Iranian rejection of the fifteen points expected with high confidence. From Tehran this is a surrender document. But if a counterproposal comes with the rejection, it is a sign the door has not closed. Recommendation: do not kill the counterproposal. Reply via Islamabad. Analyst’s view: this war ends when a formula is found that lets both sides say they won. We have not yet found that formula.

Send. She leaned back in her chair. In the corner of the screen the window with the resignation letter was open. She closed it. Without saving.

Diplomacy is finding a way to say you are winning when everyone is losing. That way does not exist yet.

Kuwait City — Salmiya, a pharmacy
March 25, 2026 (Wed.) · 12:40 p.m. Kuwait time — War Day 26
Scene 3 / 7

Fire was more frightening in the daytime. A fire at night is light. A fire in the day is smoke.

Fatima al-Rashid saw it from behind the pharmacy counter. A black column stood up in the southern sky. Toward the airport. The customers all turned at once toward the glass door. Nobody screamed. Twenty-six days had taken the screaming out of people.

On March 25 Iranian drones hit the fuel depot at Kuwait International Airport and set it on fire. Kuwaiti authorities said they had intercepted six other drones. The fire burned for three days and was not put out until March 28. The same day in Jordan debris from an intercepted Iranian missile fell near Amman, and a missile fell in a field near Hadera, site of Israel’s largest power plant, without causing damage. In northwest Iran eleven people were killed in airstrikes. Iran claimed it had fired Ghader cruise missiles at the Abraham Lincoln; the ship was not damaged.

The smoke from burning jet fuel was different. She was a pharmacist and she knew what was in that smoke. In 1991, when a retreating army set fire to the oil wells, this country had lived once under a black sky. She took boxes of masks off the shelf and stacked them on the counter. She did not charge for them. Three people already asking for asthma inhalers. Eleven in stock.

The radio said Pakistan had carried the American paper to Tehran. A peace plan, it said. Fatima listened to the radio and watched the smoke. Same day, same hour. On one side a paper was crossing over, and on the other the drones were crossing back.

Her youngest, five, stuck her head out from under the counter. The kindergarten had been closed for three weeks.

“Mama, is that a cloud?”

“No,” she said. “Clouds are white.”

Negotiations and bombing do not take turns. They come together, on the same day, under the same sky. It is not a contradiction. It is how a war ends—if it ends.

Bahrain — Mina Salman Port, the coalition liaison cell at the headquarters of the U.S. Fifth Fleet
March 25, 2026 (Wed.) · 4:50 p.m. Bahrain time — War Day 26
Scene 4 / 7

Lieutenant Kim Junho laid two pieces of paper side by side and read them.

One was a wire service outline of the fifteen points. One was the five conditions Iranian state television had just put out. Diplomatic documents were not a lieutenant’s territory. But he knew what the numbers meant. Fifteen was what America wanted. Five was what Iran demanded. The distance between them was why the ships whose names he knew were still riding at anchor. At Panmunjom it had taken two years to close a distance like that. And all that time, men kept dying on the line.

Sarah Callahan came in with her press badge around her neck. Today her notebook was in her hand. It meant she had come as a reporter.

“Iran rejected it,” she said. “So is it over, Commander?”

The question was for Lieutenant Commander Mark Henderson at the next desk. Henderson set down his coffee.

“Ma’am, have you ever seen a negotiation where the first answer was yes? The first answer has to be no for the next thing to get said. Rejection is part of negotiating.”

“Can I quote you?”

“As ‘a U.S. Navy official.’”

Sarah wrote it down. Then she looked at Kim Junho.

“And you, Lieutenant?”

Kim Junho put his finger on the last line of the five conditions. Exercise of sovereignty over the Strait of Hormuz.

“This isn’t a rejection,” he said. “It’s a price tag. For the last ten days Iran has been picking which ships get through the strait. Ships like India’s, from countries it can talk to. That’s already exercising sovereignty. What the paper says is, recognize it. And—” He took a moment to pick the words. “For the ships I count, it doesn’t matter who has sovereignty. The door just has to open.”

“Can I quote that too?”

“No.” He laughed. It was the first time in this war Sarah had seen him laugh. “I’m not an official. I’m a spectator.”

Out the window the western sky was red. It was the sunset. To the north, they said, Kuwait’s airport was still burning. It no longer took Sarah even a second to tell sunset from fire.

A rejection is something said, too. More said than silence. Three weeks ago there was no one in Tehran even to say “we.”

Alborz Mountains, Iran — north of Tehran, Iranian Air Defense Force command post
March 25, 2026 (Wed.) · afternoon, Tehran time — War Day 26
Scene 5 / 7

Colonel Darius Sepahvand could guess who had written the five conditions.

It was not written by one man. It was the things that had gone back and forth over the command network these three weeks. We must get reparations. This must never happen again. The strait is ours. Broken pieces of anger had come together into five lines. When talk becomes writing, even if a hundred people said it, the writing becomes one voice. And the writing becomes history.

The signals officer read out the good news. Kuwait airport. Fuel depot hit. Fire confirmed. Some men in the command post nodded. Darius should have nodded too. It was a reflex of the job. The reflex came up as far as the back of his neck and stopped.

Narges’s voice was there. The call eleven days ago. Before it cut off, his daughter had said one more thing. Father, what did the people of Kuwait ever do to us? He had not answered. Not answering was a third answer, neither yes nor no. How his daughter had read it, he did not know.

When he enlisted, his daughter had not been born. When she was born, he was already a man of the organization. Watching her grow up, he had hoped the organization and his daughter would face the same way. It took twenty years for that hope to break. The child did not believe in her father’s organization. The organization did not believe in the child’s freedom. He lived between them.

He read the five conditions again. Reparations. Guarantees. Sovereignty. In the summer of 1988, on the day it accepted the ceasefire, this country had called it a poisoned chalice. If these conditions were met and the war ended, how would Narges look at him? As a father who won? Or as the man who nodded while Kuwait’s airport burned?

Today he did not nod. Nobody noticed. That was the only thing he could do today. And it was nothing.

A country counts by the hundred million. A person feels one at a time. The tragedies of history come out of that difference in speed. The answer to his daughter’s question was still in his mouth.

Tehran — Imam Khomeini Hospital, makeshift classroom in the basement shelter
March 25, 2026 (Wed.) · 7:30 p.m. Tehran time — War Day 26
Scene 6 / 7

Nariman came carrying cocoa.

Professor Azad Moradi looked up from his folding chair. A seven-year-old was coming toward him holding a paper cup in both hands. She had already spilled a little. To keep from spilling more she had the tip of her tongue out.

It had been six days since he started coming to this basement. Ten minutes on foot from the university to the hospital. He had come for his blood pressure pills and seen the children gathered in the shelter. The children of doctors and nurses. The schools had been closed for twenty-six days. That day, instead of Heraclitus, he told them the hero stories of the Shahnameh. The next day he came again. It was better than an empty lecture hall. The children called him Grandpa. In seventy-two years it was the first time anyone had called him that.

“Grandpa, drink this if you’re cold. The canteen lady gave it to me.”

He took the paper cup. It was warm. It was sweet. Even in a war, cocoa was sweet. He thought it was strange, and then he thought it was not strange. The world can change and sweet things are still sweet. That was what it meant for civilization to go on.

“Nariman, did you hear that some men from Pakistan brought a paper today?”

“Uh-huh. But our country said no.”

“So it was.”

“So why did they bring the paper? If they were going to say no.”

Azad could not answer. There are times when a philosopher of seventy-two cannot answer the question of a seven-year-old. This was one of them.

“It’s how grown-ups do it,” he managed to say. “First you say no once, and then you start the real talk.”

“That’s weird.”

“Yes. It is weird.”

Nariman climbed up on the chair next to him and swung her legs. After a long while the child asked:

“Grandpa. When the war’s over, is spring prettier?”

Again he could not answer. Somewhere upstairs the child’s mother was starting her night shift. Far off a siren rose and stopped. In the war of the cities in 1988, the children of this city had heard that sound underground too. The two of them drank their cocoa without speaking.

A child asks: if they were going to say no, why bring the paper? A philosopher of seventy-two cannot answer. That silence is the truth of diplomacy.

Langley, Virginia — CIA Iran Operations Center
March 25, 2026 (Wed.) · 11:55 p.m. EDT / 7:25 a.m. March 26 in Tehran — night of War Day 26
Scene 7 / 7 — Final

Five lines came in.

Leila Shirazi read them in the Persian original. An end to attacks and assassinations. A guarantee there would be no more war. Reparations. An end of war on all fronts. Sovereignty over the strait. Five sentences. Behind each sentence stood an old wound of that country. The coup of 1953—the papers for it are in this building’s archive too. Eight years of war. The years of sanctions. And these twenty-six days.

It was not an enemy’s history. It was the history of the country where her aunt lived. Of the country where she had lived until she was twelve. The history of someone who remembered the smell of Shiraz. Rosewater ice cream. The dust in the carpet shops. The smell of dill and fava beans in her aunt’s kitchen.

She opened the file with the resignation letter. This time she pressed save.

Then she deleted it right away.

Not tonight. There was still work to do. The five lines had to be analyzed. A way had to be found to keep the Islamabad road alive. That was something she could do, here, now. It was a night when this document weighed more than a resignation letter. And if she left, there would not be one person left in this building who read Fordow’s pulse.

Midnight passed. She began to type. Separating what could be bargained over in the five lines from what could not. Sorting what Tehran really wanted from what it was calling out to raise the price.

(1) End to assassinations — real demand. Survival of the leadership. (2) Guarantee against recurrence — real demand. Form negotiable. (3) Reparations — leverage. Can be traded for lifting sanctions and frozen assets. (4) End of war on all fronts — demand includes Lebanon. Israel is the variable. (5) Sovereignty over the strait — wording non-negotiable. Substance adjustable through transit rules.

Missing: nuclear. No enrichment anywhere in the five conditions. Same silence as the March 12 statement. Tehran has not yet put that card on the table.

That was her job. Doing that job well was all she could do in this war. The minutes from three months ago could not be taken back. The sentences she wrote from now on could be different.

The document that ends a war usually comes from the hands of the people who wrote the documents that started it. Whether it is punishment or atonement, only the one writing knows.

In Seoul it was one in the afternoon on March 26. Kang Yujin’s model had taken in the fifteen points and the five conditions coming eight hours apart and moved the predicted end of the war two days earlier. For two days she had been looking for that variable. A variable she had not put in. Nowhere in the code was there a term called gap. But the model knew it. For the first time she felt a chill go down her back in front of something she had made. What else did it know?

Background Analysis
The Caspian Route — Moscow’s Place in This War

On March 18 Israel hit at least five Iranian navy vessels in the Caspian Sea. Explosions were heard in Bandar Anzali. The target was not ships but a route. Russia and Iran had been shipping weapons and parts across the Caspian in both directions, especially equipment for Shahed-family drones.

The Shahed-136 is an Iranian-made one-way attack drone that became known to the world when Russia used it in the war in Ukraine in 2022. The arithmetic of stopping drones worth tens of thousands of dollars with interceptors worth millions ruled the skies of the Gulf in this war too. On March 5 Ukraine’s president said the United States had asked for help countering Shaheds, and on March 28 Iran would claim to have struck a Ukrainian counter-drone system warehouse in Dubai.

On March 11 Russia abstained in the Security Council on the resolution condemning attacks in the Gulf, and it condemned the bombing of Natanz as a flagrant violation of international law. Moscow did not come into this war, but it stood everywhere along its edges.
