Scene 1 / 5

It was the hundred forty-first morning. The four lines had come on time.

Leila Shirazi read them. All hands. The name in the confirmation box. Not one letter had changed. What had changed was this building. Since the first week of September, a different name had been on the door at the end of the seventh-floor hallway.

Evan Braxton was forty-six and had come over from the Pentagon. In his first week he had every file on the Iran desk brought to him and read them all. He read the four channel opinions too. Then he called Leila in and asked one thing for ten minutes. What has this man done for six months? She answered. Every day, at the same hour, he wrote the same name. He nodded. She couldn’t tell whether the nod was agreement or a note to himself.

Six days ago a memo had come down from that room. Half a page.

Request for review, ZARRIN. In preparation for planning after November, direct confirmation of the current state of the Eastern Sector 3 air defense network is needed. Submit a channel opinion by October 9 on whether the source can be woken, and if so, how. — E.B.

On October 7, U.S. media reported that the Pentagon had told Central Command to prepare for a possible resumption of major combat with Iran. According to the reports, the targets under review included Iran’s energy facilities and nuclear sites. In late September Iran had put forward a “seven-day plan” — it would reopen the Strait of Hormuz within a week if the United States lifted the blockade and waived oil sanctions — and on September 26 President Trump said he was rejecting it.

After November. The memo used the phrase like a date. Leila knew what it pointed to. A map to lay over the target list. One square on that map was Eastern Sector 3, and in the middle of that square were three floors underground.

At nine she went up to seven. The room was the way it had been when Rowell had it. The desk in the same place, the same blinds on the window. Only the plant by the window was gone. Rowell had carried it out the day she left. People go, the room stays, and the room becomes the new person’s.

“You’ll have the opinion tomorrow.”

“I know. Today I just wanted to hear one thing. In the fourth opinion you wrote that you won’t give a number. That a number with no basis is a lie.”

“Yes.”

“I worked in numbers at the Pentagon for sixteen years. I’ve seen people decide without numbers. Usually they leave bigger numbers behind. Casualty counts.”

Leila didn’t answer. He wasn’t wrong. That he was right made her colder.

“If we wake him, what can he do for us?”

“Probably nothing. The moment you wake him he becomes someone else. A man who for six months took nothing from anyone becomes a man who takes his first tasking from us. From that day the four lines he writes aren’t reports anymore. They’re replies. I’ve never read his replies. I’ve only read reports.”

Braxton put down his pen. He looked out the window for a moment. In October the sky toward the Potomac hung low.

“Let’s talk tomorrow.”

A little after two in the afternoon, the caption on the wall screen changed. Someone at the desk turned up the sound.

On October 8 President Trump wrote on Truth Social, “we will not be attacking Iran at any time prior to the Midterm Elections to be held in the United States on November 3rd.” He said, “We are having productive discussions with the Islamic Republic of Iran.” But he said the blockade of the Strait of Hormuz “will remain in full force and effect,” and claimed that 22 million barrels had passed through the strait the night before, “with not one barrel coming from, or going to, Iran.” The same day the U.S. Treasury sanctioned 17 ships that had carried Iranian oil.

The office went quiet for a moment. Then everyone talked at once. The election. Twenty-six days. Is it a ceasefire? It isn’t a ceasefire. There’s no signature. He said the blockade stays.

Sam Farrell leaned on her cubicle wall.

“Twenty-six days, they’re saying.”

“I heard.”

“Is it a ceasefire?”

“No. It’s a date. A ceasefire starts without knowing its end. This one started with the end already set.”

At four a one-line email came down from seven. Pushing back the deadline on the opinion. We have time now, until November 3. — E.B.

We have time now. She looked at the words a long time. In this building time was always spent getting ready for the next thing. For someone, twenty-six days of quiet were twenty-six days of getting ready. For her too. She knew what she had to get ready.

She wrote the first line of the fifth opinion that evening. Without waiting for the later deadline.

HC-05. Recommendation: remain dormant. Basis — the twenty-six days until the election are not a reason to wake the source but a reason not to. The other side will count the same days, and a man counting days will read every strange signal that comes in between them as a sign that the end has come.

Before she went home she opened her paper notebook. She wrote the date, and the line she wrote every morning. No one relieved me. I am still in this place. And under it, for the first time, she wrote a number.

26.

A ceasefire starts without knowing its end. This one started with the end already set.

Scene 2 / 5

The call came at eleven at night. Underground, the phone didn’t bring good news in daylight.

“Have you heard, Colonel?”

It was Lieutenant Colonel Naser Taheri. The counterintelligence officer hardly ever called. He sent someone or came himself. That he had called meant there was something that had to be said before the night was out.

“I heard it on the radio. No strikes before the election.”

“The General Staff meets tomorrow morning. Before that I wanted to ask you one thing, Colonel. Do you believe it?”

Darius Sepahvand looked at the wall before he answered. The wall underground had no window. Instead it had the papers he had been pinning up for four months. The ceasefire of April 8. The memorandum of June 17. On paper, neither had an end date. Both ended.

“Believe is the wrong word, Lieutenant Colonel. This time the words come with an end date. So the only thing to believe is the date. They won’t come before November 3. That is the same sentence as: they may come on November 4.”

The other end of the line was quiet for a moment.

“Yesterday their papers wrote that they were preparing a major strike. Today they say they won’t strike. In one day.”

“It isn’t one day. It’s the front and the back of the same sentence. The preparing goes on. Only now there’s a date.”

On October 8 Iranian Foreign Minister Araghchi said Iran was reviewing the U.S. response to its “seven-day plan” and would reply within days. The same day Vice President Eslami, head of Iran’s Atomic Energy Organization, said nuclear negotiations were not on the agenda, and that Iran would neither hand over its enriched uranium stockpile nor stop enriching at home. Indirect talks were going on through Qatar.

When he hung up he opened a desk drawer. Not the third drawer. The second. In it was the calendar his daughter had sent him last year. A thin calendar a bookshop in Tehran gave away to its customers. The Iranian calendar and the Western one side by side.

He found the box for October 8. Beside the Western date, in small print, was the Iranian one. Mehr 16. He found the box for November 3. Aban 12. Then he counted the boxes in between. With his finger. Twenty-six.

Another country’s election day was sitting in a box on his country’s calendar. Nothing was printed in that box. In the Iranian calendar Aban 12 was just a Tuesday. When this country’s sky would open again depended on the day people on the other side of the earth stood in line. Four hundred years ago the kings of this land must have counted the days to a foreign envoy’s arrival the same way.

Sohrab Jahangiri came in with the watch log. The lieutenant saw the colonel’s finger stopped on the calendar.

“Colonel. What should I change in tomorrow’s morning report? Everyone says an order might come from the General Staff to lower the alert level.”

“If an order comes, change it then. Until it comes, change nothing. The four lines as they are. All hands, as it is.”

“Yes, sir.”

The lieutenant started to leave, then turned back.

“Colonel. Twenty-six days — are those days of rest, or days of waiting?”

Darius liked the question. It was the most exact question a young officer could ask.

“There hasn’t been a day of rest in this war yet. Only the days of waiting change their names. In April we called it a ceasefire, and in June we called it a memorandum. This time it has no name. Only a date. I think that’s more honest, if anything.”

When Sohrab had gone, he pinned the calendar to the wall. Beside the papers for the ceasefire and the memorandum. Then he picked up a pen and drew a small line through the box for October 8. One.

He thought of the hundred twenty-seven seconds at dawn on March 30. Then he hadn’t counted days. He had counted seconds. As many as the last three digits of his brother’s service number. Seven months later he counted days. The man counting seconds had counted for one person. The man counting days counted for twelve. The watch crew of twelve, all hands.

And for the first time he wondered. Was the person across the sea counting these days too? And if so, what were twenty-six days to that person? Days of rest, or days of waiting? Or the day someone would come to that person’s desk and say it was time to wake him?

He did not write the thought in any box on the calendar. Not writing it was his job.

They won’t come before November 3. That was the same sentence as: they may come on November 4.

The captain used the word window. Instead of open.

Lieutenant. I hear it’s twenty-six days. Is the window open?

Lieutenant Kim Junho got the text at six in the morning. One of the two lines — the tanker captain. The man who had decided in June not to leave. The man who had said his cargo was still on the pier. The cargo was all loaded by August. Since then the ship had sat at anchor for two months watching the sea turn rough again.

On October 8 U.S. Central Command said that in recent months U.S. forces had supported the shipment of over 1.25 billion barrels of crude from Gulf partners through the Strait of Hormuz. Over the same period, it said, Iran’s oil had not moved through the strait because of an “ironclad” U.S. blockade. In late August U.S. officials had confirmed clearing about a hundred suspected mines from the main shipping lanes.

He didn’t answer the captain right away. Since June he hadn’t pinned down a date. The first line he drew back then had been right by luck, not skill. An officer who trusts luck twice loses a ship the third time.

Instead he stood in front of the chart. The chart on the wall was his alone now. Henderson had gone back to San Diego in the summer. What he left was pencil lines, coffee rings, and one line in the lower right corner of the chart. Lines, not dates. — M.H.

He picked up a pencil. He drew a thin line along the main lanes they said had been cleared of mines in August. The way in and the way out. He measured the distance from the anchorage off Dubai to those lanes. He measured the time it would take to get out to the Gulf of Oman. At fourteen knots, a little under a day.

On the desk was a copy of the report he had sent up on September 11. Three pages. Headquarters hadn’t answered since. Instead, in early September there were reports that the United States had asked Korea to send forces to Hormuz, and then reports that the defense ministry was sending a survey team to the UAE. The government said it was under review. Under review was a phrase that had been running since early September.

At eight he called the shipping company’s operations manager. In Seoul it was two in the afternoon.

“The captain wants to go. What does the company think?”

“We want to send her out today too, Lieutenant. Actually, we were about to send her last week and held off. A recommendation came down from upstairs to give it one more week.”

“Upstairs.”

“All I heard was that it came out of some government analysis. We don’t know the details. As it turned out, it was a good week to wait. Given this week’s announcement.”

Kim Junho wrote it in his notebook. 10.2 Recommendation to delay departure — source unknown (government analysis). He didn’t know where the recommendation came from. He did know that once people started saying the call to wait a week had been right, the next call would start pinning down dates. A call that was right becomes the basis for the next call. A call that was wrong gets forgotten. The sea didn’t keep that kind of ledger.

“One request. Main lanes only. The Iranian side is still inside the blockade zone. And when she’s out of the Gulf of Oman, please ask the captain to send me one line.”

“We will. The captain said you cross them off in that notebook of yours, Lieutenant.”

He laughed. Rumor was the fastest cargo on the sea.

He sent the captain an answer. I don’t know about windows. I know lines. Main lanes. Let me know when you’re out of the Gulf of Oman.

The answer came. One period. Understood. I’ll be back. I’ll be back. This ship wasn’t coming back. It was going home to Korea. Still, the captain wrote it that way. For a man who had spent seven months on this sea, even leaving was a round trip.

He opened the notebook. Two lines. Beside the tanker’s line he wrote the date. 10.9. He didn’t cross it off. The line through it came after one line came from the Gulf of Oman. That was the order he had kept since June.

The other line was the Namu. The repaired ship wasn’t leaving this time either. The owner said he was waiting to hear from the insurer. War insurance did not count a quiet with a date on it as quiet. Sometimes an insurer’s arithmetic was more honest than an officer’s.

I don’t know about windows. I know lines.

Scene 4 / 5

Friday’s sheet came at six. The seventeenth sheet.

Action taken on the basis of this output — Yes: 1 / No: 6.

Kang Yujin looked at the number for a long time. It wasn’t zero. The number she had kept for more than four months had become one today.

A one-line note was attached at the bottom. The first time the notes field had been used since the form changed. Referred to output of 10.2 (seven-day outlook on transit risk in the strait, raised). One maritime-related ministry recommended that one of our ships delay departure by one week. The ship is scheduled to depart 10.9.

She found the table for October 2 and opened it. The 23:50 table. Format v2, filter v1. In the transit-risk field was the annotation she had added. Raised. Basis: frequency and tone of public U.S. statements. Low confidence. This field may be wrong.

She had written that it might be wrong. Someone read that annotation and stopped a ship anyway. And six days later Washington said it would not strike. People would call that week a good week to have waited. They would say the machine got it right.

She knew it hadn’t gotten anything right. The October 2 table hadn’t foreseen an announcement a week away. Nothing had happened in between, that was all. A week in which nothing happened becomes, for the person who stopped the ship, a week that was right. That week would become the basis for the next recommendation. The next recommendation would come easier.

It was the loop she had seen across the sea in the spring. The side that reads the answer acts, and the machine counts the changed world as its own hit. Only this time the loop turned the other way. Someone didn’t move; they stopped. They didn’t send a ship out; they held one.

She called Yoon Jaeha. He was still at the office. Since the news that there would be no strike before the election, the lights on that floor hadn’t gone off.

“There’s a yes.”

“I saw. They held a ship, I hear. Not a bad outcome, is it? Nobody got hurt.”

“That’s why I’m scared. If the outcome were bad, everyone would doubt the machine. Because it’s good, everyone will start to trust it. Stopping is an action too, sir. The ship they held also moved because the machine said so. Just in the opposite direction.”

The other end of the line went quiet for a moment.

“So what will you do?”

“I’ll fix the form. One more field. Direction of action — move, stop. I’ll count stops too. And one line in the notes. A good outcome is not evidence that the output was right.”

“Will the other side accept it?”

“There’s page four of my appointment papers. The operator may suspend transmission on her own. Someone who can shut it off can surely add one field.”

Yoon Jaeha laughed. Briefly.

When she hung up she looked out the window. The last light was on the ridgeline of Bukhansan. She didn’t know where that ship might be now. She didn’t know its name. The notes field didn’t give the ship’s name. It only said it was leaving today.

She hoped briefly for the ship with no name. Without asking the machine for a probability, she just hoped. That it would get out of the Gulf of Oman safely. And that the call that had held it for a week wouldn’t grow, next time, into a call that pinned down dates.

She drafted the new form. In the bottom field she added one line by hand.

For twenty-five days, I hope this field goes back to zero. — Operator

Scene 5 / 5 — Final

The school bag was blue. Bought on the first day of Mehr. Even in a war the stationery shops opened.

Nariman came straight from school to the hospital. In this country Saturday was the day the week began. She came up the roof stairs two at a time, caught her breath in the doorway, and took a notebook out of her bag. On the inside of the back cover, boxes were drawn. Clumsy pencil boxes. Twenty-six of them.

“Grandpa. Look. Teacher says we won’t get hit until the day America has an election.”

Professor Azad Moradi sat up in his chair. The book on his knees was the sixteenth. The spine he had glued still smelled of paste. He took the girl’s notebook. The first two boxes had small crosses in them. Thursday and Friday.

“Your teacher said that?”

“Teacher says it was in the paper. Everybody in class drew boxes. Sara drew thirty, and that was wrong. I counted them for her.”

“Good. Fixing someone else’s count is hard. Harder than telling them they’re wrong.”

Maryam stood by the railing. The two keys were still in her pocket. The generator room and the pharmacy storeroom. On Thursday night, when the director heard the news and stopped by her room, she didn’t give the keys back. The director didn’t ask for them either. They both remembered April and June.

On her phone was this morning’s line. Alive. Sea’s quiet. Everyone’s counting days. I’m not. Is the shell all right?

She believed him that he wasn’t counting. In June Davood had counted three days, and on the evening of the third he had gone back out. Since then he hadn’t counted days. Sending one word every morning stood in for the days.

On October 8 President Trump said there would be no attack on Iran before the November 3 midterm elections, but that the U.S. naval blockade of Iranian ports would remain “in full force and effect.” Iran had offered to reopen the strait within a week if the blockade was lifted and oil sanctions were waived, and the two sides had not agreed to any formal ceasefire.

“Grandpa. What’s an election?”

The old man laid the notebook on his knees. He remembered 1979. He remembered the summer of 1988. In seventy-two years in this country he had seen ballot boxes many times, and many times seen things bigger than ballot boxes decide people’s days.

“It’s when the people of a country line up on the same day and each write one name on a piece of paper. Who will look after that country. The day those people line up is November 3.”

“Do they write whether to hit us or not?”

The old man couldn’t answer. A seven-year-old’s questions usually put into sentences what grown-ups never do.

“They don’t write it like that. But what they write does change our days. You could say our peace is written on another country’s calendar. On ours it’s Aban 12, just a Tuesday.”

“Then what do we write on our calendar?”

Maryam turned around. The old man looked at her. She felt she had to be the one to answer that.

“We write the word Baba sends. Every day. Not the days other people count. The days we got it.”

Nariman thought for a moment. Then she took the notebook back and, under the twenty-six boxes, drew one more. A long box. Inside it, pressing hard with her pencil, she wrote Alive. And beside it she wrote a number. How many days the word had come. She borrowed her mother’s phone and started counting from the beginning.

Azad watched her and stroked the book on his knees. Sixteen of thirty-seven. Twenty-one to go. His daughter in Canada had called again last week. Father, now they’ve even set a date. Get out before then. He didn’t say he would. He said he would think about it. If Nariman had heard, she would have said it was grown-up talk again.

The sun went down. The city’s lights began to come on. A few more than in September, it seemed. Even in a quiet with a date on it, people turned their lights on. Lights turned on by people who knew the day it ended.

For a long while Nariman looked at her mother’s phone, folding and unfolding her fingers. Then she wrote a number. Maryam looked at it. The count was a little off. A few days were missing. She didn’t fix it. Fixing someone else’s count was hard, and this count could be wrong. This count had no end date.

Our peace is written on another country’s calendar. On ours it is Aban 12, just a Tuesday.

Not the days other people count. The days we got it. Under the twenty-six boxes lay one count with no end date.

Part Two begins in that quiet with a date on it.
