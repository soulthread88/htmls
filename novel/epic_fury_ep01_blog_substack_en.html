Epic Fury: Night Over Hormuz — Episode 1: The Day the Walls Came Down

Every war begins on somebody’s morning. An old professor in Tehran, the Pentagon’s operations chief, the captain of the USS Gerald R. Ford, a CIA analyst and a 7-year-old girl under the rubble: the first morning of the war through five sets of eyes.

This is a novel inspired by real events of Feb. 28, 2026. Characters and dialogue are invented; figures in the news sections follow reporting at the time.

Tehran · an alley near Azadi Square

Every War Begins on Somebody’s Morning

Every war began on somebody’s morning. Saturday, February 28, 2026, 9:45 a.m. The sky over Tehran was blue. Unreasonably blue. Unfairly blue. Professor Azad Moradi stood in a side street by Azadi Square. Azadi. It means freedom in Persian. In this country freedom had been only a name for close to forty-seven years. Freedom in name. Liberation on a signboard.

The professor was seventy-two. Yesterday afternoon the morality police had taken three of his graduate students. The students who talked about the public sphere had vanished from it. Azad looked up at the sky. There was something in it he could not see. His bones knew first. Today’s sky is not yesterday’s. Something was coming. Rescue or ruin. He did not know yet.

Arlington, Virginia · The Pentagon, National Military Command Center

All That Was Left Was to Watch

At that same moment it was 1:15 a.m. in Washington, D.C. Across the Potomac, under the Pentagon. The order had already come down. The signatures were done and the chain was closed. All that was left for this room was to watch. Lieutenant General Faulk, director of operations for the Joint Staff, stood in front of the screens. He had not slept in thirty-one hours. He was a man who went quiet when the tension peaked. Now he was very quiet.

From the satellite Tehran was wide and ordinary. Nine million mornings. Most of them wanting what people everywhere want. To feed their children. To be left alone. Each coordinate on the screen lay over those mornings. One-fifteen. The first impact signal came up on the screen. God, let us get this right. Faulk said it without a sound.

Iran · air-defense command post, Alborz Mountains

Help Has Arrived

A command post in the Alborz Mountains. Colonel Darius Sepahvand watched his radar screens go to noise. Thirty-one years of service. Two wars. The command network fell apart in four minutes. As he watched it go, something rose in his chest that he had not expected at all. Relief. He crushed it at once. But the feeling was there.

The phone lying face down on the desk buzzed. It was BadeSaba, the prayer-calendar app. Instead of the prayer times the screen showed one line in Persian. Help has arrived. Every phone in the command post buzzed at the same moment. The bombs and the sentences arrived together. One broke buildings. The other shook obedience. A nineteen-year-old soldier lowered his rifle. “This isn’t a war. This is something else.”

Eastern Mediterranean · bridge of the USS Gerald R. Ford

Still Hands

Captain Marcus Webb had gone twenty-six hours without sleep. When the last aircraft of the first wave left the electromagnetic catapult, his hands on the bridge were perfectly still. The stiller the hands, the worse the storm inside. Two weeks ago this ship had been in the Caribbean. Twelve feet to his left, AP war correspondent Sarah Callahan had her camera up.

Admiral Rees came up on video from Bahrain. “We’ve got a Republic of Korea Navy liaison officer here. His exact words: ‘We are witnesses to history.’” At his monitor, Lieutenant Kim Junho remembered the photographs of Operation Chromite at Incheon. “Tell him,” Webb said, “we hope we get this right.” Beside him Sarah Callahan’s shutter clicked quietly.

Langley, Virginia · CIA Iran Operations Center

Two Hometowns

Dr. Leila Shirazi had been in this room for nineteen hours. An analyst born in Shiraz, Iran, in 1988. Seven months ago she had written a classified report. The only intervention capable of changing the trajectory is kinetic intervention. When she wrote that sentence she knew what she was justifying. Now her sentence was burning on three screens as columns of smoke.

The center screen showed the Evin district of Tehran. It was where her aunt Fereshte lived. A woman who made tea with rose petals. There was a column of smoke on that screen now. Leila did not cry. She spoke without a sound. I am sorry. I believe this is necessary. I am so sorry. No one in the room knew what had just happened.

Evin district, Tehran · Golestan Street (a fictional event)

Two Hundred Meters

It was 200 meters. The distance between a Revolutionary Guard communications relay and a five-story apartment building on Golestan Street. One Small Diameter Bomb drifted that far because of a sensor malfunction. A weapon that called itself the essence of precision strike. The building came down in order. The top two floors first, then the middle, then the ground floor. There was a child in that building.

Her name was Nariman. Seven years old. A gap between her front teeth. Her mother, Maryam, a nurse, lifted the concrete with her bare hands and pulled her daughter to her. Nariman was alive. War does not choose its victims by loyalty or by guilt. War chooses by distance. By the two hundred meters between a military target and a breakfast table.

Tehran · Azadi Square

The Black Curtain

Professor Azad stood at the edge of the square and watched the smoke rise downtown to the east. Beyond the square young people were singing. The women wore no hijab. He opened his journal and wrote. Today the black curtain that pressed down on us for forty-seven years was torn. The liberators came carrying bombs. Bombs do not tell the jailer from the prisoner.

I truly cannot tell whether what I see now is liberation or destruction. Probably both. A wall falls only when something breaks on both sides. God forgive us all. He sat down on the stone edge of the square, put his face in his hands, and cried. The song did not stop. That was the one thing the most precise bomb had never managed to silence.

Eastern Mediterranean · Ford Carrier Strike Group

People Not Yet Born

Captain Webb dragged the chart on the bridge display to the east. The Strait of Hormuz. Twenty-one miles between the Iranian coast and Oman’s Musandam Peninsula. In peacetime twenty percent of the world’s oil goes through that gap. For more than forty years Iran had threatened to shut that door. “How does it feel?” Sarah asked, low. “Like starting something you understand the start of and not the end.”

“That’s always true. It scares me every time.” “Me too.” The war was a little more than two hours old. The world had already changed past turning back. Below decks the chaplain prayed quietly with twenty-three sailors. Let it be over fast. Let it not be as bad as it looks. The war had only just begun. The end, when it comes, will be written by people not yet born.
