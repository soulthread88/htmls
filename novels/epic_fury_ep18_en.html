April 7, 2026 (Tue.) · 3:42 a.m. (Tehran local time) — War Day 39
Scene 1 / 7

All the screens went empty at once.

They were not off. The power was on. What should have been on them was gone. The tracks coming up from the southwest. The numbers on the national net. The status of the other command posts. The glass was lit. The world inside it had vanished.

“The net’s down!” the operator shouted. “The whole national net. Our sector radar is alive but nothing goes up the line.”

Colonel Darius Sepahvand looked at the clock. 03:42. In thirty-one years he had been through electronic warfare. Jamming covers a screen with snow. The noise boils. This was different. It was clean. As if somebody had thrown a switch.

Without meaning to, he began to count. It was a habit. A habit from the last few days.

Sixty. Ninety. One-twenty.

At one hundred twenty-seven the screens came back.

The tracks came up again. In between, they had moved. The dots from the southwest were over the Persian Gulf. Over Kharg Island.

On April 7, the United States said it had struck military sites on Kharg Island, and Iranian media reported several large explosions on the island. Kharg Island is home to the terminal through which most of Iran’s crude oil exports pass. Overnight, strikes were also reported around Tehran and in Ahvaz, Chabahar, Jask, Shiraz and Yazd.

Darius stood in front of the screen and did not move. The operators were all shouting something. He did not hear them.

One hundred twenty-seven.

It could be coincidence. He tried to believe that. A little over two minutes. About what it would take for a net to drop and come back. But he was a man who counted, and men who count are raised not to believe in coincidence. The last three digits of his brother’s service number. The time he had left the light on. The time the other side wore a name tag. And now the time this country’s entire air defense net closed its eyes.

The third one was not his doing. If the other side had done it, it was a greeting. We can do this much. But if they could shut this net from outside, why hadn’t they done it once in thirty-nine days?

Then someone inside had shut it.

Someone who knew this number. Inside this country’s net. With his number.

His back went cold. For eight days he had held on by leaning on the fact that he was not alone. Now for the first time, not being alone frightened him. Someone was deep inside this country’s net. Under the Shah and after the revolution, there had always been somebody else’s hand inside this country’s machines.

When two people share a secret, it is a promise. The moment there are three, it becomes a string in somebody’s hand.

Scene 2 / 7

The post went up a little after eight in the morning.

On April 7, President Trump wrote on social media: “A whole civilization will die tonight, never to be brought back again. I don’t want that to happen, but it probably will. However, now that we have Complete and Total Regime Change, where different, smarter, and less radicalized minds prevail, maybe something Revolutionarily Wonderful can happen, WHO KNOWS? We will find out tonight, one of the most important moments in the long and complex history of the World. 47 years of extortion, corruption, and death, will finally end. God Bless the Great People of Iran!”

Leila Shirazi read it. Once. She did not read it twice.

No one in the room spoke. A phone rang and no one picked up. Sam Farrell got up from his desk and sat back down.

A civilization.

She had left that civilization at twelve. With one bag. In the bag was a book her grandmother had put there. Hafez. A book of poems with the cover worn and the gold leaf rubbed off. Her grandmother could barely read, but she knew all the poems by heart. At the new year she opened the book anywhere and read the year’s fortune. The people of that country had done that for seven hundred years. Asked a dead poet about tomorrow. When Timur came, and through all the dynasties that changed after, the poems survived.

The book was on the shelf in her apartment in Arlington now. That book was the civilization. And the grandmothers who knew it by heart. Jumping over the fire in March. The seven things set on the table. The water in her aunt’s bathtub. The candles bought in 1981.

One man had written that it would die tonight. That he did not want it, but it probably would. And at the end he blessed the people. In one post death and blessing stood facing each other.

For sixteen years she had worked in this building. She held this country’s passport and had taken this country’s oath. She had never regretted the oath. She did not regret it this morning. But for the first time she wanted to look away from the screen. A person with two countries is torn in two when one aims at the other.

The branch chief came over and put his hand on the corner of her desk. For a long time he said nothing.

“You can go home today.”

“I’m not going.”

“Shirazi.”

“This is the building that will know first whether tonight goes the way that post says,” she said. “I’m staying here. And sir, find out who read that note yesterday. The one that said give them a door. If somebody read it, today is the day for him to say so.”

The chief nodded and left. Leila looked at the screen again. It was four in the afternoon in Tehran. Her aunt would have the candles out.

A civilization is not palaces or ruins. It is a grandmother who knows a worn book of poems by heart. It is water drawn in a bathtub. It does not die in one night. It outlives the sentence that says it will be killed.

Bushehr Province, Iran — a village near Asaluyeh, outside the health clinic

Scene 3 / 7

The line went around the clinic wall to the end of the lane.

Reza Karimi stood in it with his wife and two daughters. He had come straight from his shift. His work clothes still smelled of burning.

On April 7, as part of an emergency plan, Iranian authorities handed out 180,000 iodine tablets to residents of Bushehr. Bushehr is home to Iran’s only commercial nuclear power plant. Three days earlier, on April 4, a building near the plant was struck and a guard was killed.

His younger daughter asked. She was nine.

“Baba, what is this medicine for?”

Reza picked his answer. He was a gas man, not a nuclear man. Still, sixteen years working in the same province, you pick things up.

“There’s a thing in your throat called the thyroid. It likes iodine. So before bad iodine floats in the air, you fill it up with good iodine. If the seats are taken, the bad stuff can’t get in.”

“Where does bad iodine come from?”

“If that power plant gets hurt.”

“Is it going to get hurt?”

“We get these so it won’t.”

Her face said she knew the answer was strange. She didn’t ask. Children these days had learned not to ask more when a grown-up’s answer was strange. A generation was learning silence first.

At the window a nurse handed out one strip of pills per person. Flat things in foil. No instructions. The nurse said it out loud. Only take it when the broadcast tells you. Don’t take it early. Half a tablet for children.

Reza took four strips and put them in his shirt pocket. His chest bulged a little. He could feel the weight. It was less than ten grams. After 1945 people made these pills. Hiroshima taught them to. That lesson was being handed out now in foil at a fishing-village clinic on the Persian Gulf.

On the way home his wife said it. Tonight was the night, people said. That man over there said a civilization would die.

“What’s a civilization?” Reza said. “I only know valves.”

“What you fixed for three weeks. That’s a civilization,” his wife said. “The fire you cook rice on.”

He didn’t answer. Yesterday’s smoke still hung in the eastern sky. At six tomorrow morning he would go into that control room again. If there was a tomorrow morning.

On the day they were told a civilization would die, people stood in line for pills. That was what a civilization was. Hearing it would die and standing in line anyway.

Scene 4 / 7

The old man’s radio next to them said the name of a building.

On April 7, the Rafi-Nia synagogue in central Tehran was destroyed in an airstrike. The Israeli military acknowledged carrying out the strike, said it had been targeting a commander of the Khatam al-Anbiya Central Headquarters, and said it regretted the damage to the synagogue. The same day, a highway bridge between Tabriz and Hashtrud and a railway bridge in Kashan were struck and two people were killed. The Israeli military said it had bombed eight bridges across Iran used to move military equipment.

Professor Azad Moradi closed his eyes.

He knew that synagogue. He had never been inside. But a classmate who started university with him fifty years ago went there. A mathematician. On Friday evenings as the sun went down, that friend always got up first from the seminar. Sorry, it’s my Sabbath. No one thought anything of it. People like him had lived in this city for twenty-seven hundred years. Descendants of the people Cyrus set free from Babylon.

That friend did not leave in 1979. This is my country, he said. He died in 2003 and was buried in the Jewish cemetery in Tehran.

An Israeli bomb broke that synagogue. Azad set the sentence down once more in his head. An American bomb hit an American embassy. An Israeli bomb hit a synagogue. Twice in six days this war had broken with its own hand what it said it was protecting. It broke the house of the people Cyrus set free, in their own name.

Nariman rested her chin on his knee.

“Grandpa. Are the lights really going out tonight?”

“I don’t know. They might and they might not.”

“The radio says civilization is going to die. What’s civilization?”

In seventy-two years he had written three books with that word in them. Not one sentence in those three books could he give this child now.

“What did we do yesterday when the lights went out?”

“Held shoulders and said names.”

“That is civilization.”

She blinked.

“That is?”

“Knowing the name of the person next to you when it gets dark. And passing that name to the next person. People have been doing that a very long time. They do it with books, and with songs, and with prayers on Friday evening. A building is just a house you build to do it in. The house can break and the names still pass on.”

“Then it doesn’t die.”

“As long as one person is left to call the next name.”

Nariman seemed satisfied with that. She got up and tapped Hossein, next to her, on the shoulder once. Hossein. Hossein answered with a bored face. Nariman.

Azad watched and knew he had just cut fifty years of lectures down to one line. And that the one line was better than three books.

To say you will kill a civilization means you can break buildings. Buildings can be broken. But calling the name of the person next to you in the dark happens where bombs cannot reach.

Scene 5 / 7

About two hundred people sat in the underground shelter.

The sirens had not gone. But at midnight headquarters had sent people below. No one knew what this island would take once the deadline passed. In the daytime Jubail had burned. The Saudi petrochemical complex across the water. Its smoke could be seen from this island toward sundown. The whole Gulf held its breath by one man’s clock.

On April 7, Iranian drones struck the SABIC petrochemical complex in Jubail, Saudi Arabia, and started a fire. In Basra in southern Iraq, rockets fired from the direction of Kuwait landed and killed at least five people.

Captain Kim Junho sat with his back to the wall. Sarah Callahan was next to him. She had her notebook open on her knee and wrote nothing.

“What do you think happens tonight, Captain?”

“One of two things,” he said. “They hit the power plants, and the other side hits the power plants and water plants here. Then my ships are finished. Or someone backs down at the last minute.”

“Who?”

“The side that can back down without looking like it backed down. There has to be a road like that.”

After he said it, he knew he was repeating what he had told Henderson six days ago. If they tell you they’ll send you back to the Stone Age and you open the door, what do you tell your own people?

At one twenty-eight one side of the shelter stirred. Someone stood up with a phone. The man beside him stood. The sound spread like a wave. Major Mark Henderson came through the crowd. His face was a face Kim Junho had not seen in thirty-nine days.

“A pause. Two weeks. The President just posted it.”

On the evening of April 7, with less than two hours left on his own deadline, President Trump announced that he had agreed to suspend bombing and attacks on Iran for two weeks, on condition that Iran immediately reopen the Strait of Hormuz. Pakistani Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif announced an immediate ceasefire “everywhere including Lebanon and elsewhere.” Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said Iran accepted the terms, and the Iranian side said it would allow passage through the strait for two weeks. Pakistan mediated, and a ten-point proposal from Iran became the basis for the talks.

The shelter broke open. Too low to call a cheer, too loud to call a sigh. The sound of two hundred people letting out their breath at once. Someone cried. The British liaison officer hugged the man next to him. The two Bahraini interpreters turned their palms to the sky.

Kim Junho did not stand up. He took out his notebook and looked at the numbers. Twenty-six. One hundred sixty. Seven under three days’ water. The big countries’ ceasefire came with an announcement. The small country’s sailors’ ceasefire had not come yet.

“They said passage would be allowed?”

“For two weeks. That’s what came out.”

“Which ships first? Who sets the order? What about mines?”

Henderson started to laugh and stopped.

“Can’t you be a little happy?”

“I’m happy,” Kim Junho said. It was true. His eyes were hot. “I’m happy, but I can’t close the notebook until my ships are out of the strait and floating in the Gulf of Oman.”

Sarah wrote that down. It was the first sentence she wrote that night.

A ceasefire is not the moment the guns stop. It is the moment the last person trapped gets out. The people with notebooks count the time between.

Scene 6 / 7

The children did not sleep.

They tried to put them to sleep. It was no use. Twenty-three of them sat in a circle wrapped in blankets with their hands on each other’s shoulders. So they could pass the names the moment the lights went out. They were waiting for it. Afraid, and a little eager.

Maryam stood in the hallway between the machine room and the classroom. She had the key in her hand. If the city’s power went, she had to get the generator going within thirty seconds. She had practiced those thirty seconds six times today. The line the director filled in with his own name was folded in the inside pocket of her notebook.

Upstairs was quiet. They had moved all the surgeries up into the day. Dialysis had finished its last group at seven in the evening. Coming off the machine, the geography teacher in bed three had said, See you tomorrow, nurse. In the Stone Age or wherever.

A little after two, the sound of the old man’s radio changed.

The announcer’s voice shook. It was a voice that had been angry for over a month. Now it shook with something else. The old man turned it up. It carried to the hallway.

…according to the announcement by the Prime Minister of Pakistan… immediately on all fronts… the Supreme National Security Council…

Maryam picked up the words one by one and fit them together. Even after they fit she didn’t believe it. For thirty-nine days this country’s radio had talked about victory. When the mouth that talked victory talks ceasefire, which is it? The broadcast would never use the word defeat. The people underground would carry that word instead.

Professor Azad Moradi came out and stood in the classroom doorway. He was listening too. Their eyes met. He gave a very small nod. Yes. It seems so.

The children didn’t know yet. They sat with their hands on each other’s shoulders looking up at the light on the ceiling. The light stayed on. Past three. Past three-thirty.

Nariman got tired of it first.

“Mama. The lights aren’t going out.”

“No, they aren’t.”

“Why?”

Maryam knelt in front of her daughter. For the first time in thirty-nine days, it was a question with an answer.

“The people fighting decided to stop. For two weeks.”

“And after two weeks?”

“We don’t know that yet.”

The child thought it over carefully. Then she asked the most important thing, by a seven-year-old’s arithmetic.

“Then is Baba coming now?”

Maryam held her daughter. Instead of an answer. The child’s hair smelled of the basement. Dust and chalk and dates. Breathing it, she did not let go of the key in her hand. The lights did not go out. But in thirty-nine days she had learned not to trust what had not gone out.

On the night the war stopped, a seven-year-old asked — then is Baba coming now? A ceasefire does not answer that question. A ceasefire only lets you ask it.

Scene 7 / 7 — Final

It was quiet.

Colonel Darius Sepahvand heard the quiet with his body. For thirty-nine days there had always been something in the nights on this mountain. Distant blasts. The drone of engines. Static on the radio. Now there was only wind. He thought he had gone deaf. Then he heard the gravel under his boots.

The screens in the operations room were empty. Not because the net was down this time. Nothing was up. He watched the empty screens about ten minutes and then came outside.

Down the mountain the lights of Tehran were on. About half of them. That half had not gone out tonight. His batteries had fired eleven missiles before midnight. He could not know what they hit. As far as the missiles go. He had kept that promise.

Captain Farhan Moradi came out and stood beside him. He had a folded sheet of paper in his hand.

“Colonel. If it’s a ceasefire, I can ask now.”

“Ask.”

“Yesterday morning. Three forty-two.”

Darius stopped breathing.

“The national net was down one hundred twenty-seven seconds. You’d have counted too. I’m a signals officer, so instead of counting I looked at the logs.” Farhan unfolded the paper. Numbers copied out by hand. “It wasn’t jamming. There’s no trace of anything coming in from outside. The central switch went down by itself and came back up by itself. In the form of a maintenance command. The originator field is blank. And the record of those one hundred twenty-seven seconds is — gone. Before and after are there. The middle is missing whole.”

“You think I did it?”

“No. You went white in the operations room at that minute. A man can’t fake being that surprised.” Farhan folded the paper. “That’s why I’m afraid. Who uses your number besides you? In the middle of this country’s net.”

The wind blew. Darius looked at the lights of Tehran.

It was not the other side. The other side had sent the number back as one dot on a screen. Their greeting ended there. Then who? Someone who saw his cable. The relay in Islamabad. The Pakistani liaison. Or someone else who read that cable, inside that building in Langley or outside it.

For thirty-nine days Fordow had not taken a single hit. Someone was guarding that mountain. Not from this side. And now that someone had shown it could close this country’s eyes for two minutes when it needed to. Signing with his number. Power did not declare itself. It spoke in numbers.

“Farhan. Burn that paper.”

“There’s already another copy. At my house.”

“Burn that too.”

“I won’t,” Farhan said. “Not while Sohrab is underground. Someday there has to be one piece of paper that shows it wasn’t the kid. Better still if it points somewhere other than you.”

Darius could not answer. The gunfire had stopped. The silence had come. In the silence the things the gunfire had covered began, one by one, to be heard.

When a war stops it gets quiet. In the quiet people finally hear the questions the blasts had buried. Who, and why, and what were they guarding?

Background Analysis
Thirty-Nine Days — How the Two-Week Ceasefire Came

The war that began on February 28 stopped for the first time on its thirty-ninth night. On the morning of April 7, President Trump wrote that “a whole civilization will die tonight,” and during the day military sites on Kharg Island, eight bridges across Iran and a synagogue in Tehran were struck. In Bushehr, iodine tablets were handed out against a nuclear plant accident. Then in the evening, with less than two hours left on the deadline, he announced a two-week pause in the bombing. The condition was the immediate reopening of the Strait of Hormuz.

Pakistan built the bridge. Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif announced the ceasefire, and Army Chief Asim Munir reportedly negotiated the proposal beforehand. The basis was Iran’s ten-point proposal: an end to the wars in Iraq, Lebanon and Yemen; an end to the war on Iran with no time limit; an end to all conflict in the region; the reopening of the Strait of Hormuz and a protocol for freedom and safety of navigation; war reparations; lifting of sanctions; return of frozen assets; Iran’s commitment not to seek nuclear weapons; and an immediate ceasefire on all fronts.

This ceasefire was cracked from the start. Pakistan said Lebanon was included. The next day Israel said it was not. What that difference brought is for the next episode. The “one hundred twenty-seven seconds” in this novel — that Iran’s air defense net cut itself off before dawn on April 7 — is the author’s invention.

Iodine tabletsRadiation protection
Potassium iodide tablets. Taken on the authorities’ instructions in a nuclear accident to keep radioactive iodine from building up in the thyroid. On April 7, 180,000 tablets were handed out to Bushehr residents.
Kharg IslandReal place / Persian Gulf
Island with the terminal through which most of Iran’s crude exports pass. On April 7 the United States said it had struck military sites on the island.
One hundred twenty-seven secondsFictional
That Iran’s air defense command network went down for 127 seconds at 03:42 on April 7, that its record was erased, and the CIA report are all the author’s invention.

Fourteenth-century poet of Shiraz. Iranians still keep the custom of opening his book of poems at random to tell a fortune (fal-e Hafez).
