Scene 1 / 5

A dot was going into the strait. After eighty-two days.

Lieutenant Kim Junho had been watching that dot since morning. A tanker loaded full of crude in Kuwait. Her name was Universal Winner. The seventh line on his list. The ship was now rounding south of Larak Island. The route Iran had allowed.

On May 20 the tanker Universal Winner, operated by a Korean shipping company, transited the Strait of Hormuz. Carrying Kuwaiti crude, the ship followed the Iran-approved route south of Larak Island. Foreign Minister Cho Hyun told the National Assembly’s Foreign Affairs and Unification Committee that it was passing without a toll after consultation with Iran. It was the first of the 26 Korean ships stranded in the Gulf since the war began to get through. The same day, two Chinese supertankers carrying 4 million barrels of crude left through the strait. Lloyd’s List reported that 54 ships had transited the strait the week before.

No one in the liaison cell raised a voice. To the American officers it was one of hundreds of dots. Kim Junho stood and watched. The dot was slow. The careful speed of a big ship in a narrow channel. A country’s winter heating and factory fires were loaded inside that dot.

Lieutenant Commander Mark Henderson came and stood beside him. He said nothing. The two of them stood that way for nearly an hour. An alliance sometimes was just that, standing side by side.

The dot rounded the south of the island. Passed through the narrowest neck of the strait. Came out into the wide water of the Gulf of Oman.

“She’s out,” Henderson said.

“She’s out.”

Only after saying those two words did Kim Junho realize his throat was tight. Eighty-two days. That ship’s crew had come into the Gulf as usual before the war and been trapped for more than eighty days. He didn’t know their faces. He had never exchanged texts with the captain. That ship moved through the company’s operations room. Still, that ship was his seventh line. In the winter of 1950, when the last ship pulled out of the pier at Hungnam, someone must have stood and watched like this.

He opened his notebook. He drew a line through the seventh line. A different line from the Namu’s. Beside it, for the first time, he wrote:

Transited. 5.20.

“Toll?” Henderson asked.

“They didn’t pay one. Seoul announced it was arranged with Iran.”

“Arranged.” The lieutenant commander rolled the word in his mouth. “Your country got a ship out by arranging it with the country we’re blockading.”

“The ships your country got out, sir, were two flying your country’s flag.”

Henderson looked at him for a moment. Then he laughed. The first laugh without a barb in it Kim Junho had seen in three months. The laugh of a big country’s officer acknowledging a small country’s arithmetic for the first time.

“That’s fair. Can’t get mad at that.” He nodded at the screen. “That route, though. South of Larak. Iran put out a map. Says those are waters it controls. A ship that goes that way is getting Iran’s permission. Our lawyers will hate that. Whose strait it is — that’s exactly what Washington and Tehran are fighting about right now. They fought about it in the eighties too. This sea has been holding that question for forty years.”

“I know.”

“But your ship got out.”

“She’s out.”

That was all. Whose strait it was wasn’t his to decide. His job was twenty-six lines. Now one line was out, one was up on a dry dock in Dubai, and twenty-four remained. Big countries argued principles; a small country got its ships out one at a time.

By midnight more than twenty texts had come. Captains of the ships left behind. Not one said congratulations. They all asked the same thing. Who’s next? Is there an order? Our cargo isn’t crude — can we still go? One ship’s escape made twenty-four ships’ waiting longer.

He answered them one by one. Only what he knew. The order isn’t known yet. The shipping companies and the government are arranging it ship by ship. The first ship getting out means there is a road. I can’t promise the road will be open tomorrow too. Keep your bags packed.

Writing the last sentence, he smiled for a moment. Words he had written on May 1. Twenty days later they meant something different for the first time. Hold on had become get ready to go.

After eighty-two days one line got out. For the first time he wrote “Transited” beside it.

Alborz Mountains, Iran — the mountain road down from the air defense command post
May 20, 2026 (Wed.) · 3:40 p.m. (Tehran local time) — Ceasefire Day 43
Scene 2 / 5

Captain Farhan Moradi went out the main gate at three-thirty. His uncle was waiting at the foot of the mountain.

The pass was for six hours. The car was his own. A fourteen-year-old white Pride. The most common car on this country’s roads. He had read somewhere that they started making it thirty years ago on the model of a small Korean car. Through the years of sanctions this country had driven its roads on another country’s old designs. On the passenger seat lay a secondhand book. Saadi’s Gulistan. Wrapped in brown paper and tied with string.

The sentry raised the barrier. He lifted a hand in greeting.

The mountain road had eleven bends. He had driven it up and down for four years. He could count the bends with his eyes closed. Rock on the right, valley on the left. A mountain where spring came late, so snow still lay in the shade and yellow flowers bloomed in the sun. Even in war the mountain kept its seasons.

He turned on the radio. The news. The other side’s president had said the talks were in the final stages. And also that he had come within an hour of starting the war again. The announcer read both sentences in the same voice. An age when peace and war were broadcast at the same volume.

He turned off the radio. The sounds of the mountain came in.

He thought of his uncle. Last time his uncle had put out dates. Because there were no sugar cubes. Today he meant to stop at the market and buy a bag of sugar cubes. And give him the book, and drink tea, and finish the conversation they hadn’t finished last time. About deciding not to know. He had thought more about it since.

Deciding not to know is your own, his uncle had said. He had passed those words on to the colonel. And six days ago he had done the opposite. He had decided to know. Spending three nights in front of the exchange’s system diagram, he had seen what the lieutenant colonel hadn’t. That the door opened both ways. He had told no one. Not saying was different from deciding not to know. It was knowing and keeping your mouth shut. How would his uncle tell those two apart? He wanted to ask him.

First bend. Second bend.

The envelope was in the lawyer’s drawer. A month. He meant to get it back within a month. If the talks really were in the final stages. If the war really ended. Then that paper would be useless, and he would put it in his uncle’s fireplace, and the two of them would watch it burn. When peace came, the truth would become paper you could burn.

He stepped on the brake. Before the third bend.

His foot went in deep. Too deep.

It took him half a second to understand. The pedal hit the floor. Nothing caught. The car didn’t slow. It was downhill, the bend turned left, and on the left was the valley.

A signals officer is someone who deals with failures. His hands moved first. He shifted down. The engine screamed. He pulled the hand brake. The rear wheels locked and the tail of the car swung. Right. Toward the rock. He wrenched the wheel toward the rock. Hit it to stop. That was the only thing.

For that second or so his head was strangely clear. Like the three nights with the system diagram.

The car was serviced the day before yesterday. At the command post motor pool. Oil change. They said they checked the brakes too.

The technical committee. Four. Believing faces.

Is there a copy, Captain? — No.

And last, he saw the brown package sliding off the passenger seat. He reached for it. Taking one hand off the wheel. A movement not in the calculation. At the last moment, people grab something that isn’t a calculation.

The book reached his hand. Seven-hundred-year-old sentences were in his hand.

Not saying was different from deciding not to know. It was knowing and keeping your mouth shut.

Alborz Mountains, Iran — Air Defense Force command post
May 20, 2026 (Wed.) · Night (Tehran local time) — Ceasefire Day 43
Scene 3 / 5

The sentry saw the smoke at 3:47. A thin line rose from the valley.

Colonel Darius Sepahvand didn’t remember the next seven hours well afterward. They stayed only in pieces. The rescue team going down on ropes. The white car on the valley floor. Roof down. One phrase from the radio. The medical officer shaking his head.

Thirty-seven. A child born the year after the Iran–Iraq War ended had died during the ceasefire of another war.

At ten at night the counterintelligence lieutenant colonel came. An hour and a half’s drive from Tehran. He looked a long time at the car hauled up from the valley. With a flashlight he put his head inside a wheel well. Darius stood beside him. No one spoke.

The lieutenant colonel stood up. Brushed the dirt off his knees.

“The brake line on the left front wheel is burst.”

“Burst, or cut?”

“I’d like to know that too.” The lieutenant colonel’s voice was flat. Too flat. “It’s a fourteen-year-old car. Rubber lines rot. A rotted line bursts under load on a downhill. In this country there are hundreds of accidents like that a year. And the burst edge of the line is clean. A line that bursts from rot frays. This one is only half frayed.”

“Half.”

“Half rotted, and half I don’t know. It could have been torn as the car rolled. I’m not a mechanic. Tomorrow I’ll call in the forensics team. Only, Colonel.” For the first time he looked straight at Darius. “This man worked with me for ten days and more. We stayed up three nights together. I liked him. And on Friday four men from Tehran took the records he wrote by hand. Without telling me. I found out yesterday.”

“What are you saying?”

“I’m not saying anything. Not until the forensics come back. But even if they say rotted line, I’m going to find out who those four men on Friday were. It isn’t my job. This time.” For the first time, the hunter began to look into his own side’s woods.

The lieutenant colonel left. Darius stood in that spot a long time. Then he went to the signals room.

Second Lieutenant Sohrab Jahangiri was at the console. His eyes were red. He started to stand, and the colonel raised a hand to keep him seated.

The third drawer.

If I’m late on Wednesday, I’ve written next week’s checklists in advance; they’re in the third drawer in the signals room.

The night before last those words had snagged. He hadn’t known what snagged. Now he knew. That man had known he might be late. Not knew, perhaps, but had counted it in. And he hadn’t stopped a man who had counted it in.

He opened the drawer. One sheet. The weekly checklist dated next Monday. Farhan’s neat handwriting. Every item filled in with a number in advance. The bottom box. The counter for dormant line Q.

419.

Darius looked at that number a long time.

Four hundred nineteen. On March 30 that counter went from four hundred seventeen to four hundred nineteen. Because the colonel had spoken to the other side twice. Farhan saw it and wrote four hundred seventeen. Every week for seven weeks. That he had misread a blurred digit. That he would make deciding to misread his own.

On the last checklist he had written the right number. Seven weeks of lies ended in one box.

It wasn’t a confession. It wasn’t an accusation. There were two people in the world who would know what that number meant, and one was dead. He just hadn’t wanted to leave a wrong number behind him. For the next person who would trust that paper. Correcting history usually begins in small boxes like this.

If you write that you know something you don’t, the next man who trusts that paper gets hurt. The second lieutenant had said that. The captain had done the opposite for seven weeks. Written that he didn’t know what he knew. To protect the colonel. And before he left, he set it right.

Darius handed the sheet to the second lieutenant.

“Submit this as it is on Monday.”

Sohrab took the sheet and read it. His eyes stopped at the bottom box. He was a signals officer. He knew what number had been on last week’s checklist.

“Colonel. The number’s different from last week.”

“It is.”

“Upstairs will ask. Why it went up by two in a week.”

“If they ask, I’ll answer. That the captain had been misreading it all along and read it right at the end. That’s the truth.”

The second lieutenant held the sheet a long time. Then he nodded. A tear was about to fall on the paper. He held the paper away from his body. So the number wouldn’t smear. He had learned in the room underground to protect the record before the grief.

He hadn’t wanted to leave a wrong number behind him. For the next person who would trust that paper. In the box that said four hundred seventeen for seven weeks, the last checklist said four hundred nineteen.

Scene 4 / 5

Leila Shirazi had been reading it for seven days. Mornings of confirming every day that one man was alive.

The Eastern Sector 3 morning report. The form message that went up every day around ten past six Tehran time. Sam Farrell had cleared a path for the raw collection to come straight up on her screen. Before the machine ate it. When she got in, she opened it before her coffee.

It was usually four lines. No unidentified tracks. No engagement. Equipment status. Confirmed: sector commander. Seeing that last line was the start of her day. He is sitting in his own chair today too. It was thinner than a note and faster than a note. Half a day, not twenty-one. An enemy army’s form had become, for her, a letter saying he was well.

On May 20 President Trump said the negotiations with Iran were in the final stages but that he was ready to attack again if no deal was reached. He said he could wait a few days to get the right answer, and that he had been an hour away from resuming the war. On May 21 the two sides were still at odds over Iran’s stockpile of enriched uranium and control of the Strait of Hormuz. Iran’s supreme leader, Mojtaba Khamenei, was reported to have directed that the enriched uranium must not be sent out of the country. Secretary of State Rubio said there were good signs.

This morning’s report was long. Long meant something had happened.

She read it line by line. In her own language.

Eastern Sector 3. May 20 05:30–May 21 05:30. No unidentified tracks. No engagement.

Personnel change: Signals officer Captain Moradi killed in the line of duty in a vehicle accident on an off-post road, approx. 15:44 on May 20. Circumstances under investigation.

Acting signals officer: Second Lieutenant Jahangiri.

Confirmed: sector commander Colonel Sepahvand.

She read those three lines many times. Killed in the line of duty. The form reduced a death to a few words.

Moradi. She didn’t know the name. Who he was, what he was to the colonel on the mountain. But she knew the title of signals officer. That dawn in March, for the colonel to speak to the other side, he had to go through the signals room. The dormant line. The Quetta relay. What was written on the paper Tariq Mehmood had handed her. The man who managed that line was the signals officer.

He had seen, or seen and pretended not to. Someone who could have known what the colonel did in that command post. That man had died. On an off-post road. In a vehicle accident.

She had been trained not to believe in accidents. And she also knew most accidents were accidents. For sixteen years she had worked between those two. In an authoritarian state an accident was always read two ways.

She looked at the confirmation line again. Sector commander Colonel Sepahvand. The form was the same. The format was the same. But the collection time was different. Other days it was around 02:40 Z. Today, 04:15 Z. An hour and a half late.

A man who hadn’t been late once in seven days was an hour and a half late. That number said more than any obituary.

What had he been doing in that hour and a half? She couldn’t see that. All she could see were numbers. 02:40 and 04:15. Between them was the time it took a man to gather the strength to copy his subordinate’s death onto a form.

’Tis bitter cold, and I am sick at heart.

He had written that more than two weeks ago. Today it would be colder.

And where was her second note now? When it comes, may you be underground. She had written about bombs. About what would come over his head. But what took a man from his side hadn’t come from the sky. It was a mountain road. What she couldn’t stop wasn’t only her own country’s bombs. His country could kill him too. A man sat between the enemy outside and the enemy inside.

She took out her paper notebook. After nine.

Ten. His signals officer is dead. They say it was an accident. Eleven. I have no way of knowing whether it was an accident. He may not know either. Twelve. Since March 30, one fewer person in that command post has seen his number. Does that make him safer, or less safe?

She couldn’t write an answer to twelve. For the first time the analyst left a box blank.

Sam passed, saw her face, and stopped.

“What happened?”

“Someone died there. One of his men.”

“Us?”

“No. Not us.” She pointed at the screen. “A car accident, they say.”

Sam couldn’t read the line. It was Persian. He put a hand on her shoulder for a moment and went. Leila saved the report with a number on it. She wrote a one-line memo for Rowell. Signals officer at source’s command post killed in the line of duty (accident, under investigation). Source in post as normal. Connection to counterintelligence investigation cannot currently be determined. Monitoring.

Monitoring. The most honest word available to someone who could see only four lines of a morning report. What could that honesty do for the man on the mountain?

A man who hadn’t been late once in seven days was an hour and a half late. Between those times was the time it took a man to gather the strength to copy his subordinate’s death onto a form.

Northern Tehran, Evin — Professor Azad’s home
May 21, 2026 (Thu.) · Evening (Tehran local time) — Ceasefire Day 44
Scene 5 / 5 — Final

The tea water had boiled twice yesterday and gone cold twice. The water of waiting.

Professor Azad Moradi knew his nephew was coming on Wednesday. Around four, he’d said. He put the water on at three. Put it on again at four-thirty. At six he stood at the window watching the alley. The pass was six hours, he’d said, so even if he was late he should be here by now.

At seven the phone rang. The army.

He remembered what came after as if he had read it in a book. As if it had happened to someone else. In seventy-two years he had received calls like that many times. In 1988, for a friend. In 2009, for a student. Each time, receiver in hand, he thought one thing. An hour ago this person was alive. The thought was of no use, and he couldn’t stop it. Every generation of this country’s intellectuals got calls like that.

Today at midday he buried his nephew. Behesht-e Zahra. The soldiers’ section. This country buries its dead within a day. The colonel came. People from the command post came. A second lieutenant in his twenties carried one corner of the coffin. The colonel held Azad’s hand a long time and couldn’t say anything. Azad was grateful for that silence. All his life he had found it hard to bear people who talk at times like this. In that cemetery the children of the eight years’ war lay without end. Today one more lay down.

The colonel had given him something. A package wrapped in brown paper. Still tied with string. On one corner of the paper was a dark red stain.

It was in the car. The captain had it in his hand. He was bringing it to you, Professor.

Azad hadn’t untied it yet. If he untied it, the boy would really be gone.

Around sunset there was a knock at the door. An old man. Wearing a vest, leaning on a cane. It took Azad a little while to recognize the face. Thirty-eight years.

“Your Honor.”

“It’s been forty-seven years since I stopped being a judge, Professor.” The old lawyer took off his shoes at the door. “May I come in? This isn’t something to say standing up.”

In the study the lawyer showed him the key from his vest pocket. Then he took an envelope from his inside pocket and laid it on the desk. Sealed. Nothing written on the outside.

“Your nephew left this with me on Sunday. Said if I heard he was dead or arrested, to bring it to you, hand to hand. If I heard nothing, after a month. I hoped he would come back in a month and take it. Instead I’m the one carrying it, after four days.”

Azad looked at the envelope. He didn’t touch it. One sheet of paper looked heavier than a coffin.

There was his nephew’s slip. Behind the drawing in the Shahnameh. The lawyer’s name and address. It would be best if he doesn’t bring it. If he does, open it. One sheet. There are a few numbers and two times on it.

“Do you know what’s inside?”

“I didn’t ask. Only one thing. Whether the person who received it would be hurt.”

“What did he say?”

“He said he could be. That that’s why it was you. Someone who can decide for himself what to do after he reads. That you could burn it, or keep it, or show it to someone. That he couldn’t make that decision for you, but he wanted the decision to be in your hands.”

Azad closed his eyes. He could hear the boy’s voice. In this room, three weeks ago. What you don’t know now protects you. And the boy had believed there would be at least a month in which his uncle could be protected by not knowing. It was four days.

“I told that boy: the not-knowing may not be yours, but deciding not to know is. That turning back at the door is a decision too.” His voice cracked. “Clever words. The kind a teacher says. He heard them and decided not to turn back. And he died.”

“Professor,” the lawyer said. Both hands on his cane. “For forty-seven years I’ve kept other people’s envelopes. When the one who left it dies, the one who receives it always says that. If only I hadn’t said that. If only I’d held him back that day. Everyone does. And it has never once been true. Your nephew didn’t do it because of what you said. What you said reached his ears because he was that kind of man.”

Azad was silent a long time.

“Will you open it?”

“I can’t today. Today is the day I buried him. Whatever this is, it might be why he died. If I learn that today, I’ll hate this paper. He wanted me to read it, not to hate it.” Even grief has an order.

“Then when?”

“In three days. After the third-day memorial.” He picked up the envelope. For the first time. It was light. One sheet, he had said. “Until then I want to grieve him without knowing what this is. Only as my nephew.”

The lawyer nodded and stood. At the door he turned.

“When you wrote about my case in the paper in 1988, I couldn’t thank you. I was in prison. And after I got out I couldn’t either. I heard you couldn’t teach for three years, and I was ashamed. After thirty-eight years, I come on an errand like this and get to say it. Thank you.”

After he left, Azad put the envelope in the Shahnameh. Next to his nephew’s slip. Behind the drawing from when he was ten. The two men on horseback with a question mark drawn over each head. Rostam killed Sohrab not knowing he was his son. A thousand-year-old tragedy held one more page of record.

Only then did he untie the string on the brown package.

It was Saadi. The Gulistan. Secondhand. His own copy had taken water in March. The boy had remembered. He opened the book. In the margin of the first page was his nephew’s handwriting. Pencil.

To Uncle. How is deciding not to know different from knowing and keeping your mouth shut? I’ll ask you next time I see you. — F.

There was no next time. Only the question remained. The heaviest thing that can be left to someone who teaches. To inherit a question with no answer. That was the legacy this country’s teachers had received generation after generation.

“Your nephew didn’t do it because of what you said. What you said reached his ears because he was that kind of man.”

Background Analysis
South of Larak Island — Whose Strait Is It?

On May 20 the Korean tanker Universal Winner transited the Strait of Hormuz by the Iran-approved route south of Larak Island. The South Korean government said it had consulted with Iran and paid no toll. Early in the war, after closing the strait, Iran was reported to have allowed some ships through for tolls of up to $2 million per ship, and to have opened the way for ships of a few countries, including China, Russia, India and Pakistan.

The same day, Iran published a map of the waters it “controls.” By May 21 the issues between the two sides had narrowed to two. One: whether Iran’s stockpile of enriched uranium would be sent out of the country. Two: whether the strait was a waterway open to all without tolls, or one Iran gave permission to use. President Trump said the strait must be open without tolls; Iran asserted sovereignty over the strait and said it could reopen it to friendly countries.

In between, ships got out one at a time. According to Lloyd’s List, 54 ships transited the strait the week before, and 31 in the 24 hours to May 21. Far short of the daily traffic before the war, but the strait was becoming neither closed nor open. The only things in this episode that actually happened are the Universal Winner’s transit and the reports on the negotiations. Farhan Moradi’s death and the scenes that follow are all the author’s invention.

Author’s Note
Universal WinnerReal / May 20
A tanker operated by a Korean shipping company. Carrying Kuwaiti crude, it transited the Strait of Hormuz by the Iran-approved route south of Larak Island. The South Korean government said it had consulted with Iran and paid no toll. Its number on Kim Junho’s list is fictional, and this episode does not portray the ship’s captain or crew.
Larak IslandGeography
An island inside the Strait of Hormuz, near the Iranian mainland and Qeshm Island.
The May 20–21 negotiationsReal
The reports of President Trump’s “final stages” remark, the standoff over enriched uranium and control of the strait, and Khamenei’s directive are real.
Farhan Moradi’s deathFictional
The character, the accident, the brake line, the “technical committee” and the checklist number are all the author’s invention. Whether it was an accident is not revealed even within the story.
PrideFact
A small car Iran’s SAIPA produced from the 1990s on, based on Kia’s Pride from Korea. For a long time it was the most common car in Iran.
Behesht-e ZahraReal place
A large cemetery south of Tehran. Islamic funerals are usually held within a day of death, with memorials on the third day, seventh day and fortieth day.
