Tehran — Imam Khomeini Hospital, rooftop
April 8, 2026 (Wed.) · 7:00 a.m. (Tehran local time) — Ceasefire Day 1 · War Day 40
Scene 1 / 6

Seven in the morning. The director gave permission. They took the children up to the roof.

Twenty-three of them climbed the stairs. Children who had lived underground more than thirty days. The eleven-year-old boy in front pushed the steel door. Light poured in. The children covered their eyes all at once. Like a generation seeing light for the first time.

Professor Azad Moradi came up last, holding the rail. His knees counted the steps. When he stood on the roof the wind hit his face. Tehran wind in April. The Alborz was to the north. There was still snow at the top. He had not seen the mountain in forty days.

The city was quiet. In the quiet city the sounds came back one at a time. A motorbike. A shutter going up. A radio somewhere. People were out in the streets. They did not walk fast. Every few steps they all lifted their heads. A city of ten million looking at the sky at once. In the summer of 1988 this city had looked at the sky like that too.

On April 8, Middle East time, the ceasefire took effect. A pro-government rally was held in Tehran to celebrate it. Iran International reported mixed reactions among Iranian dissidents. Some spoke of anger and despair, and some urged patience and spoke of hope.

Nariman tugged at his hand.

“Grandpa, why is everybody looking up?”

“It’s a habit they’ve got. For forty days things came from up there.”

“They don’t anymore.”

“The head knows that. The neck doesn’t know yet.”

When she heard that, she made a point of looking down at her feet. Then she couldn’t help it and looked up at the sky. There was nothing in the sky. Two clouds. Pigeons.

“Grandpa. Is it really over?”

Azad did not answer that easily. Last night the radio had said two weeks. It had said victory too. With the same mouth. In seventy-two years he had learned what this country’s radio hid when it talked about victory. And how far the words two weeks were from the word over.

“It isn’t over. It’s stopped.”

“What’s the difference?”

“What’s over doesn’t start again. What’s stopped moves if someone pushes it again. Right now everyone has agreed not to push for two weeks.”

“And after two weeks?”

“In between, the grown-ups have to sit at a table and find a way to end it.”

“What if they don’t find it?”

Instead of answering, he put his hand on her shoulder. The way they did in the dark. She understood and said, Grandpa. He answered, Nariman.

Across by the rail stood Maryam. She had the face of someone up all night. She still had the key in her hand. She was not looking at the city. She was looking at the hospital gate. At the people coming in, one by one. In uniform or not. The wives of this country watch the gate even on the day a war ends.

On the first morning the war stopped, people looked at the sky as they walked. The head knows it is over. The neck does not know yet.

South Lebanon — an empty house in a village back from the border
April 8, 2026 (Wed.) · Afternoon (Lebanon local time) — Ceasefire Day 1
Scene 2 / 6

Mohammad Qasir heard the news at dawn and took off his shoes.

It had been three weeks. Since he heard tank treads on March 17, he had slept with his shoes on. In those three weeks the feet of a twenty-six-year-old had become someone else’s feet. Sitting on the kitchen floor of the empty house, he spread his toes one at a time. In 1982 and in 2006 the men of this land had slept in their shoes. His father had. His grandfather had.

The words of the Pakistani prime minister came over the radio. Everywhere. Including Lebanon. Of the four with him, one laughed. One didn’t believe it. Mohammad believed it because he wanted to.

In the afternoon the sky tore open.

On April 8, Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu said Lebanon was not included in the ceasefire. That day the Israeli Air Force, under the name “Operation Eternal Darkness,” carried out a hundred strikes in ten minutes on more than a hundred targets across Lebanon. It was the largest attack on Lebanon since the war began. At least 254 people were killed and 837 injured. Hezbollah fired rockets into northern Israel in response to what it called “violations of the ceasefire.”

Ten minutes. He heard later that it was ten minutes. Inside it there was no time. Sound came from the north, from the west, from every village whose name he knew. Not one at a time. All at once. The ground boomed like a drum. All the glass in the empty house went out. He lay barefoot on the kitchen floor.

When the sound stopped he got up and looked for his shoes. There was glass in them. He shook it out and put them on. His hands would not do what he told them when he tied the laces.

The radio was still on. A different voice was coming out of it. What the prime minister over there had said. And a little later, what the president across the sea had said. That the Pakistani had it wrong. That this country was not on that paper.

Not on it. He turned the words over. For twelve hours he had thought it was on it. His mother. His sister in Tyre. His cousins in Beirut. The whole country had taken off its shoes for half a day. Then in ten minutes it learned that the people who wrote the paper did not even agree on whether they had written this country’s name. The fate of small countries had always been written in the margins of big countries’ paper.

U.S. Vice President JD Vance said there had been a “legitimate misunderstanding.” He said the Iranians seemed to have thought the ceasefire included Lebanon, that it did not, and that the United States had never made that promise. He explained that Israel had promised to hold back somewhat in Lebanon to help the talks, but that this was not part of the ceasefire. Iran’s Tasnim news agency reported that Iran would withdraw from the agreement if Israeli attacks on Lebanon continued.

Misunderstanding. Mohammad knew the word in English and in Arabic. The word you use when you take the wrong road. The word you use when you heard the meeting time differently. He hadn’t known it was the word for two hundred fifty-four dead.

The most dangerous word in a ceasefire document is “everywhere.” Every man who writes it puts a different list of countries inside it.

April 8, 2026 (Wed.) · 9:00 p.m. (Bahrain local time) — Ceasefire Day 1
Scene 3 / 6

In the morning a few dots moved.

Captain Kim Junho saw it on the ship-tracking screen. Of the dots that had been stuck inside the strait for forty days, a few began to crawl east. Not his ships. Other countries’ ships. Still, he held his breath and watched. He had not known a dot moving could be such a beautiful thing.

He opened his notebook. Next to the names of the twenty-six ships he drew a box each. A box to check when they got out.

At noon there was a sound from the south of the island.

Even after the ceasefire took effect on April 8, attacks went on in the Gulf. Early in the morning unexplained explosions were reported on Iran’s Sirri and Lavan islands, and the National Iranian Oil Refining and Distribution Company said around 10 a.m. that the refinery on Lavan Island had been hit in a “cowardly enemy attack.” There were no casualties. Neither the United States nor Israel said it had carried out the attack. Later, Iranian missiles and drones were intercepted over the UAE, and Iranian drones hit energy and desalination facilities in Kuwait, causing heavy damage. In Sitra, Bahrain, two people were injured by debris. Iranian state TV said these attacks came hours after the oil facility on Lavan Island was targeted.

Sitra. Twenty minutes by car from the liaison cell. Major Mark Henderson put down the phone and said:

“First day of the ceasefire and somebody got hurt on this island.”

“Who hit Lavan?”

“Not us. That much I know. Israel says not them either.” Henderson shrugged. There was no joke in the shrug. “On the first morning of a ceasefire a refinery doesn’t blow up by itself. Somebody fired. Somebody who didn’t raise his hand.”

After six in the evening the dots on the screen stopped. The ones out front, and the ones that had just started moving behind them.

A semi-official news agency affiliated with the IRGC reported that Iran had halted passage through the Strait of Hormuz in response to Israel’s attacks on Lebanon. President Masoud Pezeshkian said the ceasefire with the United States was broken because Lavan and Sirri islands had been bombed. Vice President Vance said traffic through the strait had already increased, but warned that if Iran did not keep its promise to reopen the strait, the President would not keep the American side of the terms either.

Kim Junho looked down at the twenty-six empty boxes in his notebook. He had not checked one. Seventeen hours. That was how long the door had been open. The ships that got out in that time you could count on your fingers. His ships were near the back of the line. The line always formed in order of power.

In the evening Sarah Callahan asked him.

“Captain. Remember what you said last night? That you couldn’t close the notebook?”

“I remember.”

“I put that in the first sentence of my story. The desk told me to take it out. Said it didn’t fit a ceasefire story.” She gave a sour smile. “Tonight they want it back in.”

A document that stops a war needs signatures. To start a war again you need no signature. Someone fires one round without raising his hand.

Alborz Mountains, Iran — Air Defense Force command post, the colonel’s office
April 8, 2026 (Wed.) · 9:00 p.m. (Tehran local time) — Ceasefire Day 1
Scene 4 / 6

There were two sheets of paper on Colonel Darius Sepahvand’s desk.

One was a report up from the southern air defense sector. 04.08 approx. 10:00, Lavan Island refinery hit. Failed to detect approach track. Low-altitude penetration presumed. Launch point unknown. The other was the numbers Farhan Moradi had copied by hand. 03:42:00. 03:44:07. The blank between.

He set the two side by side. One happened before the ceasefire. One after. One inside the net. One out over the sea. They had one thing in common. In both, there was no one.

In a war, the side that breaks something usually announces it. Announcing is half the reason for breaking it. For thirty-nine days Washington and Tel Aviv had announced every day what they hit. To hit and say nothing was a different kind of thing. The work of someone who would be in trouble if he said it. Power fought with announcements and worked in silence.

Farhan came in without knocking. He didn’t knock these days.

“The President says the ceasefire is broken. Because of Lavan.”

“I heard.”

“Is it broken?”

“I don’t know. Nothing shows on my screen. Not one of their planes has come across. A broken ceasefire can’t be this quiet.” Darius pressed his finger on the Lavan report. “So who did this? If not them.”

“Across the Gulf,” Farhan said. He didn’t hesitate. “For forty days we hit those countries’ refineries and water plants and smelters. Those countries never once fired back. America fired for them. Yesterday America stopped. So whoever still has something to pay back has to pay it with his own hand. A hand that didn’t sign the ceasefire.”

Darius looked at the young officer. Thirty-seven. In three weeks every extra word had gone out of the man’s talk. The war had made the young officer old and had stood the old colonel back in front of the questions of his youth.

“If you’re right, that paper has a hole in it. Only the countries that signed are tied. Every country that didn’t sign is loose.”

“Lebanon fell through that hole too.”

At that, neither of them said anything for a while.

Darius opened the drawer. The copy of the statement lay next to his wife’s old phone. He put today’s two sheets under it. The drawer was getting heavier a little at a time. A drawer of things with no one behind them.

“Any word on Sohrab?”

“None. I don’t know whether he went to Evin or is in the military lockup. Since the intelligence chief died there’s no one to ask.” Farhan turned toward the door and stopped. “Tomorrow is the fortieth day, Colonel. The Leader’s.”

On April 9, a fortieth-day memorial was held for Ali Khamenei, killed in the strikes of February 28, with mourners from inside and outside the country. The same day, former Foreign Minister Kamal Kharazi, injured in the April 1 strike, died.

Forty days. The time it took in this country to send off the dead. The war had taken exactly that long too. Darius did not believe the coincidence. He no longer believed in the coincidence of any number. In 1978 this country had held funerals every forty days too, and with every funeral the streets burned. That ring of forty days ended the monarchy.

When the man who fired raises his hand, it is war. When no one raises his hand, it is something else. Something with no name yet.

Langley, Virginia — CIA Iran Operations Center
April 8, 2026 (Wed.) · 5:00 p.m. EDT — Ceasefire Day 1
Scene 5 / 6

All day Leila Shirazi chased one number.

One hundred twenty-seven. Last night a single line from the SIGINT side had kept her awake. Iranian air defense command network, out for one hundred twenty-seven seconds from 03:42. She asked the electronic warfare shop. Did we do it? The answer came in two hours. No. Nothing touched that net at that time.

Then where did this number come from?

She opened the ZARRIN identity report. Dated March 30. The highest control level. A document not one line of which could go anywhere without the originator’s permission. She pulled the access log. Who had opened this document.

There should have been five lines. Her. The branch chief. Two people upstairs. The station chief in Islamabad.

There were six lines.

The sixth was not a person’s name. It was an account name. PC-LNO-03. Accessed March 31 at 02:11. Half a day before she wrote the request for the drone transponder. The terminal that opened it was not in this building. Fort Meade.

PC. She remembered right away where she had heard those two letters. Lunch on March 19. Something a liaison officer on loan from the National Security Agency let slip over a sandwich. When she asked without thinking, he laughed and said: Persian Code? That’s a canceled project.

An account from a canceled project had opened the most deeply buried document in this building at two in the morning.

She went to the branch chief’s office. She put the access log on his desk. He looked at it a long time. He did not rub the corner of the desk. That made her more uneasy. It was not an uncomfortable face. It was a surprised one.

“I never gave this account access.”

“Then who did?”

“There are only two positions in this building that can add access to a controlled document without the originator knowing. Both of them are on seven.” He turned the paper face down. “Shirazi. This stops here. I’ll go up and ask.”

“And if the answer is ‘noted’?”

The chief didn’t answer.

She went back to her desk. All afternoon the Vice President’s words went around on the screens. Legitimate misunderstanding. Hearing it, she thought of the assessment she had written. Words that got heavier as they were carried. This time it was a country that fell out as it was carried. Whether someone left it out on purpose, or it fell out by mistake, or each side heard it differently. She was no longer sure she could tell those three apart. She couldn’t even tell them apart inside this building.

At five the branch chief came down. He said nothing about the access log. He held out a different sheet.

“Travel orders. Islamabad. Flight tomorrow night. There’s a meeting this weekend. The Vice President is going. You attach to the station and read what the Iranian delegation says.”

“And the access log?”

“Sending you there is the answer.” The chief lowered his voice. “Read it the good way, they’re sending you where you’re needed most. Read it the bad way, they’re separating you from this building’s records. I don’t know which. I know one thing. Islamabad is where this end of the Quetta line is.”

Leila took the orders. Quetta. The closed line. The other side had closed it. But a line has two ends. The agency was sending her far away. She knew the far place might be the nearest one.

The surest way to guard a secret document is to count the people who have read it. Where there should be five and there are six, the sixth is a bigger secret than the document.

Scene 6 / 6 — Final

Kang Yujin drank cold coffee and scrolled the logs.

It was a habit. Every morning she skimmed the output of the last twenty-four hours. Her model had been built to estimate when a war would stop. For three and a half years it had been a toy inside papers. Not since forty days ago. When a real war broke out she fed it public data every day. Announced strikes. Announced casualties. The number of people on each side who could speak for it. The model ate that and spat out a number. Ceasefire probability.

The night before last that number had been ninety-one. Then the ceasefire came. She did not brag about it to anyone. Getting it right bothered her. She did not fully know what the model had seen to get it right.

Scrolling down, her hand stopped.

April 7, 09:12:00. Korea time. It was not output she had asked for. It was a line the model had written on its own. The variable name was one she had never put in. The name that had been turning up now and then for three weeks.

interval = 127

Under it, one line. 09:14:07 — interval closed.

She converted Korea time to Tehran time. Took off five and a half hours. Three forty-two in the morning. She didn’t know what had happened in Tehran at that hour that day. The news had only that Kharg Island was hit.

What was one hundred twenty-seven? Seconds, minutes, people? The model gave no unit. But subtract the times on the two lines and it was one hundred twenty-seven seconds. The model had written down that something had opened for one hundred twenty-seven seconds and closed. From data she had not put in.

Data she had not put in.

She opened the input records. All that went in through her account was public data. But the model had read forty times as much as she had fed it. The rest had come in through another channel. The channel’s name was four English letters and some numbers. The account that opened that channel was not hers.

It was the lab director’s.

Park Sejun. Seven years her senior. The only person in this lab she showed her code to. On the morning of March 27 he had said: Don’t delete the logs, keep them. Did you tell anybody? And he had nodded a little too long.

Kang Yujin leaned back in her chair. There were no windows on the third basement level. The clock on the wall said eight forty-four. Park Sejun came in at nine. This country was calculating someone else’s war underground. Who had ordered the calculation, the researcher did not know.

She took a storage drive out of the drawer. A thing the size of a fingernail. It took four minutes to download the logs whole. For those four minutes she watched the door. When it finished she pulled it out and pushed it under the foil on the inside of the lid of an instant-noodle cup that had been rolling around her desk. Then she closed the screen and made a fresh coffee.

At two minutes past nine the door opened.

“You’re in early,” Park Sejun said, taking off his coat. His usual voice. “Heard you called the ceasefire. Congratulations.”

“Luck.”

“Not luck. It’s a good model.” Going past, he glanced at her screen. At the closed screen. “Logs piling up all right?”

“Yes. You told me not to delete them.”

He nodded. A little too long again.

A model does not lie. It only spits out what it ate. So if a model spits out a word it does not know, there is only one thing to ask. Who fed it that?

Background Analysis
Four Cracks on the First Day of the Ceasefire

The first crack was Lebanon. Pakistan’s prime minister announced that the ceasefire applied “everywhere including Lebanon.” The next day Israel’s prime minister said Lebanon was not included, and the U.S. president said Israel’s reading was right. The vice president used the words “legitimate misunderstanding.” That day Israel struck Lebanon harder than at any time since the war began, and at least 254 people were killed.

The second crack was the strait. The condition of the ceasefire was the immediate reopening of the Strait of Hormuz. Iran allowed passage, then halted it again in response to the strikes on Lebanon, Iranian media reported. Iran later restricted passage and charged tolls. From the start the two sides meant different things by “reopen.”

The third crack was attacks no one claimed. On the morning the ceasefire took effect there were explosions at the refinery on Iran’s Lavan Island and on Sirri Island. Neither the United States nor Israel said it had done it. Five weeks later, in mid-May, the Wall Street Journal reported that the attack on Lavan Island was carried out by the United Arab Emirates. That day Iran fired missiles and drones at Gulf countries, and its president said the ceasefire was broken.

The fourth crack was time. Two weeks. Within them talks had to be held in Islamabad, and the talks had to produce a framework to end the war. From the start the ceasefire was not an ending but a reprieve. The “sixth reader” in this novel and the model in Seoul are both the author’s invention.

The Vice President flies to Islamabad. Across the table sit Iran’s foreign minister and the speaker of its parliament. Twenty-one hours. Meanwhile, in the strait, ships that sweep mines are moving. In the basement of the station, Leila looks at this end of the line to Quetta. And on the day the talks end, Washington brings out a new word — blockade.
