Episode 1, “The Day the Walls Came Down”

Scene 1. Tehran, Iran — an alley near Azadi Square

February 28, 2026 (Sat.) · 9:45 a.m. (Tehran local time)

Every war began on somebody’s morning.

At four in the morning on June 25, 1950, gunfire cut across the 38th parallel. Somewhere in Seoul someone had just opened his eyes. When the sky burned over Pearl Harbor, someone at the harbor was pouring coffee. On the summer morning the Enola Gay circled over Hiroshima, a child at the edge of the market was laughing. That is how history comes. As an ordinary morning. With nobody knowing.

Saturday, February 28, 2026, 9:45 a.m.

The sky over Tehran was blue. Unreasonably blue. Unfairly blue.

Professor Azad Moradi stood in a side street by Azadi Square. Azadi. It means freedom in Persian. In this country freedom had been only a name for close to forty-seven years. Freedom in name. Liberation on a signboard. The Iranians choked on the irony and learned to live with it anyway. One man adjusted, then a generation, then the whole country. Adjusting is the saddest thing people can do.

The professor was seventy-two. He had seen the Pahlavi monarchy, and he had seen Khomeini’s revolution, and in 2009 he had watched the Green Movement come into flower and watched it go under the boots. He had taught for fifty years in the philosophy department of the University of Tehran. He had put two wives in the ground. Four children had left the country. Each of them in their own way.

Yesterday afternoon the morality police had taken three of his graduate students. There were three charges. They had discussed Habermas in a café. Men and women had sat at the same table. A girl’s hijab had fallen short of whatever standard the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps had set that day. The students who talked about the public sphere had vanished from it.

“Is this the will of God? These executions? These silences?”

Azad reached for his leather journal and stopped. His hand was shaking. It was not the cold. The shaking came from somewhere older. From under the breastbone, where a man stacks the things he cannot say out loud.

He looked at the square. The white arch the Shah had built for himself, the Azadi Tower. The revolution had let it stand. It cost too much to tear down and it made too good a backdrop for photographs. It still stood on the skyline. White. Tall. Stubborn to the point of foolishness. The king went and the clerics came and the tower stood. As if it meant to outlive whatever men settled with weapons and bombers, and watch to the end.

Azad looked up at the sky.

There was something in the sky. He could not see it. His bones knew first. The old, stubborn bones of a philosopher who had come through fifty years of history were telling him: today’s sky is not yesterday’s.

Something was coming. Rescue or ruin. He did not know yet. The two could come with the same face. Then what would this square be called tomorrow?

Scene 2. Arlington, Virginia — The Pentagon, National Military Command Center (NMCC)

February 28, 2026 · 9:45 a.m. Tehran / 1:15 a.m. Washington (EST)

At that same moment it was 1:15 a.m. in Washington, D.C.

Across the Potomac, under the Pentagon. The air in the National Military Command Center smelled of old decisions and cold coffee. The order had already come down. At 3:38 the afternoon before, President Donald Trump had ordered Operation Epic Fury to go. The signatures were done and the chain was closed. Nine hours and thirty-seven minutes had passed since. All that was left for this room was to watch.

What the screens showed was this. The tracks of Tomahawk cruise missiles fired from U.S. Navy destroyers. B-2 Spirit stealth bombers, B-1 Lancers, and B-52s on their way to hardened ballistic missile sites. Some 200 fighter jets climbing out of Israel—the largest sortie in Israeli history, code-named Roaring Lion. And in the eastern Mediterranean, off the Israeli coast, the Gerald R. Ford Carrier Strike Group. Full-load displacement, about 100,000 tons. The largest warship human beings had ever built.

Lieutenant General Faulk, director of operations for the Joint Staff, stood in front of the screens. He had not slept in thirty-one hours and it showed. He was a man who went quiet when the tension peaked. Now he was very quiet.

In January Iranian security forces had fired on antigovernment protesters. In the two months after, the United States massed more forces in the region than at any time since the 2003 invasion of Iraq. Nuclear talks ran all through February. The ships kept arriving while the table was still open. Someone in Tehran must have believed it was a show of force to win at the table.

That belief was wrong.

Faulk thought about the number forty-seven. Forty-seven years since the revolution. Somebody in this room had called it the years eighty million people spent in a prison named a republic. Somebody believed it. Faulk did not deal in belief. He dealt in coordinates.

He looked at the screen. From the satellite Tehran was wide and ordinary. Nine million mornings. Most of them awake. Most of them wanting what people everywhere want. To feed their children. To be left alone. A life you could call dignified. Each coordinate on the screen lay over those mornings.

0115. The first impact signal came up on the screen.

God, let us get this right. Faulk said it without a sound. What was right? That city would give the answer.

Scene 3. Over Iran / Tehran

February 28, 2026 · 9:45 a.m. (Tehran)

9:45 a.m.

The sky over the Middle East tore open.

The missiles came first. Tomahawks fired from Navy destroyers came in low. Army HIMARS hammered the targets in range. B-2 Spirit stealth bombers, B-1 Lancers, and B-52s went for the hardened ballistic-missile sites. That day U.S. Central Command said it had used, for the first time in combat, a cheap one-way attack drone modeled on Iran’s Shahed-136. They had copied the enemy’s weapon and sent it back to him.

The Israeli Air Force put up about 200 fighter jets. It was the largest sortie in Israeli history. From the F-15s fell a newly developed air-launched ballistic missile, the Black Sparrow. The targets were the Revolutionary Guards’ command-and-control sites, air defenses, missile and drone launch bases, and military airfields. Israel said that in the first 24 hours it used more than 1,200 bombs on 500 military targets. In Tehran the explosions ran along University Street and Jomhouri Street and up north through Seyed Khandan. In Qom, in Kermanshah, in Isfahan and Karaj and Bushehr too.

At 10:15 a.m. the Israeli Air Force hit a residential compound in Tehran. Three meetings were under way beneath that roof. It would be hours before the world learned who had been sitting there.

Nobody writes the novel where the precision strike is perfect.

A command post in the Alborz Mountains. Colonel Darius Sepahvand watched his radar screens go to noise. Thirty-one years of service. Two wars. Seven decorations. The command network fell apart in four minutes. As he watched it go, something rose in his chest that he had not expected at all.

Relief.

He crushed it at once, the way a man does who has built thirty years of career on the performance of obedience. Since 1979 everyone who wore this country’s uniform had learned that performance. But the feeling was there. Under the fear, under the professional shock. Relief.

He thought of his daughter. Twenty-four. Arrested twice by the morality police. Last year in the kitchen she had said it in a voice so low it hardly stirred the air. “Baba, the regime isn’t Iran. Iran is something else. Something they stole from us.”

He had not answered her then. In his own kitchen he had been a coward. This country had more kitchens like that than anyone could count.

Then something else happened.

The phone lying face down on the desk buzzed. It was BadeSaba, the prayer-calendar app. More than five million downloads in Iran. Instead of the prayer times the screen showed one line in Persian. Help has arrived. Then another. It is the hour of reckoning. After that came a call to lay down arms and turn away.

Every phone in the command post buzzed at the same moment. That morning Israel had broken into Iran’s infrastructure, its broadcasters, and its phone apps all at once. The bombs and the sentences arrived together. One broke buildings. The other shook obedience.

A nineteen-year-old Revolutionary Guard soldier stopped firing his rifle into the empty sky and looked down at his phone. He read the line twice. Who sent it? The enemy or God? Or were they on the same side, just this morning? Tears ran down his face. He could not explain them. Not to his commander, not to God, not to himself.

“This isn’t a war.”

He said it to no one in particular.

“This is something else.”

Scene 4. Eastern Mediterranean, off the coast of Israel — U.S. aircraft carrier USS Gerald R. Ford (CVN-78), bridge / flight deck

February 28, 2026 · 10:08 a.m. Tehran / 8:38 a.m. local

Captain Marcus Webb had gone twenty-six hours without sleep. When the last aircraft of the first wave left the electromagnetic catapult, his hands on the bridge were perfectly still. His fellow officers had long ago learned to read that stillness. The stiller the hands, the worse the storm inside.

He was forty-four. He had enlisted two weeks after 9/11. The world had split open and he had to put himself into the gap. Twenty-five years in. Now he stood on the bridge of the largest warship in human history and watched the fighters he had launched grow small in the eastern sky. Two weeks ago this ship had been in the Caribbean. She crossed the Atlantic and passed Gibraltar on February 20 and took her station off Israel only yesterday. It was a sight to raise the hair on your arms. It was also a sight to turn your stomach.

Sarah Callahan leaned on the bulkhead twelve feet to his left with her camera up. War correspondent for the Associated Press. Thirty-five. She had covered the fall of Mosul, the siege of Mariupol, the civil war in Ethiopia, and two Category 5 hurricanes. She had the stillness that only the best war correspondents have. It looked like calm. It was focus, honed very fine. She was focused now.

He did not know it, but she was watching Webb.

They had met three years ago in Bahrain, at a CENTCOM briefing she was covering. For six months he was her source. They had dinner twice. Once on the record and once not. Then he was transferred to Pacific Command. She did not call. He did not call. Eight days ago, when she came down by transport onto this deck as the ship passed Gibraltar, the silence between them was like a sentence broken off in the middle. A sentence waiting for its next word.

Neither of them finished it. They were both professionals, each in their own way.

“Air control to Captain Webb,” the communications officer said. “First wave all launched. All aircraft up. Lead reports air-defense radar is—hardly painting anything, Captain. They say it’s just off.”

Webb kept his eyes on the screen.

That was modern war. The speed of the algorithm decided more than human courage did. The decisions came faster than any person could react. The people on both sides were only witnesses.

Admiral Rees at the U.S. Fifth Fleet base in Bahrain came up on video. “Captain Webb. We’ve got a Republic of Korea Navy liaison officer here watching this operation. He asked me to pass along something in person. His exact words: ‘We are witnesses to history.’”

For a moment Webb said nothing.

Lieutenant Kim Junho, Republic of Korea Navy liaison officer. He sat in front of a monitor at the Fifth Fleet base in Bahrain and watched the numbers. Cold numbers. Human anger and the name of God were both data here. He remembered the pictures from a photo book on the Korean War, the day of Operation Chromite. General MacArthur’s bridge. Code name Chromite. Had there been screens like these that day? Had there been hands as still as these? That day, under that bridge, the fate of his own country had hung.

He could not know. He did not yet dare to guess what page of history he was looking at.

“Tell him,” Webb said, “we hope we get this right.”

Beside him Sarah Callahan’s shutter clicked quietly. What would that picture one day be the proof of?

Scene 5. Langley, Virginia — CIA Iran Operations Center

February 28, 2026 · 2:10 a.m. EST / 10:40 a.m. Tehran time

Dr. Leila Shirazi had been in this room for nineteen hours. The coffee had quit on her eight hours ago. Now she watched, on three satellite feeds, the capital of the country she was born in being bombed.

She was thirty-seven. She had been born in 1988 in Shiraz, Iran. That July the U.S. Navy cruiser USS Vincennes shot down Iran Air Flight 655 and killed 290 civilians. The U.S. government called it an accident, and the Iranian government called it murder. Both, each in its own way, were true. Her father was a doctor. Her mother taught mathematics at Shiraz University. In 2000, when Leila was twelve, her father made a decision—after a fellow professor was arrested, and he felt a certain door beginning to narrow. The arithmetic of survival. They came to the United States on academic visas and did not go back.

She had joined the CIA in 2010 at twenty-two, the year she finished her undergraduate degree at Georgetown University. Analysts who spoke Persian as a native language were rare even then. She earned her doctorate in political science at the same school while she worked. She spoke Persian, Arabic, French, and English. This was her sixteenth year as an Iran analyst. Seven months ago she had written a classified report. It came to one conclusion. Iran’s supreme leadership would not give up its nuclear-threshold posture whatever the economic pressure. And one sentence on the last page. “The only intervention capable of changing the trajectory is kinetic intervention.”

When she wrote that sentence she knew what she was justifying. In 1953 the people who came before her in this agency had written documents about Tehran too. At the end of those documents the Mossadegh government fell. Now her sentence was burning on three screens as columns of smoke.

The center screen showed satellite imagery of the Evin district in Tehran. She knew Evin well. It was where her aunt Fereshte lived. Seventy. A retired elementary-school teacher. A woman who made tea with rose petals and kept three cats. A woman who had never in her life done one thing anyone could call political.

There was a column of smoke on that screen now.

One precision-guided bomb had missed its target by 200 meters.

Leila stared at the screen and thought. I wrote the report. I built the strike model. I told them where the fault lines were. She thought. I am a patriot. I am an American. I believe in what we are trying to do. She thought. My aunt makes tea with rose petals.

There is a saying in Korea. A person with two nationalities has two hometowns of the heart. When the two go to war, the heart tears in two. Leila Shirazi did not cry. She had not been trained to cry. She sat like someone holding something very heavy. Very still, in case moving made her drop it.

“Shirazi,” the section chief called from across the room. “Can you look at the IRGC response-time analysis?”

“Yes,” she said. Her voice was steady. “Give me one minute.”

For that minute she watched the smoke on the screen and spoke without a sound. To her aunt Fereshte. To the city she had gone in and out of as a child. To the country that had made her first, before she became who she was now.

I am sorry. I believe this is necessary. I am so sorry.

Then she got up and walked over to look at the IRGC response-time analysis. No one in the room knew what had just happened. She would not tell them. Maybe never. That silence would not go into any document. Then who would remember it?

Scene 6. Tehran — residential streets in the northwest of the Evin district, Golestan Street

February 28, 2026 · 10:22 a.m.

But under the most precise operation, under the most carefully figured target list, under the battlefield where the finest targeting system ran—the mathematics of error was alive.

It was 200 meters.

In the geometry of the city, two hundred meters was the distance between a hardened Revolutionary Guard communications relay and a five-story apartment building on Golestan Street in Evin. One GBU-39 Small Diameter Bomb drifted. A weapon built to take out military sites with minimal collateral damage, a weapon that called itself the essence of precision strike. It went two hundred meters off its ballistic solution. The cause was a sensor malfunction. A strike review that only one CENTCOM subcommittee would ever read would one day say so. In 1988, when the Vincennes read an airliner as a fighter, the error ended up as one line in a report too.

The building came down in order. The top two floors first, then the middle, and then the ground floor settled onto the underground parking structure. The sound was not like an explosion in a movie. It was lower, and longer. After it there was only the sound of dust settling, of steel groaning, the small separate noises of what had been a home turning into rubble.

There was a child in that building.

Her name was Nariman. Seven years old. Big eyes. A gap between her front teeth. Two weeks ago she had put the lost tooth under her pillow. Her mother said everyone in the world did it, and her father laughed and said the custom really came from the West. They did it anyway. Nariman had been eating breakfast.

Her mother, Maryam, found her. Under a concrete slab that had come down over the child like a tent. Maryam was thirty-one, a nurse at Imam Khomeini Hospital. A woman who had come through the crackdown on the Green Movement, the COVID years, and three wars. Her hands were small and very strong. She lifted the concrete with her bare hands and pulled her daughter to her. In the dust and the silence and the far drone of aircraft engines she made a sound that is in no language.

War does not choose its victims by loyalty or by guilt.

War chooses by distance. By the geometry of error. By the two hundred meters between a military target and a breakfast table.

Nariman was alive. Her mother’s arms were around her. She was afraid and she did not understand. She would not understand for years. That is how trauma comes. It goes into the body first and reaches language long after.

In the street people were running, praying, or just looking up at the sky. Someone’s phone was still going off. The hacked prayer app still showed the same line. Help has arrived. The tools of a war that might be breaking this country and freeing it at the same time were sending words of salvation on the same morning. The irony was too big for one person to hold. Only history had a vessel that size. What would the child who lived through the rubble someday read in those words?

Scene 7. Tehran — Azadi Square, looking toward the smoke rising over the city center

February 28, 2026 · 11:50 a.m.

Professor Azad Moradi stood at the edge of the square and watched the smoke rise downtown to the east. With shaking hands he opened his leather journal.

Dust blew. A siren wailed somewhere. The Azadi Tower still stood on the skyline. White, tall, stubborn to the point of foolishness. The morning light came down on it. The light did not tell what had fallen from what still stood. The light never did.

Beyond the square young people were singing. Twenty of them, thirty. Men and women together. The women wore no hijab. They had no fear. It was a whole new kind of fearlessness, and that made it frightening. They sang the forbidden songs with their own throats. Not a line somebody had sent them. Their own throats. Not a hacked screen. Their own lungs. In the winter of 1979 there had been singing in this square too. That singing pulled down a king, and after it came forty-seven years of silence. Now the melodies forced into that silence came out of human mouths again.

The professor watched them, then opened the journal. And he wrote.

February 28, 2026. Today the black curtain that pressed down on us for forty-seven years was torn. The liberators came carrying bombs. Bombs do not tell the jailer from the prisoner. They fall on everyone alike—maybe this is the only democracy this country has known in half a century. I am seventy-two. I have lived my whole adult life inside this prison. I truly cannot tell whether what I see now is liberation or destruction. Probably both. Maybe one never comes without the other. Perhaps that is the root truth of every revolution—that a wall falls only when something breaks on both sides. God forgive us all. For what we have done to each other. And for what we are about to do.

He closed the journal. He looked out at the square.

The young people were still singing. In the dust, in the smoke, in the far sound of aircraft engines. The song did not stop. That was the one thing the oldest government, the strongest army, and the most precise bomb had never managed to silence.

Azad sat down on the stone edge of the square and put his face in his hands. And he cried. It was not only grief. It was not only joy. It was the weight of both being true at once. Only someone who has lived a long time can carry that weight. Long enough to see, all at once, that the moment of rupture is the thing he waited his whole life for and the thing he never once imagined.

Beyond the columns of dust on the horizon the sky was still blue. Cruel and indifferent and astonishingly beautiful. Would that song still be in this square tomorrow morning?

Scene 8. Eastern Mediterranean — the U.S. Ford Carrier Strike Group

February 28, 2026 · Noon Tehran / 10:30 a.m. local

The strike group moved like a city walking on the water.

The Gerald R. Ford was at the center. Every time the hundred-thousand-ton carrier swung her bow into the wind, fighters left her deck. Aegis destroyers ringed her and a replenishment ship followed. Add up the firepower of the group and it was more than the entire military of most countries on earth.

Captain Webb dragged the chart on the bridge display to the east. Past Israel and Jordan and Iraq, past Iran, to the narrow gap at its southern end. The Strait of Hormuz. Twenty-one miles between the Iranian coast and Oman’s Musandam Peninsula. In peacetime twenty percent of the world’s oil and seventeen percent of its LNG go through that gap. For more than forty years Iran had threatened to shut that door. Would this morning’s bombing keep the door open or make them close it? Nobody on this ship knew.

Sarah Callahan came up beside him. At the proper distance. The professional distance between a commander being covered and the reporter covering him. Still, she was there. Webb was aware of her without looking. The way you are aware of something that matters without looking at it.

“How does it feel?” she said, low enough that only he could hear.

He looked at the radar. He thought of the right answer. The professional answer an active-duty naval officer gives an AP correspondent on the record. Then he decided to give another one.

“Like starting something you understand the start of and not the end,” he said. “That’s always true. It scares me every time.”

She was quiet for a moment. The shutter clicked once. Then she said, very low:

“Me too.”

The war was a little more than two hours old. The world had already changed past turning back. Below decks the chaplain prayed quietly with twenty-three sailors who had come to his cabin without being asked. A former Marine with two tours in Afghanistan. A man who had come home with dreams that woke him at three every morning, nightmares that lived in him like permanent tenants. Some prayed for the operation to succeed. Some prayed for the civilians in Tehran. Most prayed for what every person in every war has prayed for. Let it be over fast. Let it not be as bad as it looks.

The war had only just begun.

The end, when it comes, will be written by people not yet born.

Operation Epic Fury — The Road to War

In January 2026, Iran’s security forces put down antigovernment protests in blood. Through January and February, the United States massed its largest force in the Middle East since the 2003 invasion of Iraq. All the while, nuclear talks between the United States and Iran went on, right through February. At 3:38 p.m. Eastern Standard Time on February 27, President Trump ordered the operation carried out. At 9:45 a.m. Tehran time on February 28, America’s Epic Fury and Israel’s Operation Roaring Lion began at the same moment. The world changed.

The Strait of Hormuz — The Most Important 21 Miles in the World

The Strait of Hormuz is the waterway that joins the Persian Gulf to the Gulf of Oman. At its narrowest it is 21 miles (about 33 km) wide. About 20% of the world’s traded crude oil and about 17% of its LNG pass through that gap. The trade is worth roughly $1.5 trillion a year. Since 1979 Iran has threatened 19 times to use the strait as a strategic weapon. Every U.S. administration since Carter has held to the principle that such a closure would be regarded as an act of war. In January and February 2026, the United States massed its largest force in the region since the 2003 invasion of Iraq. When the war began on February 28, the number of ships going through the strait fell fast, and between March 1 and 2 transits effectively stopped.

What Is Fact and What Is Fiction

Epic Fury — Operation name / Real

Operation Epic Fury is the actual name of the U.S. military operation against Iran that began on February 28, 2026. The Korean title of this novel is a translation of it. The operational details, the chain of command, and the conversations in the story are the author’s invention.

Operation Roaring Lion — Operation name / Real

The name of Israel’s operation against Iran, which began on February 28, 2026, at the same moment as America’s Epic Fury. The Israeli military said it was the largest sortie in its history, with about 200 fighter jets taking part, and that in the first 24 hours it used more than 1,200 bombs on 500 military targets.

Operation True Promise IV — Operation name / Real

The name of Iran’s retaliatory operation, which began a few hours after the start of the war. From 12:35 p.m. Tehran time on February 28, ballistic missiles and drones flew toward Israel and toward U.S. bases in the Gulf states. The Revolutionary Guards said they had targeted Al Udeid in Qatar, Ali Al Salem in Kuwait, Al Dhafra in the UAE, and the headquarters of the U.S. Fifth Fleet in Bahrain.

Tomahawk — Real weapon / U.S. Navy

A long-range cruise missile fired from U.S. Navy ships and submarines. It flies at low altitude, following the terrain, to slip past air defenses. U.S. Navy destroyers fired them at Iran on the first day of the war.

B-2 Spirit — Real weapon / U.S. Air Force

The U.S. Air Force’s stealth strategic bomber, built by Northrop Grumman. The U.S. military said that on the first day of the war it sent B-2s, B-1 Lancers, and B-52s against Iran’s hardened ballistic missile sites.

Black Sparrow — Real weapon / Israel

An air-launched ballistic missile newly developed by Israel. According to Israeli military sources, it was fired from F-15 fighters on the first day of the war.

Low-cost one-way attack drone (LUCAS) — Real weapon / U.S. military

The U.S. military’s Low-cost Uncrewed Combat Attack System, an expendable attack drone modeled on Iran’s Shahed-136. U.S. Central Command said this operation was its first use in combat.

BadeSaba — Real app / Cyberwarfare

A prayer-time and calendar app downloaded more than 5 million times in Iran. It was hacked on the morning of February 28 and, through Persian-language alerts such as “Help has arrived” and “The hour of judgment has come,” urged soldiers and civilians to desert and to resist. On the same day Israel also carried out cyberattacks on Iran’s infrastructure and broadcasters.

GBU-39 SDB (Small Diameter Bomb) — Real weapon / U.S. Air Force

A small precision-guided bomb made by Boeing. It weighs 130 kg and uses combined GPS and inertial guidance. A single fighter can carry several. Its stated design goal is “minimal collateral damage.” The errant strike on Golestan Street in the Evin district is an event invented for this novel.

USS Gerald R. Ford (CVN-78) — Real ship / U.S. Navy

Lead ship of the U.S. Navy’s Gerald R. Ford-class aircraft carriers. Full-load displacement about 100,000 tons; length 337 m. Nuclear powered. She carries the next-generation Electromagnetic Aircraft Launch System (EMALS). Commissioned in 2017. The largest warship in existence. In February 2026 she moved from the Caribbean to the Mediterranean, arrived off Israel on February 27, and launched her aircraft as the war began.

USS Vincennes / Iran Air Flight 655 — Real event / 1988

On July 3, 1988, the U.S. Navy cruiser USS Vincennes, in the Persian Gulf, mistook Iran Air Flight 655 for an enemy fighter and shot it down, killing all 290 people on board. The U.S. government treated it as a “mistake” and did not apologize; Iran maintains that it was a deliberate attack. It is one of the central wounds in the history of U.S.–Iranian distrust, and it happened in the year Leila Shirazi was born.

Enola Gay — Real aircraft / History

The U.S. Army Air Forces B-29 bomber that dropped the first nuclear weapon in human history, Little Boy, on Hiroshima, Japan, on August 6, 1945. Its pilot, Paul Tibbets, named it after his mother. The Hiroshima bomb killed 70,000 to 80,000 people outright that day alone. It is the archetype of the “war that began as a morning” that this novel summons in its opening paragraph.

NSA (National Security Agency) — Real agency

The principal U.S. intelligence agency responsible for collecting and analyzing signals intelligence (SIGINT). It intercepts and analyzes communications networks around the world. The breadth of its activities became public in 2013 through the disclosures of Edward Snowden.

CENTCOM (U.S. Central Command) — Real agency

The U.S. unified combatant command whose area of responsibility spans 20 countries in the Middle East, Central Asia, and East Africa. It ran the wars in Iraq and Afghanistan and the operations in Syria, among others. Its headquarters is at MacDill Air Force Base in Florida. It commanded Operation Epic Fury.

CIA (Central Intelligence Agency) — Real agency

The principal U.S. agency for foreign intelligence collection, analysis, and covert action. It is headquartered in Langley, Virginia. In this novel it is where Leila Shirazi has worked for 16 years as an Iran analyst. How a single sentence in a CIA analyst’s report comes to pull the trigger of a war is another of the novel’s axes.

Operation Chromite (the Inchon landing) — Real operation / Korean War

The decisive operation of the Korean War, commanded by General Douglas MacArthur on September 15, 1950. A surprise landing at the port of Inchon, in the North Korean army’s rear, reversed the course of the war. MacArthur himself chose the code name, Chromite. It is one of the boldest amphibious operations in military history, and it is the scene Lieutenant Kim Junho remembers laid over the history of today.

Azadi Tower — Real place / Tehran

The monument that is Tehran’s emblem, built in 1971 to mark the 2,500th anniversary of the founding of the Persian Empire. Azadi is Persian for “freedom.” It was not torn down after the 1979 Islamic Revolution; only the name was kept, and the building came to stand for the irony of Iran’s freedom without freedom. It is 45 m tall and built of marble, granite, and Isfahan tile.

Marcus Webb — Fictional character

U.S. Navy captain, commanding officer of USS Gerald R. Ford (fictional). Of the generation that enlisted right after 9/11—he was 19. The stillness of the hands and the storm inside are two faces of the same man.

Leila Shirazi — Fictional character

Senior analyst at the CIA Iran Operations Center (fictional). Born in Shiraz, Iran, in 1988; emigrated to the United States at 12. What a person loyal to two countries goes through at the moment those two countries set about destroying each other—that is the moral core of this novel.

Azad Moradi — Fictional character

Professor of philosophy at the University of Tehran (fictional). Age 72. A secularist and a moderate reformist. Azad is Persian for “free.” The choice is deliberate on the author’s part.

Nariman — Fictional character

Seven years old. Evin district, Tehran (fictional). She is in this novel because every war is, in the end, her war. In Persian, Nariman means “one who shines.” She was eating breakfast.

Sarah Callahan — Fictional character

War correspondent for the Associated Press (fictional). Age 35. Covered Mosul, Mariupol, and the civil war in Ethiopia. She believes that bearing witness can be an ethic. In this war that belief is put to the test.

Lieutenant Kim Junho — Fictional character

Republic of Korea Navy liaison officer (fictional), on assignment at the U.S. Fifth Fleet base in Bahrain. The Korean observer’s point of view: a man watching today’s history with the Inchon landing of the Korean War on his mind.

Darius Sepahvand — Fictional character

Colonel in Iran’s Revolutionary Guards, commander of an air-defense command post in the Alborz Mountains (fictional). Thirty-one years of service. The man who felt relief as he watched his command network come apart in four minutes. What that relief means is the question this novel asks.

Ali Khamenei — Real person / Supreme Leader of Iran

Second Supreme Leader of the Islamic Republic of Iran (1989–2026). He was killed on February 28, 2026, along with members of his family and the top military command, in an Israeli Air Force strike on his residential compound in Tehran. The Iranian government officially confirmed his death on March 1 and declared 40 days of mourning.

Minab School Attack — Real event / Southern Iran

On the morning of February 28, 2026, Shajareh Tayyebeh girls’ elementary school in Minab, southern Iran, was hit by a missile. Iranian media reported 180 dead; later tallies put the toll at more than 150, about 120 of them children. The strike has been attributed to a U.S. Tomahawk missile. It is the incident in which the most civilians died at one time in this war.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. What is Epic Fury Episode 1 about?

A. Some walls come down in a single night. Clearing the rubble takes a generation.

Q. What is Epic Fury?

A. Operation Epic Fury is the actual name of the U.S. military operation against Iran that began on February 28, 2026. The Korean title of this novel is a translation of it. The operational details, the chain of command, and the conversations in the story are the author’s invention.

Q. What is Operation Roaring Lion?

A. The name of Israel’s operation against Iran, which began on February 28, 2026, at the same moment as America’s Epic Fury. The Israeli military said it was the largest sortie in its history, with about 200 fighter jets taking part, and that in the first 24 hours it used more than 1,200 bombs on 500 military targets.

Q. What is Operation True Promise IV?

A. The name of Iran’s retaliatory operation, which began a few hours after the start of the war. From 12:35 p.m. Tehran time on February 28, ballistic missiles and drones flew toward Israel and toward U.S. bases in the Gulf states. The Revolutionary Guards said they had targeted Al Udeid in Qatar, Ali Al Salem in Kuwait, Al Dhafra in the UAE, and the headquarters of the U.S. Fifth Fleet in Bahrain.

Q. Is this novel a true story?

A. No. It is fiction built on facts confirmed by public reporting. Characters such as Marcus Webb, Azad Moradi, Sarah Callahan, Kang Yujin, and all dialogue and classified documents, are invented.

Q. Can I watch it as a video?
