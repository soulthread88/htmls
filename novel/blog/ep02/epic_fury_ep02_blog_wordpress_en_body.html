Episode 2, “The Arithmetic of Decapitation”

Scene 1. Langley, Virginia — CIA Iran Operations Center

March 1, 2026 (Sun.) · 3:12 a.m. EST / 11:42 a.m. Tehran — War Day 2

The screen changed first.

Dr. Leila Shirazi was setting down her third coffee when her hand stopped. The third monitor on the right, the electromagnetic signal channel for the Fordow nuclear site. That screen was showing her something it should not have been able to show.

The Fordow Fuel Enrichment Plant is a uranium enrichment site buried deep inside a mountain about 30 kilometers north of Qom, Iran. Until it was exposed in 2009, its very existence was a secret. On June 22, 2025, six U.S. Air Force B-2 bombers dropped twelve GBU-57 MOPs (Massive Ordnance Penetrators, the largest bunker busters in the arsenal) on it. Satellite photographs showed penetration holes directly above the enrichment halls, and that September Rafael Grossi, director general of the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA), confirmed that nearly all of the sensitive equipment had been destroyed. So Fordow was not on the target list for this war. On the first day of the war, the IAEA said it had found no evidence that any of Iran’s nuclear facilities had been hit. Fordow—officially—was already a dead site.

Leila pulled her chair in. The signal dropped out and came back. Every 5.7 seconds. Steady as a pulse. A low-frequency electromagnetic emission. The kind that comes up only from underground facilities.

The centrifuges were turning.

Fordow was alive.

She looked at the screen for thirty seconds. Then she called over the partition.

“Sam. Get over here.”

Sam Farrell came. Senior satellite analyst. Forty-six. From Nashville. Three days of beard, which meant he had not been home since the war started. He looked at the screen. Then he swore under his breath.

“Let’s cross-check,” he said. “Run it against the seismic data. We have to rule out the tunnels that caved in last year settling. Machine vibration or collapse.”

“Already did,” Leila said. “The vibration’s regular. It’s not a collapse. And this period—” she put her finger on the number “—is the operating frequency of an IR-6 centrifuge. I’ve looked at nothing else for seven years.”

Sam did not say anything. The silence was his answer.

Leila opened a draft of the report and stopped. This agency had brought down a government in Tehran in 1953. The people under the mountain had not forgotten the year. On the screen the signal came and went under the thickest concrete men had ever poured, like a pulse, stubborn.

Deep under the mountain someone was running the machines. While the world burned. While the Supreme Leader died. While history turned.

Somebody had brought the dead machines back to life. And had decided not to shut them off.

Even when a nation falls, a scientist sometimes will not stop doing his job. Is that loyalty or madness? Out at this edge the two words go by the same name.

Leila saved the report. The title was three words: Fordow—Confirmation Required. No bomber, no cruise missile, nobody in this war had those coordinates in their sights. Tomorrow, whose hands would they be in?

Scene 2. Alborz Mountains, Iran — north of Tehran, Iranian Air Defense Force command post

March 1, 2026 (Sun.) · 12:20 p.m. (Tehran local time) — War Day 2

The dead man’s name finally came on state television.

Colonel Darius Sepahvand stood in a corner of the command post in front of an old television. A black band across the screen. A reading from the Koran. The anchor’s voice shaking. The martyrdom of the Supreme Leader, Ali Khamenei. Forty days of mourning. The news the enemy’s broadcasts had been shouting all day yesterday he heard this morning, at last, in his own country’s voice.

On March 1 the Iranian government officially confirmed Khamenei’s death and declared a forty-day period of national mourning. The same day Ali Larijani, secretary of the Supreme National Security Council, announced the formation of an interim leadership council to run the country until a successor was chosen. The bombing did not stop. In Tehran that day the Revolutionary Court building was destroyed and the Revolutionary Guards’ Tharallah headquarters came down, and explosions continued near Azadi Square and Milad Tower. The Chinese-made HQ-9B long-range air-defense batteries ringing the capital were reported to have been neutralized.

Darius had known that last sentence before anyone. Since yesterday there had been nothing on his radar but noise. For thirty-one years he had guarded the sky. Now the sky belonged to someone else. Enemy aircraft came and went over Tehran as if it were their own yard. In the spring of 1988, when Iraq’s missiles fell on this city, there had still been men to guard the sky. Now there were no batteries left to take an order to intercept. There was nobody left to give it. The chief of staff, the commander of the Revolutionary Guards, and the defense minister had died yesterday in one meeting room.

The young officers looked at him. Eyes waiting for orders. Darius met them one at a time. There was grief in them. There was anger. Under both was the thing nobody said. Who speaks to us now?

“The south is still firing,” the signals officer said. “Revolutionary Guard Aerospace Force. Into the Gulf. At Tel Aviv.”

“I know,” Darius said.

There was a sum nobody in the room would work out loud. You fire missiles and they run out. A launcher gives away its position the moment it fires, and a launcher that has given itself away is gone inside an hour. What was flying now was an answer. An answer fired for the world to see. On the day they ran out, what would they answer with? There was no leader in this country now to take that question.

He thought of the apartment west of Tehran. Where his daughter Narges was. Twenty-four. He had not been able to reach her since yesterday afternoon. The internet was running at a hundredth of normal. A soldier holds his post. For thirty-one years he had believed that sentence without doubt. Today he was holding on to it. Believing a thing and holding on to it are different. He learned that for the first time at fifty-two.

The head was cut off. The body still stood, waiting for orders.

Scene 3. Bahrain — Mina Salman Port, headquarters of the U.S. Fifth Fleet

March 1, 2026 (Sun.) · 2:30 p.m. (Bahrain local time)

Lieutenant Kim Junho was in the head when the missile-warning sirens went off.

He was twenty-nine. He had been posted as a Republic of Korea Navy liaison officer for eight months. He had never seen real war. Not until yesterday morning. At 12:26 yesterday afternoon the first Iranian missile hit a support facility on this base. From that moment on he was what the world calls a man who has seen war.

The sirens did not stop. He ran out into the corridor. Everybody was running. People in protective suits, people in helmets, people carrying stacks of files down to the underground command center. He fell in with them. The moment he came into the command center he saw the screens.

The Revolutionary Guards’ missiles and drones were already in the air. The tracks multiplied one by one. On the target-analysis screen Mina Salman Port came up. Bahrain. Fifth Fleet headquarters.

Where he was standing.

For the next ninety seconds the air-defense system walked through its intercept sequence. Later he would remember them as the longest ninety seconds of his life. The Patriot PAC-3 batteries fired again and again. Missiles came into Bahraini airspace and burst. You could see the flashes in broad daylight. The shock waves shook the floor of the underground command center.

He was holding on to the leg of a desk. His hands were shaking.

Nearly all of them were intercepted. Nearly. A few minutes later the report came up. One drone that got through the net had hit the Crowne Plaza hotel in downtown Manama. The Revolutionary Guards sent the next wave. And the next. The Israeli military estimated that about 170 ballistic missiles flew at Israel on the first day alone. Nobody had finished counting what came down on the Gulf. The air defenses held. For now.

When the alert was lifted Kim Junho sat in a chair and looked down at his shaking hands. Yesterday, thinking of the bridge at Inchon, he had asked himself whether there had been still hands that day too. Now he knew half the answer. His were not still, at least. Still hands take something like twenty-five years to make. Or a man is born with them. Either way, he did not have them yet.

He worked out the time in Korea. Eight-thirty in the evening in Seoul. His mother would have the news on. His father would be sitting in front of that TV without a word. The same pictures would be on in countless living rooms all over Seoul. He did not call. Not once since the war began. He did not know what to say. To be honest, he was afraid they would hear his voice shake.

“You okay?” the American officer next to him asked.

“Yes,” he said. It was a lie. It was also true. It was the most complicated kind of answer. Would he be able to give the same answer at the next siren?

Scene 4. Tehran — Vali-e Asr Avenue, northern end of the old city

March 1, 2026 (Sun.) · 3:45 p.m. (Tehran local time) — the day Khamenei’s death was officially confirmed

The sky over Tehran was black. Smoke from the ruined Revolutionary Court and the Sarallah headquarters blotted out the sun. But the streets were not quiet.

On the afternoon state television announced Khamenei’s death, there were two crowds in Tehran. In Enghelab Square, which is named for the revolution, mourners gathered in black. On Valiasr Avenue people had gathered for another reason. Gunfire and explosions came in bursts. Between them came singing.

“Freedom! Freedom! Iran is free!” (آزادی! آزادی! ایران آزاد!)

At first the demonstrators were a few dozen. Thirty minutes later they were a few hundred. They threw off their hijabs. They waved the Lion and Sun flag of the Pahlavi dynasty. Some held up pictures of the exiled crown prince, Reza Pahlavi. Some held nothing and only shouted. What had been pressed down in their throats for forty-seven years came out ahead of the bombs.

And then the Basij came.

The Basij, the mobilization force of Iran’s Islamic Revolution, is a paramilitary militia under the Revolutionary Guards. Since the 1979 revolution it has been called out to put down anti-government protests, to act as morality police, and to keep internal order. It crushed demonstrators by force in the suppression of the 2009 Green Movement, and it was on the front line again in the Mahsa Amini protests of 2022. As word of Khamenei’s death spread, cheering people came out into the streets of several Iranian cities, and the security forces opened fire on them. There were accounts, too, of shots fired at people chanting slogans from inside the windows of their homes. Special forces were deployed in Tehran’s Jannatabad district. Iran’s internet traffic fell to 1 percent of normal, and the shutdown lasted more than 60 hours.

The Basij pushed into the crowd. Tear gas first. Then batons. Somewhere in an alley a shot went off. People scattered and gathered again. Then, right in front of Soraya, a young militiaman who had gotten off a motorcycle stopped. His feet stopped and his baton came down. He turned his head away. There was an expression you could read on his face.

If you gave the expression a name it was I don’t know either.

The war had brought the enemy in from outside. The crack it opened was starting to split the inside. Sometimes a revolution begins not with bombing from outside but with someone stopping inside. In 1989 the Berlin Wall came down not with gunfire but with hesitation.

Soraya Tahir was in that street. Twenty-two. A third-year electrical engineering student at Sharif University of Technology. She had been in the street during the Mahsa Amini protests in 2022. She was arrested and held for six months and let go. She came out again. Without a hijab. This time she had no fear. No, there was fear. There was something bigger than fear.

She saw that one man stop. The other militiamen did not stop. Still she watched him stop and thought.

He’s a person too. The one with the gun is a person too. People stop sometimes.

That was what made it different from the bombing outside. Bombs do not stop. People can. How many would stop tomorrow?

Scene 5. Eastern Mediterranean, off the coast of Israel — bridge of the U.S. aircraft carrier USS Gerald R. Ford (CVN-78)

March 2, 2026 (Mon.) · 6:44 a.m. (Israel local time) — War Day 3

Shadows had settled under Captain Marcus Webb’s eyes.

Since the war began he had not slept six hours all told. His hands were still anyway. The officers on the bridge took comfort from that stillness. They did not know that it did not come from comfort. Nor from knowing what to do. He had simply decided to show nothing.

Overnight the war had grown as wide as a country. Hezbollah in Lebanon fired rockets at Haifa. The Israeli military called it a formal declaration of war. Now, past the horizon northeast of the Ford, the Israeli Air Force was hitting Beirut. The air wing never stopped flying. Each time the electromagnetic catapult let out its breath the glass on the bridge trembled faintly.

The dawn intelligence summary lay on the desk. Webb’s eyes were not on the Mediterranean. They had stopped on an item from another sea more than a thousand miles away.

Iran had declared the Strait of Hormuz closed. On March 1, five nautical miles north of Khasab, Oman, the tanker Skylight was hit and four people were injured, and aboard the tanker MKD Vyom one person was killed. When the insurers pulled out, tanker transits effectively stopped. About 150 cargo ships were stranded in and around the strait. On the morning of Monday, March 2, Brent crude jumped more than 7 percent to trade around $78 a barrel, and just after the Sunday-night open it had briefly gone above $80. The same day QatarEnergy said two of its gas facilities had been attacked and halted LNG production. Saudi Arabia’s refinery complex at Ras Tanura was damaged as well. And at the port of Shuaiba in Kuwait, an Iranian drone attack killed six U.S. service members. They were the first Americans killed in action in this war.

Six. Webb read the number twice. No names yet. The families had to be told first. He knew the procedure. Two people in uniform standing at a front door. In twenty-five years he had twice been on the side that knocks.

His ship was far from that strait. Orders had put him here and he understood the orders. Guard the Israeli sky. Hold up the northern front so it did not give way. But Iran’s arithmetic was running on another sea. Lose the head and hold on anyway. Take the world economy by the throat. Raise the price America pays a day at a time.

Webb drank his coffee. It was cold. He thought: this is modern war. A war where algorithms and the price of oil and insurance premiums and diplomacy and ballistic missiles all turn at once. A war about who can hold out longer.

Sarah Callahan came and stood beside him. Today she did not keep her distance. They looked at the eastern horizon. Where the sun comes up. Very far beyond it was a channel twenty-one nautical miles wide. The world’s throat.

“I filed a story yesterday,” she said. “About a little girl named Nariman. Evin district. Our Tehran correspondent sent the reporting right before the internet went down, and I wrote it up.”

He remembered the name. He had not heard it himself. Somebody had read it in a report. Civilian casualties, Evin district, including a child. The child in the report had no name. She had found one.

“Is she alive?” he asked.

“She’s alive,” she said. “Her mother found her. Under the concrete.” A silence. “The second it ran it got shared everywhere. The desk says millions of people read it in one day.”

He looked at the sea. The world wanted that child’s story. Not the oil-price chart. Not the sortie count. Not the strategic map.

“That’s right,” he said. “The world ought to remember that.”

“It was our bomb that did it,” she said quietly. It was not an accusation. It was a fact.

He did not answer. Not answering was the answer. In the summer of 1988 men in the same uniform had shot an Iranian airliner out of the sky over the Gulf. The Navy did not forget it, and Iran forgot it less. He knew that, and he stood on this bridge. A soldier on active duty is someone who knows and keeps going. That was the weight of the job. Who would remember the names this time?

Scene 6. Tehran — University of Tehran, Department of Philosophy office, Building 16

March 3, 2026 (Tue.) · 11:10 a.m. (Tehran local time) — War Day 4

For the first time in fifty-three years, Professor Azad Moradi opened the drawer of his office desk.

At the bottom of the drawer was a notebook he had bought in 1973. The Shah’s time. The last golden years of the Pahlavi dynasty. A notebook a twenty-year-old had bought, giddy after his first philosophy class. On the first page, in his handwriting: ‘Truth is uncomfortable. But a lie hurts longer.’

He took the notebook out and put it on the desk. A column of black smoke stood outside the window. That morning his teaching assistant had brought the news. The Qajar dynasty’s Golestan Palace had been damaged by a bomb that fell nearby. He had walked students through its Hall of Mirrors thirty times. There was another explosion somewhere. Far off. What was close was the singing beyond the window.

The students were singing. In the courtyard. Without hijabs. Men and women together. The last time that had happened in the courtyard of the University of Tehran was before 1979. Forty-seven years.

Professor Azad opened the notebook and found the last blank page. A page with nothing on it. A page he had not once filled in fifty-three years. He picked up his pen.

March 3, 2026. Khamenei is dead. I do not grieve his death. I still do not know whether that betrays this country or is faithful to the truth. Both may be so. Outside the window the students are singing. Did these voices, pressed down for forty-seven years, burst out because of the bombs, or would they have burst out someday without them? I do not know. But I know this. That I can hear those voices now. That I am listening to them. And that I, a seventy-two-year-old philosopher, am crying. Why am I crying? I do not know that either. Maybe that is the answer.

He put down the pen. He closed the notebook. Fifty-three years. The last page was full.

The singing outside the window did not stop. In the smoke. In the war. At the end of forty-seven years of silence. That singing was stranger than the bombs, and more frightening. A bomb breaks something and is done. A song is the will to build something. If the will to build is left, hope is left. That was the most astonishing fact of this war. There had been singing in this courtyard in 1979 too. What would this song build?

Scene 7. Langley, Virginia — CIA Iran Operations Center, emergency conference room

March 3, 2026 (Tue.) · 6:55 a.m. EST / 3:25 p.m. Tehran — War Day 4

A meeting was called to analyze the Fordow signal. It had been fifty-one hours since Leila filed her report.

There were seven people in the room. The CIA deputy director. An analyst from the Defense Intelligence Agency. The CENTCOM liaison officer. A representative of the National Security Agency’s technical analysis team. Leila Shirazi and Sam Farrell. And one more. A man from the National Security Council (NSC) staff, introduced without a name.

Leila began the briefing. The signal’s characteristics. The frequency pattern. A comparison with the operating profile of the IR-6 centrifuge. The cross-check against the seismic data. Conclusion: there is better than an 85 percent probability that some of the centrifuge halls in the Fordow underground facility are still operating.

The conference room went quiet.

“Meaning,” the CENTCOM liaison officer said, “last June’s strike was incomplete? Twelve bunker busters?”

“Meaning,” Leila said, “there are people under the mountain at Fordow right now, and they’re running machines. What they’ve put back in there over nine months, I don’t know.”

“We hit Natanz yesterday,” the DIA analyst said. “Took out the entrance buildings. Fordow wasn’t on the list.”

“The reason it wasn’t on the list just went away,” Sam said.

The silence came down again.

“Are you recommending another strike?” somebody asked.

Leila had expected the question. She had thought about it while she wrote the report. She had thought about it all night. She already knew her answer.

“That’s not mine to decide,” she said. “I’m an analyst. I give you the information. Other people decide what to do with it.”

It was true. And she knew it. It was her information that had made those other people’s decisions. A report seven months ago had given this war its justification. A signal report fifty-one hours ago could put a mountain nobody was aiming at on the target list. The old hands in this building remembered Iraq in 2003. The year a few pages of reports opened the door to a war.

She was an analyst. She stated facts. That was right. But was stating a fact while knowing what it would call down the same as just stating a fact?

Under the fluorescent lights at Langley she did not know the answer yet. She might never know it.

The meeting ended. She walked down the corridor. Through the window at the end she could see the Virginia morning sky. A blue sky. About 6,300 miles away, the sky over Tehran was black.

The two skies did not know each other. She was somewhere between them. Under the mountain at Fordow were the people who made the signal she had seen. People whose names she did not know. People who, for their country or for something else, had decided in the middle of a war not to turn the machines off.

We believe we can beat an enemy we have never understood. War taxes that belief without end.

Leila went back to her desk. The monitors were waiting. The signal was waiting. The war was waiting. That was her job, and it was why she was in this room today. And it was why she could not sleep. Which mountain would the next report point to?

At the same hour in Seoul, Kang Yujin had not turned off her screen. Her lab, close to nine at night. She was writing an algorithm to predict the hostile behavior of AI systems. A breaking-news alert popped up. She did not close the window. For a moment, just a moment, she looked at it.

The Death of Khamenei — A Crack in Iran’s 47-Year Theocracy

Ali Khamenei (1939–2026) succeeded Ruhollah Khomeini and from 1989 was the Supreme Leader (Rahbar) of the Islamic Republic of Iran. For more than 36 years he gave final approval to every major decision Iran made, and whether the nuclear program went on was his to decide as well. On the morning of February 28, 2026, the Israeli air force struck the Supreme Leader’s residential compound in Tehran, and he died there. The Iranian government officially confirmed his death the next day, March 1.

The problem was the succession. The Supreme Leader is elected by the Assembly of Experts (88 clerics). Under Velayat-e Faqih, the “guardianship of the jurist” that is the theoretical foundation of Iran’s theocracy, the office demands high clerical rank and theological authority. On March 3 the Assembly of Experts building and the Supreme National Security Council building were bombed. Iranian state media reported that Khamenei’s second son, Mojtaba Khamenei, was alive, and the Assembly of Experts later elected him successor. Whether he meets the requirement of clerical rank was an old controversy. But in the middle of a war in which the building of the body that does the electing was being bombed, no one had the leisure to argue the point. History sometimes puts survival ahead of qualification.

The Stalled Strait — The Bill the World Economy Has Begun to Receive

A strait can be stopped without a single mine. When two tankers were hit near Hormuz on March 1, it was not the Iranian navy that brought the ships to a halt but the insurers. Shipowners would not send vessels into waters where war-risk coverage had vanished. About 150 ships came to a stop in and around the strait. The crude that passes through here runs to 13 million to 15 million barrels a day, about 20 percent of world supply.

The market’s first reaction was less panic than arithmetic. On the morning of Monday, March 2, Brent crude rose more than 7 percent to trade around $78 a barrel, and just after the open it briefly went above $80. Analysts warned that it could top $100 if the halt in transits dragged on. The sharper shock was gas. QatarEnergy halted LNG production after two of its gas facilities were attacked. Qatar accounts for about a fifth of the world’s LNG supply. The power plants and boilers of South Korea, Japan, and Taiwan are tied to those ships. This war began as a Middle Eastern war, but the bill was being made out to the world.

What Is Fact and What Is Fiction

True Promise IV — Real operation name / Iran

The name of the retaliatory operation that Iran’s Revolutionary Guards launched at 12:35 p.m. (Tehran time) on February 28, 2026. “True Promise” (وعده صادق) was being used for the fourth time, after the attacks on Israel of April and October 2024 and June 2025. The targets were Israel and the U.S. bases, ports, and airports in the Gulf states. On March 1 a missile fell on a synagogue in Beit Shemesh, Israel, killing 9 people and wounding 65.

Fordow Fuel Enrichment Plant — Real facility · Fictional

An underground uranium enrichment facility in the mountains north of Qom. On June 22, 2025, six U.S. Air Force B-2s dropped twelve GBU-57s on it, and that September the IAEA confirmed that nearly all of the sensitive equipment had been destroyed. There is no public record of the site being struck again in the early days of the 2026 war. The “operating signal” that Leila Shirazi picks up in this novel, and the CIA assessment document, are the author’s invention.

IR-6 Centrifuge — Real technology / Iran’s nuclear program

A high-performance uranium enrichment centrifuge developed by Iran itself. It is understood to have several times the enrichment capacity of the early IR-1, and before June 2025 it was installed at Fordow as well.

Interim Leadership Council — Real event / Iran

The body that exercises the Supreme Leader’s powers, when the office falls vacant, until a successor is elected. Ali Larijani, secretary of the Supreme National Security Council, announced its formation on March 1. The power to elect the Supreme Leader rests with the Assembly of Experts, which is made up of 88 clerics.

Mojtaba Khamenei — Real person / Iran

Second son of Ali Khamenei. Born in 1969. He is known as a figure who, without holding any official post, has wielded influence between his father’s office and the Revolutionary Guards. After the Assembly of Experts building was bombed on March 3, Iranian state media reported that he was alive, and the Assembly of Experts later elected him successor. This novel does not invent his words or his private thoughts.

Hezbollah Enters the War — Real event / Lebanon

When Hezbollah attacked Haifa on March 2, the Israeli military characterized it as an official declaration of war and launched airstrikes on Beirut. On March 3 Israel approved a ground operation in Lebanon.

The Drone Attack on Shuaiba Port — Real event / Kuwait

On March 1 the port of Shuaiba, near Camp Arifjan in Kuwait, came under attack by Iranian drones, and 6 U.S. service members were killed. They are the first Americans killed in action in this war. On March 2 three U.S. Air Force F-15s went down over Kuwait after being fired on by friendly forces in error.

Hormuz at a Standstill — Real event

Iran declared the strait closed immediately after the war began. On March 1 the tankers Skylight (5 nautical miles north of Khasab, Oman; 4 injured) and MKD Vyom (1 killed) were hit, and about 150 ships were stranded. The halt in transits in this period was brought about not by mines but by drone and missile attacks and by insurers’ refusal to underwrite. Iranian minelaying is confirmed only from March 10.

Basij — Real organization / Iran

A paramilitary militia under Iran’s Revolutionary Guards. It was founded on Khomeini’s orders just after the 1979 revolution. After word of Khamenei’s death spread, there were reports that security forces had fired on cheering civilians in several Iranian cities. The scene in this novel in which one member stops in the middle of the crackdown is the author’s invention.

Golestan Palace — Real heritage site / Tehran

A palace of the Qajar dynasty and a UNESCO World Heritage site. It was damaged by nearby bombing on March 3.

Reza Pahlavi — Real person

Iran’s last crown prince. Born in 1960. He has lived in exile in the United States since the Pahlavi dynasty fell in the 1979 revolution, and he is a symbolic figure for part of the Iranian opposition.

Soraya Tahir — Fictional character

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. What is Epic Fury Episode 2 about?
A. They cut off the head. But the body lived. And the body—the body still remembered its fury.
Q. What is True Promise IV?
A. The name of the retaliatory operation that Iran’s Revolutionary Guards launched at 12:35 p.m. (Tehran time) on February 28, 2026. “True Promise” (وعده صادق) was being used for the fourth time, after the attacks on Israel of April and October 2024 and June 2025. The targets were Israel and the U.S. bases, ports, and airports in the Gulf states. On March 1 a missile fell on a synagogue in Beit Shemesh, Israel, killing 9 people and wounding 65.
Q. What is Fordow Fuel Enrichment Plant?
A. An underground uranium enrichment facility in the mountains north of Qom. On June 22, 2025, six U.S. Air Force B-2s dropped twelve GBU-57s on it, and that September the IAEA confirmed that nearly all of the sensitive equipment had been destroyed. There is no public record of the site being struck again in the early days of the 2026 war. The “operating signal” that Leila Shirazi picks up in this novel, and the CIA assessment document, are the author’s invention.
Q. What is IR-6 Centrifuge?
A. A high-performance uranium enrichment centrifuge developed by Iran itself. It is understood to have several times the enrichment capacity of the early IR-1, and before June 2025 it was installed at Fordow as well.
Q. Is this novel a true story?
A. No. It is fiction built on facts confirmed by public reporting. Characters such as Leila Shirazi, Marcus Webb, Darius Sepahvand, Kim Junho, and all dialogue and classified documents, are invented.
