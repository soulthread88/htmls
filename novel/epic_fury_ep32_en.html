May 14, 2026 (Thu.) · 6:00 a.m. (Tehran local time) — Ceasefire Day 37 · War Day 76
Scene 1 / 5

06:00:00. A dot rose. He already knew.

This time it wasn’t beyond the ridge. Right over the Damavand road. The sky over the spot where he had turned on the decoy a month and a half ago. Civilian code.

Darius Sepahvand was standing. His breath went in and out evenly. On April 28 he had held his breath at one twenty-seven. Today there was nothing to hold. One sheet of paper had given him back his breath.

Turn nothing on at the top of the hour.

The person who wrote that sentence doesn’t turn anything on at the top of the hour. So what was wearing a name tag in that sky now wasn’t that person. That was what the paper under the saucer had given him. An ear that could pick out one person’s voice. The ear sentries had carried for thousands of years.

“Hold fire. Wait.”

At the console sat Second Lieutenant Sohrab Jahangiri. Two days since the hold on him was lifted. His first dawn watch back in the signals room. The second lieutenant read the seconds. His voice was even and low.

“Thirty. Sixty. Ninety.”

Darius watched the dot on the screen. Slow, even, without a tremor. He remembered there had been something in the April 1 dot. Someone over there holding their breath and watching him. Even that memory wasn’t certain anymore. But in today’s dot there was no one. That much was certain. In a perfect thing, there was no person.

“One twenty. One twenty-five. One twenty-six. One twenty-seven. — Off.”

Exact. Not off by a second. The exactness was the evidence.

Sohrab looked up from the console. Looked at the colonel. One twenty-seven. The second lieutenant knew that number. The number he had heard for three weeks in the room underground. The length of time this command post’s decoy radar had been on, on the morning of March 30, while he was counting fuel in the generator room.

The second lieutenant didn’t ask anything. He asked by not asking. The young people of this country learned early to speak that way.

“Write what you saw,” Darius said. “Unidentified track. Civilian code response, one hundred twenty-seven seconds. No engagement.”

“One hundred twenty-seven seconds. No engagement.” Sohrab wrote it down. The pen stopped once. “Colonel. The reason box?”

“Reason unknown.”

The second lieutenant wrote those words. The words he had written six times in his inventory report eleven days ago. If you write that you know something you don’t, the next man who trusts that paper gets hurt, he had said. Today the words were used the other way. Saying you don’t know what you know. It protected someone.

Darius left the operations room. The corridor was cool.

He had expected to be angry. That number was his brother’s. The last three digits of the tag that had hung around the neck of a boy who died at seventeen on the southern front. For thirty-nine years he had told no one. And one dawn in March he had given it to exactly one person in the world. Someone whose face he didn’t know. It was the deepest thing he had.

Now the other side’s machine had that number. A tool to take out when needed and fly in the sky. Exactly. Not off by a second. With no meaning. A boy’s death had become a variable in another country’s machine.

But he wasn’t angry. It was strange. He looked for the reason a long time.

A number can be imitated. It always could. It was the only thing he had been able to give the other side, but a number is something anyone can count. His brother wasn’t that number. His brother was the thirteen-year-old at the far end of the roof shouting Halt, who goes there. Firing the water pistol if you got it wrong. The machine couldn’t pick that up. And someone on the other side of the world, without even knowing about that roof, had called to him with the same scene of the same book.

They could have one twenty-seven. He had something better. Memory couldn’t be counted.

Back in his office he opened the desk drawer. His brother’s photo, taken down and put away the day the war began. Seventeen. The uniform too big. He took it out and hung it back on the nail on the wall. After seventy-five days. A boy who died in one war came back into the room of another.

His brother could watch what happened in this room. Now.

They could have one twenty-seven. His brother wasn’t that number. His brother was the thirteen-year-old at the far end of the roof shouting “Halt, who goes there.”

Scene 2 / 5

At ten on Thursday, Yoon Jaeha began with Park Sejun’s whereabouts.

“On the night of May 4 he flew from Incheon to Istanbul. Two days later, from there to Baku. After that, we don’t know either. The number that texted you is Azerbaijani.”

“Are you going to go get him?”

“For what crime?” he said. “Signing a four-page document isn’t in the criminal code. He got scared, and scared people run. But one thing he said is right. That a day could come when a leaker is needed. My job is to make sure you’re not standing in that spot that day. You not signing yesterday, you putting your requests in the minutes. That’s all that job.”

Kang Yujin couldn’t tell whether those words made her grateful or afraid. This man was protecting her. And he didn’t hide that protecting her would become a card this country could put down in front of the other side. Inside an alliance, a small country spoke even of protecting its own people in the language of negotiation.

At eleven she reached the operations room. Thirty minutes until 02:30 UTC.

Six days ago the model had put out a table. In format v2. LIKELY (0.83) — counterpart response to 127-second identification, 05-14 02:30Z. She had watched it go out and let it pass as usual. The counterpart’s response. To a 127-second identification signal. To her it had been something between a radar and a transponder. Between machines.

At 11:34 lines sprouted.

interval = 127. source: airborne. 02:30:00–02:32:07 UTC. counterpart response: none (window +6h pending).

And beneath them, the list of tags on the inputs the model had used as its basis. Usually collapsed. Since last week she had gotten into the habit of keeping it open.

basis: LNO-INGEST / analytic product ×7. keywords: SOURCE, HANDLER, RECONTACT, ASSET STATUS.

She read the words. Once. Twice.

Source. Handler. Re-contact. Asset status.

Those weren’t machine words. She hadn’t read many spy novels, but she knew that much. A source. The person handling that source. Reaching them again. And asset. The word of people who call people assets.

Counterpart. The other party. She had read it as a radar. For six days. It was a person. Someone somewhere in that country who was supposed to answer when one hundred twenty-seven seconds rose in the sky. An Iranian working for America. A person who dies if caught. Her machine had been putting a probability on one person’s life.

And her model had sent out the probability that this person would answer as zero point eight three. LIKELY. In capitals. In the format she had fixed. Someone on the other side had probably seen that word and decided to fly the signal. Because the machine said it was likely.

The person didn’t answer. Silence beat the number.

Why hadn’t he answered? Caught? Dead? Or had he thought the signal was a trap? She had no way of knowing. She knew only that the person was now in some room in that country, with the eyes that had just seen what rose in the sky, enduring something.

Ten days ago, one night, she had been ashamed watching the news. That she hadn’t known there were mothers under a sky she had seen only in intervals and seconds. That had still been a lumped-together shame. Today it was one person. The one her model called counterpart instead of a name. War always hid people behind words. Collateral damage. Target. Asset.

Six hours later the model would close the window and write: no realization. weight 0.83 → something. And next time it would speak more carefully. The model learns. Who pays for the learning wasn’t in the model’s loss function.

Priya Narayan had said she would start sending the tags next week. Then Yujin could block predictions coming from analytic products. She had said she could build it in a day.

She built it that night. Without waiting for next week. With no tags yet, she filtered by words. If the input contained SOURCE, HANDLER, ASSET, the model would read but put out no table. A coarse net. Things would leak through. Still, from tonight her model would not send out, as a number, whether one person would answer or not. One small net had been strung in front of one person’s life.

filter rev. 1 applied. operator Y.K. reason: model not validated for human-subject prediction.

Writing that in the reason box, she knew it was true. This model was built to read the rhythm of radars. It wasn’t built to guess a person’s heart. No one had validated that. Not even she.

The model learns. When it’s wrong, next time it speaks more carefully. Who pays for the learning wasn’t in the loss function.

Langley, Virginia — CIA Iran Operations Center
May 14, 2026 (Thu.) · 9:00 a.m.–3:00 p.m. EDT — Ceasefire Day 37
Scene 3 / 5

At nine in the morning a memo came down from seven. The day the machine was wrong, seven suspected the person.

Reassessment of source ZARRIN status. No response to 05-14 0230Z identification signal (127 sec, exact authentication value). Assessment: (a) source detained or control lost, (b) source turned. In either case, channel reliability downgraded. Channel to cease all contact attempts with source and stand by. Re-verification of past reporting related to source to begin. — C.R.

Leila Shirazi read it standing up. Exactly what the chief had said. Caught, or turned. And the last line. Re-verification of past reporting. That room was pulling out again the possibility that ZARRIN had been bait from the start. The question she and the chief had closed a month and a half ago. When the machine is wrong, the person gets suspected. That was this system’s arithmetic.

In her wallet was a paper. He is alive, and sitting in his own chair. That was eight days ago. And she couldn’t take it out.

A road that isn’t paper. The chief’s assignment.

She thought about it all night. This building accepted only one kind of evidence. What was inside the system. What had been collected. What had a number. Then the fact that he was alive and sitting in his own chair had to be inside the system somewhere already. Something he did every day that got caught in this side’s ear. The same ear had put him in danger. The same ear could save him.

She went to Sam Farrell’s desk.

“Sam. What did you say Persian Code has been listening to since 2019? The rhythm that country’s air defense net breathes in.”

“Right. Command communications. Sector reports. Scheduled checks.”

“Sector reports. What every air defense sector sends up to higher command in the morning. What happened overnight.”

“Every day between 06:10 and 06:30. In a fixed format.”

“Can you see this morning’s? The sector east of Tehran. After six their time.”

Sam looked at her. He didn’t ask what she was looking for. He typed. It was within his access. Raw collection. What it was before the machine ate it. The kind of thing you have to know Persian to read directly.

Lines came up on the screen. Not a transcription of voice but the full text of a form. Numbers and abbreviations.

Leila read it. In her own language.

Eastern Sector 3. 05:30–06:30. 1 unidentified track. Civilian code response 127 sec. 06:00:00–06:02:07. No engagement. Reason unknown. Reported by: duty second lieutenant Jahangiri. Confirmed: sector commander Colonel Sepahvand.

She looked at the last line a long time. A person’s name was in the last box of the form.

Confirmed. Sector commander. His name. This morning. At twelve past six. He sat in his own chair, read what the second lieutenant wrote, and confirmed it in his own name. No engagement. Reason unknown.

Reason unknown. He knew the reason. But he wrote it that way. As if he didn’t know. And only she could read those words. It was a report sent up to his command, and at the same time — though he couldn’t know it — a second acknowledgment that reached her. I am here. I know what that is. I am saying nothing.

“Sam. Pull this for me with a number on it. The original text and the collection time.”

She wrote it in an hour.

Channel opinion HC-02. On the source’s non-response of 05-14.

1. The source has not been detained. Basis: the Eastern Sector 3 morning report collected at 05-14 0242Z (Tehran 06:12) includes the sector commander’s confirming signature in normal format. No indication of change of commander or acting signature. Format, timing and phrasing match reports from the same sector on 4.28 and earlier.

2. The source observed the track, did not engage, and reported it as “reason unknown.” A turned source would have an incentive to report this signal upward as a contact attempt. He did not.

3. Both (a) and (b) are therefore weakly supported. The remaining explanation is HC-01 item 3. Since the wrong authentication value of 4.28, the source distrusts this entire format of signal, read the reappearance of the correct value as “a third party has corrected the value,” and chose silence. This is the correct behavior of a trained source.

4. Recommendation: re-verification of past reporting unnecessary. The source is intact. This format (top of hour · airborne · 127) is spent and should be retired. Wait for the source to initiate the next contact.

Before submitting she reread item 3. The note wasn’t in it. But what the note had done was written there whole. Why he had stayed silent. She wrote it as though she had inferred it from collection, not learned it from a paper. As inference, too, it was right. Knowing the truth twice. Once by hand, once by the rules.

At three an answer came. This time not three lines. One line.

HC-02 adopted. Re-verification halted. You were right, and it said 0.83. For the record. — C.R.

Leila read that line several times. That woman doesn’t say things that are wrong. And that woman was also someone who wrote that she was wrong when she was wrong. It was the first good thing Leila had learned about that room. Not an enemy, but someone with a different arithmetic.

Sam read it from behind her and whistled.

“You won. For real this time.”

“A person beat the machine once. Once.”

“Once is a lot.”

She didn’t smile. It wasn’t she who had won. It was the man up on the mountain who had had “reason unknown” written down. With his silence he had made her prediction come true. She hadn’t predicted his silence; the two of them had arranged it in advance with one sheet of paper. The machine couldn’t read that paper. So it was wrong.

It wasn’t a fair contest. She wasn’t the least bit sorry. The only place a person could win was where the machine couldn’t read.

Knowing the truth twice. Once by hand, once by the rules. She wrote what she learned from a paper as though she had inferred it from collection.

Scene 4 / 5

Sarah Callahan had lost weight in three months. War got written on a reporter’s face too.

Lieutenant Kim Junho had first seen her in a shelter in early March. She had been a reporter just off a carrier, and he a lieutenant halfway through a letter. The two of them met at this teahouse now and then. She asked and he answered only what he could. Between a reporter and a liaison officer, that was about right.

On May 14 the UK Maritime Trade Operations (UKMTO) said unidentified people had boarded a vessel without authorization 38 nautical miles off Fujairah and taken it toward Iran. The ship was later identified as the Honduran-flagged Huichuan, which had been used as a floating armory storing weapons for private security companies. On May 15 the Israel–Lebanon ceasefire was extended by another 45 days. The same day, an Israeli airstrike killed at least six people, including three paramedics, in Haruf in southern Lebanon.

“The day they extended the ceasefire, paramedics died,” Sarah said, turning her tea glass. “How do you put those two sentences in one story? The desk wants the first one as the headline.”

“And the second?”

“Fourth paragraph.”

Kim Junho thought of his own report. Where he had written that on the day the words great progress came out, no ship got through. He had put those two sentences on one line. A reporter couldn’t, it seemed. The headline carried power’s sentence, and death was pushed down to the fourth paragraph.

“Lieutenant. Tomorrow the Ford comes into Norfolk.”

“I heard. Three hundred and some days.”

“Three hundred twenty-six. It left last June. It was supposed to be six months.” She looked out the window. The sun was setting, and over the headquarters wall the antennas were red. “I got on that ship in February. Passing Gibraltar. A ship with four thousand five hundred people. I saw it when the laundry caught fire and six hundred people lost their bunks.”

“I read your story from the end of March. ‘We have never been told how this war ends.’”

Sarah’s hand stopped over her tea.

“The man who said that comes down the pier tomorrow.”

“A senior U.S. Navy officer.”

“That’s how I wrote it. He told me to write it that way. Not his name yet. Not yet, he said.” She laughed quietly. “That ‘yet’ has turned into nearly two months. I know what he’s going to go through tomorrow. The secretary’s coming. The band, the families. And after that someone will call him into a quiet room. Who talked to that reporter?”

“And if they ask?”

“I don’t name sources. That’s my job. But he…” She took a long time choosing the words. “He’s the kind of man who’ll speak before he’s asked. That’s what worries me. And that’s also why I ran what he said.”

Kim Junho drank his tea. It had gone cold. He thought of the captain whose face he didn’t know. A ship whose deployment had stretched from six months to eleven. Four thousand five hundred people who circled the edge of the war, caught fire, pulled back for repairs, and in the end went home without firing a shot. And one sentence that ship’s captain had said to a reporter. Even the highest man on the biggest ship was asking about the end.

“Ms. Callahan. I got a judgment wrong in April. I sent a correction in my report. In my own name.”

“I heard. Lieutenant Commander Henderson told me. He said it was the first sentence like that he’d seen in this building in three months.”

“When I wrote it I was scared. After I wrote it, whatever I wrote afterward was less scary.” He set down his glass. “He’ll be the same. A man who speaks before he’s asked feels lighter once he’s spoken. He’ll pay for it, though.”

Sarah looked at him a long time. He wasn’t the lieutenant of three months ago. She started to take out her notebook, then didn’t. This wasn’t a story.

“And your ships? Twenty-five.”

“One hundred thirty-six people,” he corrected her. “Still the same.”

A man who speaks before he’s asked feels lighter once he’s spoken. He’ll pay for it, though. The man who will pay comes down the pier tomorrow.

Scene 5 / 5 — Final

After three hundred twenty-six days the ship was made fast. The sound of chain winding onto the pier.

On May 16, Carrier Strike Group 12, with the aircraft carrier USS Gerald R. Ford as its flagship, returned to Naval Station Norfolk in Virginia. Defense Secretary Hegseth, the chief of naval operations and the master chief petty officer of the Navy welcomed the sailors on the pier. The Ford had sailed in June 2025.

Captain Marcus Webb looked down at the pier from the bridge wing. People. Thousands. Handwritten signs. Balloons. Babies seeing their fathers for the first time were in the front row. That was tradition. The fathers of children born while the ship was away come off first. This time there were more than sixty of them. Eleven months is a long time.

The band played. The secretary was in the VIP seats. Webb straightened the collar of his dress uniform. In twenty-five years of service, it was the motion he had made most.

The ship hadn’t fired a shot. He had never been able to decide whether he should be ashamed of that or proud. In February his planes had flown in the eastern Mediterranean. Then on March 12 the laundry caught fire, six hundred people lost their bunks, and the ship pulled back to Crete, to Split. For most of the days the war was hottest, his ship was under repair. Four thousand five hundred came home. Without losing one. He decided to hold on to that. The captains at Midway in 1942 held on to something else. He held on to what belonged to his own time.

After the ceremony, after the handshakes, after the families flooded onto the deck and his wife held him around the neck and didn’t let go for a long time — then it came.

His aide came over and said quietly that the fleet chief of staff wanted ten minutes in the pier office.

The office was small. One desk, two chairs, a chart of the harbor on the wall. The chief of staff was two classes ahead of him at the Academy. Not on first-name terms, but each knew the other’s reputation. The man who had called Split on March 31, talked for four minutes and hung up. He told him to sit, offered coffee, and sincerely commended the eleven-month deployment. Then he put a folder on the desk. He didn’t open it.

“Captain Webb. I said I’d ask when the ship got back. The AP story of March 31.”

“You don’t have to ask.”

The chief of staff’s hand stopped on the folder.

“It’s me. ‘A senior U.S. Navy officer.’ March 24 and 27, two calls. The reporter is Sarah Callahan. She was aboard my ship in February. What I said is exactly what ran in the story. Not one word added.”

The chief of staff said nothing for a long time. Outside the window, children were shouting. A celebration and an interrogation, with one wall between them.

“On March 31, I didn’t ask. And you didn’t tell me.”

“I’ve thought for a month and a half about whether I should have told you that day. Then, the ship had to go to sea two days later. There were four thousand five hundred people aboard, and I was that ship’s captain. If a captain is relieved just before sailing, the crew pays for it. So I took the reprieve you gave me. Only until we reached port. Today we reached it.”

“I said as long as I didn’t ask, you were the captain.”

“I held on to those words to get here. And I wanted to tell you before you asked. Not be a man who answered what he was asked.”

The chief of staff pushed the folder aside. He never opened it.

“Why did you do it? That’s not for the paperwork. I’m asking because I want to know.”

Webb had asked himself that question for three months. The answer had changed several times. The answer he had today was the shortest.

“On March 21 the president said he was giving them forty-eight hours. That he’d wipe out the power plants. I called in my legal officer and asked him. If that order comes, what am I supposed to do? He couldn’t answer. For twenty-five years I’ve told my people: if there’s something you don’t know, ask. Better to ask and get chewed out than die without asking. But I had nowhere to ask. Nowhere in the chain of command was there a box to ask ‘How does this war end?’ So I asked where there was a box. In a newspaper.”

“That isn’t an officer’s job.”

“No, sir. I know.”

“There won’t be a next assignment for you. Your name will come off the flag selection list. I’ll do what I can to keep them from touching your pension.”

“Thank you, sir.”

The chief of staff stood and held out his hand. Webb took it. A firm hand.

“Captain Webb. I’ll put down that you spoke before I asked. It won’t change anything. But I’ll put it down.”

When he stepped out of the office the sunlight stabbed his eyes. The pier was still full of people. His twelve-year-old daughter ran through the crowd looking for him. She had grown a hand’s span in eleven months. He started to lift her up and realized she was too big now. So he just held her. What the war had taken from him and what it had given back were in the circle of one arm.

“Dad, you’re not leaving again?”

“Not leaving.”

“Really? What about the next ship?”

“There’s no next ship.”

His daughter didn’t understand everything that meant. She heard it as good news. Over her head Webb looked up at the ship. A hundred thousand tons. Gray. His for three hundred twenty-six days. He saluted it once. No one saw.

He felt light. He had paid, and he felt light. He thought that was strange, and knew it wasn’t. The weight of what you can’t say is heavier than the price of what you say.

“Nowhere in the chain of command was there a box to ask ‘How does this war end?’ So I asked where there was a box.”

Background Analysis
Three Hundred Twenty-Six Days — The Ford Comes Home

The aircraft carrier USS Gerald R. Ford left Norfolk in June 2025 and returned on May 16, 2026. Three hundred twenty-six days. A typical U.S. Navy carrier deployment runs six to seven months. The Ford passed through the Strait of Gibraltar into the Mediterranean on February 20, arrived off Israel on February 27, and went through the Suez Canal into the Red Sea on March 5.

On March 12 a fire broke out in the ship’s laundry area, and about 600 crew lost their berthing. The ship was repaired at Souda Bay on Crete and at Split in Croatia. On March 7 the United States sent a third carrier, USS George H.W. Bush, to the Middle East, and after the blockade began, operations in the Gulf and Arabian Sea were carried by the Bush, USS Abraham Lincoln and the amphibious assault ship USS Tripoli. On May 16, the defense secretary and the chief of naval operations came to the pier at Norfolk.

That same week the war wasn’t ending. On May 14 off Fujairah, the Huichuan, used as a floating armory, was seized and taken toward Iran; on May 15 the Israel–Lebanon ceasefire was extended by 45 days. On May 17 a drone would fall on the edge of the UAE’s Barakah nuclear plant and start a fire. Captain Marcus Webb and his confession, the signal over Tehran on May 14 and the documents at Langley are all the author’s invention.
