Langley, Virginia — CIA Iran Operations Center
April 21, 2026 (Tue.) · Afternoon EDT — Ceasefire Day 14 · War Day 53
Scene 1 / 5

The day the two-week deadline ran out. The deadline vanished.

On April 21 Iran did not send a delegation to the second round of talks in Islamabad. The same day, President Trump said that at the request of the mediator, Pakistan, he would extend the ceasefire to give Iran time to put forward a proposal. No end date was set. He said Iran had violated the ceasefire “numerous times,” kept the naval blockade in place, and told the military to be ready to resume combat.

When the news came up on the wall screen of the operations center, no one stood. On the night of April 7 someone had pounded a desk. Today the keyboards went quiet a moment and started again. A ceasefire with no day it ends. It was another name for a war that does not end. The 1953 armistice on the Korean Peninsula had not written down an end date either. For more than seventy years.

Leila Shirazi had a blank document open. A report for the branch chief. She had gotten as far as the title. On the entry of PC-ASSESS output into policy channels.

The cursor blinked. She could not write the first sentence.

If you write it, it gets read. That was what she had learned yesterday. Every document she saved in this building went through one intake account. PC-LNO-03. What that account ate went to a nameless “analysis system.” The machine read it, wrote on paper what she would do next, and slipped it into the bundle going to the President’s desk. If she wrote a report about the machine, the machine would read the report. And know what she would do after that.

For sixteen years her job had been writing. Writing what she saw, writing that she didn’t know what she didn’t know, writing that wrong things were wrong. Writing was the only power she had in this building. Now that power was making her someone who got read. If she wrote, she was watched. If she didn’t, she didn’t exist.

She closed the document. Don’t save. That would be in the record too. An analyst who opened a blank document with only a title and closed it. The machine would read something in that too.

She took a notebook out of her drawer. A paper notebook. The one she used for Persian words. Old words from her aunt’s letters. She opened it from the back.

She wrote by hand. In Persian. In this building, two people besides her could read her handwriting.

One. The machine eats my documents. Two. The machine writes down what I will do before I do. Three. What it writes goes upstairs. Four. So everything I say to ZARRIN passes through the machine. Five. ZARRIN does not know that.

On the fifth line the pen stopped.

The man on the mountain had turned on a light. As long as his brother’s service number. And he had gotten an answer that someone saw it. He believed the answer came from a person. He would take his next step on that belief. She had asked him: halt, who goes there, show your light. Now it was his turn to ask the same. He didn’t even know he needed to.

She wrote the sixth line.

Six. He has to be told. By a road the machine cannot read.

Was there such a road? The Quetta line was closed. The drone was a road the machine had thought of first. Anything that went by radio got caught in somebody’s collection net, and everything caught in a collection net flowed into that machine.

One thing was left. Something not radio. Paper. And a person who carries the paper on foot. The way to get around the newest machine was the oldest road.

She went to the chief’s office. With the notebook. The chief was watching the ceasefire extension on his screen and turned his head.

“The report?”

“I’m not writing it.”

The chief’s eyebrows went up. She opened the notebook and held it out. The chief read Persian. Slow but never wrong, learned twenty-some years ago in some city that was not Tehran. It took him a minute to read six lines.

“You’re telling me you won’t use this building’s reporting system.”

“On this matter only. If I write it, it reads it. If you write it too.”

“What isn’t written doesn’t exist. In this building.”

“That’s why it can’t read it.”

The chief closed the notebook. He looked out the window a long time. The trees in the parking lot had leafed out. When the war began they had been bare.

“When I started this job it was all paper. The day ended with the smell of burning paper,” he said. “So we’re going back. To paper.”

A woman whose only power was writing learned that what she wrote got read. From that day her most important sentences were saved nowhere.

Scene 2 / 5

On the first morning of the ceasefire with no end, a new order had come down to the command post.

Maintain current alert posture. The ceasefire extension is a unilateral announcement by the enemy. Two lines. Colonel Darius Sepahvand read it and signed. The same posture for fifty-four days. For fifty-four days the same men had sat in the same chairs watching the same screens. What had changed was the sky. Nothing came up on the screens. Two weeks now. Power would not say the word ceasefire with its own mouth to the end.

Captain Farhan Moradi was alone in the signals room. The lieutenant’s seat was empty. Removed from communications duties. Sohrab was counting the inventory in a storeroom in the barracks building. This time it was really counting.

Darius shut the door. The captain stood.

“Sit.”

“I’ll stand.” Farhan’s voice was lower than usual. “Colonel. I heard what you said at the gate three days ago. That you told me to correct the checklist.”

“I said that.”

“There was no such order.”

“No, there wasn’t.”

The captain took a breath. For more than three weeks the two had not talked about this. The counter’s number. Four-seventeen becoming four-nineteen. The captain seeing it and writing four-seventeen. The colonel signing that checklist. Each knew what the other knew, and neither had ever said it out loud. As long as they didn’t say it, they were both men who knew nothing.

“Why did you take what was mine?”

“It wasn’t yours. It was mine from the start.”

“I wrote it. With my own hand. I knew what I saw and I wrote it.” For the first time Farhan looked straight at him. “I didn’t ask why the number went up by two. Deciding not to ask was my decision too. You can’t take that from me as well.”

Darius looked at the thirty-seven-year-old signals officer. Professor Azad’s nephew. The only man at this post he could talk to about books.

“I won’t ask why you didn’t ask.”

“I’ll tell you. I don’t know what you did. I don’t want to know. But on the night of April 7 I saw the net shut itself off with my own eyes. That wasn’t you. At that minute you were standing next to me. So there’s a hand touching this country’s net besides yours. That hand scares me more.”

“Me too.”

That was all the two said about it. The captain opened the desk drawer and took out a folded paper. A copy of what he had written by hand on the night of April 7. 03:42:00. 03:44:07. Originator field blank.

“The original is somewhere else. I won’t tell you where. If anything happens to me, it goes to my uncle.”

“To the professor?”

“My uncle has kept records his whole life. What to do with it, he’ll decide.”

Darius didn’t take the paper. The captain put it back.

Leaving the command post, the colonel thought. He had believed he was alone in this building. He decided alone, turned on the light alone, wrote five sentences alone. But beside him for three weeks one man had carried the same weight, without a word. That silence was not betrayal. It was not complicity either. It was something with no name. In this country that was how people protected each other. By not asking. Forty-seven years since the revolution had taught them how.

As long as they didn’t say it, they were both men who knew nothing. In some countries that is the only way one person can protect another.

Bahrain — Mina Salman Port, the coalition liaison cell at the headquarters of the U.S. Fifth Fleet
April 22, 2026 (Wed.) · Afternoon (Bahrain local time) — Ceasefire Day 15
Scene 3 / 5

Captain Kim Junho’s sentence did not hold up three days.

On April 22 IRGC fast boats attacked three container ships in the Strait of Hormuz. The Epaminondas and the MSC Francesca were seized and towed to the Iranian coast. Iran cited safety violations. The third ship, the Euphoria, reported that its bridge had been badly damaged by the boats’ gunfire and rockets.

They don’t want to widen the fight. He had said that Sunday evening. Major Henderson had written it down, and it had probably gone up in Monday morning’s report under somebody’s name.

On the screen was a photo of the container ship with its bridge smashed. Window frames with all the glass gone. Scorched white steel. That ship was not bound for an Iranian port. It was not an American warship. It was just a ship passing through.

Major Mark Henderson came and stood beside him. He didn’t tease. That was harder to bear.

“You weren’t wrong,” the major said. “You were half right. They didn’t hit across the Gulf.”

“They widened it on the sea.”

“We grabbed their ship. So they grab ships. One for one. Rockets for a gun.”

“That ship wasn’t ours. It wasn’t American either.”

“That’s a blockade, Captain. We close their ports, they close the strait. Whatever’s caught in between is a hostage, whatever flag it flies.”

Kim Junho opened his notebook. He did not strike through Sunday’s line. Under it he wrote something new.

4.22 Correction. Iran does not widen on land but widens at sea. Looking only at the direction of the drones was a mistake. Should have looked at the direction of the fast boats too. The ceasefire is only in the sky. Not on the water.

Then he took out the list. Twenty-six lines. He knew the ship names by heart now. Some ships he could tell apart by the captain’s voice. The captain who coughed a lot. The captain who always ended a text with two periods. The captain whose daughter was taking the college entrance exam.

None of them was hurt today. He could not tell anyone the same would be true tomorrow. While the big countries closed each other’s ports, the small country’s ships held their breath in between.

In the evening he added a line at the end of his report to the Joint Chiefs. He didn’t delete it. He sent it.

This liaison officer’s 4.19 judgment (Iran’s intent to avoid escalation) was wrong as regards the sea. Correction made.

Only after he sent it did he think about what that line would become in his personnel file. And he thought about his father. A man who had spent thirty years at the steelworks in Pohang. He had once written a wrong temperature for the molten iron in the work log. That night he went to the foreman and told him, he said. Being wrong isn’t the shame, Junho. Leaving the wrong thing in the log is the shame. The next man reads that log.

A wrong judgment is not erased. It is corrected in writing. The next person reads that paper.

Seoul, South Korea, Jingwan-dong, Eunpyeong-gu — Kang Yujin’s home
April 22, 2026 (Wed.) · 9:00 p.m. (Korea time) — Ceasefire Day 15
Scene 4 / 5

The business card was being used as a bookmark.

Kang Yujin found it between the pages of a conference program. White paper, a three-syllable name and one number. No affiliation. At the after-party the man had introduced himself as National Intelligence Service, cyber side. About forty. He didn’t drink. He was one of the few who listened to her whole talk, and he asked only one question. Can your model say it doesn’t know what it doesn’t know? She had thought it was a good question.

Eleven days ago she had started dialing this number and stopped. Then she hadn’t known what she meant to report. Now she knew. That made it harder.

What would she say? A model I made is feeding on classified American material. Under my signature. I didn’t know for three months. The model sent out in advance the time Iran’s air defense net would go dark, and at that time the net went dark. I don’t know who did it. Park says there is no leaker.

Every sentence tied her up. A setup where the one who reports becomes the first defendant. She was standing inside it.

She picked up the phone. She dialed the number. All the way this time.

It rang twice.

“Yes.”

“Hello, this is Kang Yujin from KAIA Lab. You gave me your card at the conference last month —”

“I remember, Doctor.” The voice was the same as that day. Low, unhurried. “What is it, this late?”

“I have something to tell you about a model I run. In the logs there’s something I can’t explain —”

“Doctor.”

He cut her off. It wasn’t rude. It was like catching the arm of someone about to miss a step on the stairs.

“Better not to talk about that over the phone. Tomorrow at ten a.m., the third-floor reading room of the Eunpyeong public library is quiet. Leave your phone at home. Your laptop too.”

“…Should I bring the copy of the logs?”

“Don’t bring anything. What’s in your head is enough.”

The call ended.

She sat holding the phone. Call time, forty-one seconds.

He hadn’t asked. What model? What logs? How urgent? The first things anyone taking a report asks. He asked none of them. And — no, she was the one who said copy. Copy of the logs. He wasn’t surprised by it. That a researcher had a copy of closed-network logs at home. That alone was grounds for discipline.

A man who doesn’t ask is one of two things. Not interested, or already knows.

She went to the window. The night in Jingwan-dong was quiet. Toward Bukhansan the unlit ridge was black. A few windows were lit in the building across the way. She could not know who was looking at this window. Becoming someone who thought that way had happened in eleven days. Until a generation ago this country had lived thinking that way. She had been taught that era was over.

On the paper on her desk she looked again at the line she had written the day before yesterday. Someone with hands is using the model’s predictions as an order sheet.

Under it she wrote one more line.

I just called one pair of hands without knowing whose hands they are.

A man who doesn’t ask is one of two things. Not interested, or already knows.

April 22, 2026 (Wed.) · 6:00 p.m. EDT — Ceasefire Day 15
Scene 5 / 5 — Final

In one day the chief had found a road.

“Islamabad,” he said. After closing the door and putting his phone in a drawer. “The Pakistani you met. Assistant Secretary Mehmood.”

Leila Shirazi pictured the face. The small meeting room at the Foreign Ministry. The hand that gave her one folded sheet. A man who had carried other people’s paper for thirty years.

“Does he reach Tehran?”

“He doesn’t. His people do. Pakistan is the only mailman going between the two capitals right now. Carries Washington’s words to Tehran and brings Tehran’s back. On paper. By hand. You saw it yourself, those people still use printers.”

“The machine’s sentence came out of that printer.”

“Right. So that road isn’t safe either. But a man who puts a handwritten note in his pocket and crosses a border — that it can’t read. Not yet.”

Not yet. Leila did not let that go by.

“There are conditions,” the chief said. “One, this is not an operation. An operation needs approval, approval is a record, and records get eaten. This is something like a letter you send your aunt. I know nothing about it.”

“Understood.”

“Two, one line only. Any longer and the man carrying it is in danger. Three.” He paused. “The other side has to have a reason to believe the note. A man who just learned the light didn’t come from a person — how does he believe one slip of paper did?”

That was the hardest question. Leila opened her notebook. Last night she had sat with that question until dawn.

She couldn’t use one-twenty-seven. That number belonged to the machine now too. So did the coordinates, and six on the hour. Everything that had passed between him and her, the machine had read. There had to be something the machine hadn’t read. Something never once written in a document.

There was one thing.

Before dawn on March 28 she had chosen the first question to send him. Halt. Who goes there? Show your light. That went into a cable and the machine ate it. But why she chose those words she had written nowhere. They came from the opening of Hamlet. What the sentry on the battlements calls out to footsteps coming at him in the dark. The Persian translation her father read aloud in his study. A book from Tehran with its cover coming off.

And in the play the challenge has an answer. The next line. What the man in the dark says back to the sentry.

Only someone who has read the book knows that answer. The machine knows Hamlet too. But it does not know the question came from Hamlet. Because she never wrote it.

Whether the colonel on the mountain would recognize it, she couldn’t know. Whether a man who had been a soldier thirty-one years had read Shakespeare. But she remembered the sentences in the cables he sent. They were not a soldier’s sentences. They were the sentences of a man who read books.

She tore a page from the notebook. A slip half the size of her palm. She wrote one line in Persian.

The one who asked was a person, but the one who answered was not only a person. Turn nothing on at the top of the hour. — Long live the king.

The chief pointed at the last words.

“And this?”

“The answer to the sentry’s challenge. In the play. When he asks who’s there, the other one says ‘Long live the king.’ It was the password, that night.”

“You want to send an Iranian soldier ‘Long live the king.’ To the country that threw out its king in 1979.”

“That’s why the machine won’t pick this answer,” she said. “Because it’s unlikely. Only people say what is unlikely.”

The chief looked at the slip a long time. Then he folded it and put it in his inside pocket.

“Their attaché goes home Friday. I’ll buy him lunch.”

Leaving the office, Leila thought about what she had just done. For the first time in sixteen years she had done something that left no trace anywhere. Whether the note would get there, whether it would be read if it did, whether it would be believed if read, she might never know. A report comes back with a receipt. A note doesn’t.

That was the weight words between people always had. You send them and wait, not knowing. For thousands of years messengers had crossed mountains that way.

Only people say what is unlikely. The road the machine could not read was inside a sentence a machine would never choose.

Background Analysis
A Ceasefire With No End Date — Neither Ending Nor Beginning

The ceasefire declared on the night of April 7 (U.S. Eastern) was for two weeks. The first round of talks in Islamabad on April 11–12 broke down after twenty-one hours, and on April 13 the United States began a naval blockade stopping ships going in and out of Iran’s ports. On April 21, the day the second round was set, Iran sent no delegation. The same day, President Trump accepted Pakistan’s request and extended the ceasefire with no end date.

What was extended was the ceasefire in the sky. The sea was different. On April 19 a U.S. destroyer fired on the Iranian cargo ship Touska and seized it, and on April 22 IRGC fast boats attacked three container ships in the Strait of Hormuz and seized two. On April 23 President Trump ordered the Navy to sink any Iranian boat laying mines in the strait. The same day the Israel–Lebanon ceasefire was extended three weeks.

This state of things went on for a long time after that. Through the agreement and lifting of the blockade in June, and the collapse of the ceasefire and the renewed blockade in July, the war passed through a time when it was hard to say either that it had ended or that it went on. The five scenes of this episode — the notebook and slip in Langley, the conversation in the Alborz, the correction report in Bahrain, the phone call in Seoul — are all the author’s invention.
