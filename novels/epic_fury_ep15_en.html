Alborz Mountains, Iran — Air Defense Force command post, operations room
April 1, 2026 (Wed.) · 5:58 a.m. Tehran time — War Day 33
Scene 1 / 6

Five fifty-eight in the morning. Colonel Darius Sepahvand had come down to the operations room for no reason.

Not for no reason. He could not sleep. If he sat in his office he could see the copy of his statement in the drawer. Five sentences. In the operations room there were at least screens. Screens asked nothing.

The night-shift detection officer swallowed a yawn. On the screens were the usual things. Six tracks coming in from the west. Two from the south. Dots without name tags. They did not wear name tags in this sky. They had no reason to.

At five fifty-nine a dot came into the corner of the eastern screen. Slow. High. A drone’s pace. Over the road to Damavand. He knew that spot. It was right above the ridgeline where two days ago he had turned on the decoy with his own hands.

His heart gave one big beat. Had counterintelligence found that spot? No. That thing was theirs. Then a bombing? An empty ridgeline?

On the hour.

Letters came up beside the dot.

“Colonel.” The sleep went out of the detection officer’s voice. “Eastern track, its transponder’s on. A code’s coming up. This is… it’s a civilian code.”

Darius walked to the screen. One step. Two. He had started counting in his head the moment he saw the letters. Ten. Twenty. The drone did not run. It did not change altitude. It circled one spot slowly. In the middle of the enemy’s sky, wearing a name tag. With its hand up, saying here I am.

“Should I report it for intercept? At that altitude Battery 3 could—”

“Wait.”

Sixty. Seventy. He knew his pulse was faster than the count. He slowed his breathing to match the numbers. Ninety. A hundred.

At a hundred twenty-seven the letters disappeared.

The dot was a nameless dot again. And it slipped slowly away to the east, the way it had come.

“It’s off,” the detection officer said. “What was that? A malfunction?”

Darius did not take his eyes off the screen. The last three digits of his brother’s service number. There were two people in the world who knew that number. His dead mother and him. Now there were three. The person on the other side of the mountain who had used the old army’s challenge. The number of a boy who never came back from the front in the 1980s had called his name, forty years later, in the enemy’s sky.

We saw. That was what they were saying. Without a single letter.

“A malfunction, probably,” he said. “Log it. Enemy drone transponder malfunction, presumed, about a hundred twenty seconds. No intercept opportunity.”

The detection officer wrote it down. That was a lie too. He told lies several times a day now. This lie tasted different from yesterday’s. Yesterday’s five sentences were a lie that kept a man underground. Today’s one line was a lie that protected the fact that someone had seen him. The lie was the same. What it protected was different.

He left the operations room. In the hallway he put a hand on the wall once. For the first time in thirty-one years—no, thirty-one years and three days—he was not alone. It did not save him. Sohrab had been transferred to Tehran this morning. But the price had not fallen into empty air. Someone had received it.

One dot wearing a name tag for one hundred twenty-seven seconds in the enemy’s sky. It was the shortest letter in the world—received.

Langley, Virginia — CIA Iran Operations Center
April 1, 2026 (Wed.) · 11:40 a.m. EDT — War Day 33
Scene 2 / 6

The drone came back.

Leila Shirazi had gotten the one line at eleven last night. Mission complete. Emission 127 s. Presumed detected. No intercept attempt. Airframe returned. No intercept attempt. She looked at those words a long time. A drone at that altitude had circled for two minutes wearing a name tag and no one had fired. Someone had said not to fire. That was all the reply she could get. It was enough.

This morning’s work was something else. The branch chief put two sheets of paper on her desk.

“Upstairs wants one page within the hour. Which of these two is lying.”

On April 1 President Trump claimed that Iran’s president had asked the United States for a ceasefire. He wrote that until the strait was open the United States was blasting Iran “back to the Stone Ages,” as they say. The Iranian government denied the claim as “false and baseless.” The same day President Masoud Pezeshkian released an open letter to the American people. He wrote that in modern history Iran had never chosen the path of aggression, expansion, colonialism, or domination, that Iranians bore no enmity toward any nation, including the American people, and he argued that the United States was acting as Israel’s proxy.

One sheet was Washington’s post. One was Tehran’s letter. Leila set them side by side.

Sixteen years as an analyst. What she had learned was that the question of who is lying is usually the wrong question. Lies were rare. What was common was translation.

Someone in Tehran would have passed word by way of Islamabad. That they could talk about conditions for stopping the fighting. In Persian it was an offer. Passing through Pakistan’s Urdu it became an intention. By the time it reached Washington’s English it was a request. And in the president’s post the request became a cousin of begging. One sentence, crossing three languages, had gone down a little further on its knees with each one. In the summer of 1988 too, this country’s leader called the ceasefire a poisoned chalice. When accepting sounds like surrender, no one lifts the cup.

Tehran could not accept that last word. So it denied everything. False and baseless. That was not a lie either. They had never requested anything. They had only offered.

She wrote.

Assessment: neither side likely engaged in deliberate falsehood. Probable that conditions were actually sounded out via an indirect channel. The phrase “ceasefire request” judged to have been rendered more strongly than the sender intended. Tehran’s blanket denial is not a denial of the contact itself but of that phrasing. Warning: if the other side’s soundings are publicly labeled a “request,” the sounder’s domestic standing collapses and the next sounding does not come.

She had written the last sentence six days ago too. When the word was “begging.” Nothing had happened then either. She knew now where her warnings went. To where things are received. Received was the quietest grave in this building.

“Shirazi,” the branch chief said after reading the page. “Upstairs wants one word. They’re lying, or we are.”

“Neither.”

“That answer is no use to anybody.”

“Sometimes the answer that’s no use is the right answer,” she said. “You taught me that.”

The branch chief did not smile. He took the paper and went out. In the doorway, without turning around, he said:

“Last night. Good work. Call the favor I owed the operations side paid.”

The most common lie in war is not made-up words. It is words that got heavier in the carrying. An offer becomes a request, and a request becomes begging. In between, no one told a lie.

Tehran — Imam Khomeini Hospital, makeshift classroom in the basement shelter
April 1, 2026 (Wed.) · 2 p.m. Tehran time — War Day 33
Scene 3 / 6

The old man’s radio next to him was talking about Taleghani Street.

On April 1 the old American embassy building in Tehran was reported damaged in a U.S. strike. It is the building seized in the 1979 revolution and used since then as a museum. The nearby St. Nicholas Orthodox Cathedral was also damaged. The same day the Iranian outlet Nour News reported that the home of former foreign minister Kamal Kharrazi had been hit in a strike, badly wounding Kharrazi and killing his wife.

Professor Azad Moradi put down his chalk. That wall. He remembered the day he stood in front of that wall.

It was November 1979. He was twenty-six, a young lecturer at the university. He watched from across the street as the students went over the wall. One of them had been in his class. On top of the wall that student recognized him and waved. Come on, Professor. He did not go. Not because he was against it. Even then he had the habit of stepping back when a crowd ran in one direction. That habit had made him a coward and a survivor for the rest of his life. Is an intellectual’s place on top of the wall, or across the street? For forty-seven years he had not been able to answer that question.

For forty-seven years that building had been at the center of the story this country told itself. The den of spies. That was how the textbooks put it. Children went on field trips. Every year new murals were painted on the wall. And today an American bomb had hit the American embassy.

He laughed without a sound. It was funny only to a philosopher. For forty-seven years the two countries had told different stories about that building. On one side, the 444 days of the hostages. On the other, the house where the coup of 1953 was plotted. Both were facts. Each called the other’s fact a lie. The memories of two peoples had stood facing each other on one wall for forty-seven years.

Nariman tugged at his sleeve.

“Grandpa. The radio keeps saying lie. Who lied?”

“Two countries are each saying the other one is lying.”

“Then one of them is a liar.”

“Not necessarily.” He crouched down next to the child. His knees made a sound. “When you were carrying words between Hossein and Amir, say Hossein said, ‘If he says sorry first, I’ll think about it.’ If you go to Amir and say, ‘Hossein wants to make up,’ is that a lie?”

The child thought about it seriously.

“A little.”

“A little. But what if Amir tells the whole class, ‘Hossein begged me’?”

“That’s a lot. Then Hossein gets mad and says none of it’s true.”

“That’s what happened on the radio today.”

The child nodded. A seven-year-old had learned diplomacy in three days, and the shape of its failure in two. Getting up, Azad thought: what he had taught in this basement was more useful than fifty years of teaching at the university.

Then the other news on the radio reached his ears, late. The former foreign minister’s wife. He had seen her once. Long ago, at some academy dinner. A quiet person. While her husband talked she smoothed the wrinkles in the tablecloth with her fingers. He remembered those fingers. He could not remember her name.

If there is only one liar, the world is easy. What is hard is when both sides are telling the truth and the truths cannot meet.

Split, Croatia — the captain’s cabin of the U.S. aircraft carrier USS Gerald R. Ford (CVN-78), in port
April 1, 2026 (Wed.) · 4 p.m. Croatia time — War Day 33
Scene 4 / 6

Yesterday’s call had been four minutes.

For more than thirty hours Captain Marcus Webb had been going back over those four minutes. The chief of staff at Norfolk was two classes ahead of him at the Academy. They were not on a first-name basis, but they knew each other’s reputations.

The chief of staff had said: I read the story. I’m not going to ask who that officer is. As long as I don’t ask, you’re the captain of that ship. Take her out the day after tomorrow. When she comes back, I’ll ask then.

It was mercy. It was also a reprieve. He understood both.

Not asking meant not making him lie. Sparing him from having to say no, sir. Twenty-five years in the Navy. The biggest favor he had ever received was not being asked a question. That was how the system protected a man, and how it tied him down.

The executive officer came in with tomorrow’s sailing plan. The repairs were done. Of the six hundred bunks, four hundred had been restored. The other two hundred sailors were still sleeping on the hangar deck.

“Captain. That story’s going around the crew.”

“What are they saying?”

The executive officer hesitated. He was an honest man, and an honest man takes longer to deliver bad news than good. But his face said he did not know himself which this was.

“One of the sailors sleeping on the hangar deck cut the story out and taped it to the pipe over his cot. The chief told him to take it down, and he didn’t.”

Webb said nothing for a long while.

“Don’t make him take it down.”

“Aye, sir.”

After the executive officer left he looked at the harbor through the porthole. Dalmatian afternoon light. Tourists at the end of the pier were taking pictures of this ship. To them this ship was a sight to see. Tomorrow this ship would go back to sea. What for, he still could not tell his sailors. But at least one sailor had read on paper that the captain did not know either. That sailor had not taken the paper down.

Tonight in Washington the president was giving a speech, they said. He decided to listen. For the first time, with the ears of a man waiting for an answer.

They say that when a commander says “I don’t know,” the unit falls apart. Sometimes it doesn’t. The soldiers already know the commander doesn’t know. What they can’t stand is pretending to know.

Bahrain — shelter and quarters near Mina Salman Port
April 2, 2026 (Thu.) · 4 a.m. Bahrain time / 9 p.m. April 1 in Washington — before dawn, War Day 34
Scene 5 / 6

Eleven people were gathered in front of the television in the quarters lounge.

It was four in the morning. Four American officers. One British liaison officer. Two Bahraini interpreters. Three reporters. And Lieutenant Kim Junho. Nobody sat down. On the screen the president was talking.

On the night of April 1, in an address to the nation, President Trump said the war’s goals were “nearing completion.” Claiming the United States had destroyed Iran’s navy and air force and neutralized its ballistic missile and nuclear programs, he said strikes would continue “extremely hard over the next two to three weeks.” If Iran did not reopen the Strait of Hormuz and undo the disruption to world energy markets, he said, “we are going to bring them back to the Stone Ages, where they belong.” He also mentioned concern about rising gasoline prices at home.

Two to three weeks. Kim Junho started calculating from those words. Four ships with less than three days of drinking water. Since yesterday, five. Two to three weeks was time for those ships to die seven times over. Power talked in weeks. Seafarers lived in days.

Next to him Lieutenant Commander Mark Henderson spoke low.

“Nearing completion. But two or three more weeks. And the Stone Age.” He shook his head. “One speech with the end, keep going, and a threat all in it. What’s Tehran supposed to understand from that?”

“All three, isn’t it?” Kim Junho said. “We want to end it. If you don’t let us end it, we keep going. If we keep going, we break everything.”

“You think that’ll work?”

“I don’t know. But on the side hearing those words there’ll be someone who has to give a speech too. In front of his own people. If he opens the door after hearing he’ll be sent back to the Stone Age, what does he tell his people?”

Henderson did not answer.

When the speech was over people drifted off. Sarah Callahan stayed by the window. In three days her face had gotten thin. Since the story ran, she said, Navy public affairs had called the bureau three times. Calls asking about the source. Three times her desk had given the same answer. We don’t name sources.

“Is he all right?” Kim Junho asked.

“I don’t contact him. I can’t, not now.” She looked at the dark harbor out the window. “Lieutenant. You know what a reporter’s most afraid of? Not writing something wrong. Writing something right and nothing happening. And the only one who gets hurt is the person who told you.”

Kim Junho knew what she meant. He had sent five reports to Seoul. He did not know that one paragraph of the third report had been pasted into a first secretary’s document. All he knew was that not a single reply had come. The reports went up. Nothing came down.

“It won’t be nothing,” he said. “Somebody might have cut it out and taped it up somewhere, don’t you think?”

Sarah turned to look at him. For the first time in a while, she smiled a little.

Words about ending a war and words about widening it can go into the same speech. The listener picks out the part he is afraid of.

Tehran — Imam Khomeini Hospital, dialysis room
April 2, 2026 (Thu.) · 6:30 a.m. Tehran time — morning of War Day 34
Scene 6 / 6 — Final

Morning in the dialysis room began with the sound of the machines.

Six were running. Six people were lying down. Four hours a person. Three runs a day made eighteen people. In this hospital there were forty-two people who had to have dialysis to live. Maryam could do that division with her eyes closed now.

The television in the nurses’ station carried last night’s speech. The announcer’s voice shook. Whether it was anger or fear, you could not tell. The Stone Age. The words were repeated many times. The broadcast turned those words into anger and sold it. The machines in the dialysis room calculated those words in electricity.

The old man in bed three opened his eyes. He was a retired geography teacher. Three times a week for eleven years he had come to this room.

“Nurse. They didn’t have dialysis machines in the Stone Age, did they.”

Maryam did not stop her hands, which were checking the needle sites on his arm.

“No, sir, they didn’t.”

“Then what would have happened to someone like me in the Stone Age?”

She chose her answer. With a man who had taught geography for years, evasions did not work.

“In the Stone Age someone like you wouldn’t have lived to sixty. So there wouldn’t have been anyone to worry about it.”

The old man laughed. A dry laugh.

“I see. So he means to send us back to a time with nothing to worry about.”

The machine hummed low. Blood ran out through the tube, was filtered, and came back. Electricity did that. In the tank underground there were five days left. Yesterday the Red Crescent had brought two drums of fuel. One more day. She wrote that day in her notebook. That correcting one number could make her this happy was something she had learned in this war.

Children’s voices came up from the basement classroom. Today it was not the times tables. It was a song. Whoever had taught it, it was a spring song.

The words Stone Age bring to mind stone axes and caves. In a hospital the words mean something else. An age without electricity. In that age the six people lying in this room do not exist.

Background Analysis
The Wall on Taleghani Street — 1953, 1979, 2026

The old American embassy on Taleghani Street in Tehran is the place where the memories of the two countries cut against each other most sharply. In 1953 American and British intelligence services helped the coup that brought down Prime Minister Mosaddegh, and Iranians remember the plot as having been hatched in this building. On November 4, 1979, students went over the wall and seized the embassy, and fifty-two American hostages were held for 444 days. Americans remember this building by those 444 days.

After the seizure the building became a museum called the “Den of Spies,” and anti-American murals were painted on its wall. On April 1, 2026, the building was reported damaged in a U.S. strike. The nearby St. Nicholas Orthodox Cathedral was also damaged. This Russian Orthodox cathedral, built in the 1940s, had been used by Tehran’s Russian community.

On the same day Washington and Tehran said exactly opposite things about a “ceasefire request.” One side said Iran’s president had requested it; the other called that false and baseless. That day Iran’s president wrote in an open letter to the American people that Iran had no enmity toward the American people. The reading by this novel’s analyst—that an indirect sounding became a “request” in the carrying—is a character’s view, not a confirmed fact.
