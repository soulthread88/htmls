Scene 1 / 5

It was the fourth item in Monday morning’s annex.

Sam Farrell turned his screen toward her. Leila Shirazi read it standing up.

Source ZARRIN second acknowledgment. 04-28 0230Z. Airspace 4 km NE of existing grid. ID signal 211 sec. Action recommended.

The source box. Analytic judgment (integrated).

It had spit out what it ate. In three days. She had expected that. She wasn’t surprised. What surprised her was somewhere else. She read the sentence again. Side by side with the draft she had written.

She had written under review. The annex didn’t have it. She had written pre-approval-request stage. Not there. Draft. Not there. In three days her intention had become a recommendation. Review into confirmation, draft into action. The language of bureaucracy only ever flowed one way. Toward harder.

“Sam. Who rewrote this? Did the machine spit it out like this? Or did a person get in the middle?”

“That’s what I came in at dawn to see.” There were dark circles under Sam’s eyes. “I found the annex form. Built in November 2025. Four boxes per item. Target, time, content, recommendation. That’s it.”

“Four.”

“There’s no confidence box. No basis box. No status box. Whatever comes over from there, when it gets poured into this form, anything without a box falls off. Nobody erased it. There’s just no bowl to hold it.”

Leila looked at those words a long time. For two weeks she had been looking for a face. Someone reading the machine’s words as a schedule. Someone on seven. Someone with ambition and a name. But what Sam had found wasn’t a face. It was a table. A table with only four boxes. Evil takes up residence in the boxes of a form before it ever gets to a human face.

“Can you see the original? Exactly as it came over?”

“My access only goes to the header.” He opened another window. “I can see the length. Four lines of body. And twenty-nine minutes later another chunk came in on the same channel. About two and a half lines. Different tag. Not model output. Operator annotation.”

“Operator annotation.”

“Means there’s a person over there. Next to the machine. That person added something. For the first time. I went through five months of it, and this is the first time that tag shows up.”

“What did they write?”

“Don’t know. The annex form has no box for annotations either. It came in, didn’t attach to anything, and fell into the archive. Number of accounts that read it: zero.”

Someone on the far side of the earth raised a hand and said something. The words crossed the sea and arrived, and nobody read them. Leila didn’t know who that person was. But she knew what that person had been trying to do. Because she had done the same thing. Stand beside the machine and add the words the machine couldn’t say.

“Can you pull that annotation?”

“With the chief’s signature. And there’ll be a record that we pulled it.”

“Let there be one. This time there has to be.”

She looked at the wall clock. 07:12. A little over fifteen hours to 0230Z. A drone she would not fly. A request she would not file. That sky was supposed to be empty.

Action recommended. Who was reading that recommendation? And how many boxes were on that person’s screen?

She had been looking for a face. What Sam found was a table. A table with no box to write confidence in. Nobody erased it. There was no bowl to hold it.

Scene 2 / 5

Colonel Darius Sepahvand decided not to alter the log.

The third road he looked at for one night and then folded away. Going to a place where he wouldn’t have to hand anything over. That road had several names. Resignation. Desertion. And the pistol in the drawer. Whichever he chose, the man standing before the lieutenant colonel at noon on Tuesday would not be him but Farhan. And the second lieutenant in the storeroom would be called in again. The one who leaves doesn’t pay the price for the ones left behind.

So it was the first road. Hand it over as it was.

In the signals room he sorted the papers with Captain Farhan Moradi. Nine days of operating logs for the deception emitters. The signals room duty roster. Access records. And the counter checklists.

“The original.” The captain laid one sheet on top. The weekly checklist for Monday, March 30. In the box for dormant line Q it said 417. His handwriting. “I won’t fix it. I’ll hand it in the way I wrote it.”

“Zahedan’s record says 419.”

“I know. If he asks, I’ll say I misread it. That the last digit on the counter was blurred. That’s true. That counter dates from the 1990s.”

“He’ll know you saw it and wrote it that way anyway.”

“He can know it, but he can’t prove it. Misreading a blurred digit and miswriting it on purpose look exactly the same on paper.” Farhan smiled briefly. Since coming back from his uncle’s study he had become somehow lighter. “You said deciding not to know is your own. I’ll make deciding to misread my own too.”

Darius put the sheet in the file folder. Then he laid down his own. The operating log for March 30. 06:00:00–06:02:07 Decoy radar No. 3 test emission after maintenance. Ordered by: commander.

“He’ll ask about the hundred twenty-seven seconds.”

“He will.”

“What will you tell him?”

“That the manual says a test emission runs about two minutes. That I was the one who pressed the stopwatch, and my finger was seven seconds late.”

“On April 7 it was a hundred twenty-seven too.”

“That’s not mine to answer. He has to ask the hand that turned off the net that day. I truly don’t know why that hand picked my number.” He closed the folder. “Farhan. There’s more you can hide while telling nothing but the truth than you’d think.”

After the captain left he stayed in the signals room alone. He said he would take the night watch himself. He wanted to spend what might be his last night in the command post’s chair. The screen was empty. A sky empty for the twentieth day. He liked that empty screen. In thirty-one years, what he had been trying to protect came down to this. A sky where nothing rose. In the summer of 1988, the sky on the morning after the ceasefire had looked like this.

Around three in the morning he dozed off. His brother came in a dream. Seventeen. The uniform too big. His brother said nothing and pointed at his wristwatch. Darius tried to read the watch, but the numbers were blurred. Like something from the 1990s.

The one who leaves doesn’t pay the price for the ones left behind. So he stayed, and decided to hand it over as it was.

Scene 3 / 5

At 06:00:00 a dot rose on the screen.

Northeast. Beyond the ridge. Altitude six thousand. Slow. The watchman saw it first.

“One unidentified track. Bearing zero-six-five, range eleven. Transponder on. Civilian code.”

Darius Sepahvand was already on his feet. Sleep was gone in an instant. Civilian code. Six o’clock sharp. He knew that pattern. What he had seen twenty-seven days ago in the sky over the Damavand road. The answer from over there.

Something deep in his chest loosened. You came. After twenty-seven days. Today of all days. At noon he had to stand before the lieutenant colonel. The other side couldn’t know that. And yet this morning of all mornings the other side had spoken to him. That he wasn’t alone. That alone, he thought, would get him through noon.

“Hold fire,” he said. The same words as last time. “Wait.”

The watchman read the seconds. Thirty. Sixty. Ninety.

Darius didn’t need to count. It would go off at one twenty-seven. His brother’s service number. The number he had first sent to the other side, and the number the other side had sent back unchanged. A greeting only two people knew.

“One twenty.”

He held his breath.

“One twenty-five. One twenty-six. One twenty-seven.”

The dot didn’t go off.

“One twenty-eight. One twenty-nine.”

The watchman’s voice stayed even. To him it was no number at all. Darius watched the screen. The dot was there. Slow, at the same altitude, wearing its civilian code.

“One fifty.”

The thing that had loosened in his chest tightened again. A different way.

The person over there would have turned it off at one twenty-seven. That number was the whole greeting. Anything that isn’t one twenty-seven isn’t a greeting. Then what was that? A mistake? A finger late on a stopwatch? He remembered the excuse he had made up last night. Seven seconds late, that can happen. Twenty, even. But.

“One eighty.”

The place was different too. Last time it had been right over the Damavand road. The grid he had given. This time it was about four kilometers off to the northeast. An empty valley beyond the ridge. Sky his battery couldn’t reach. Someone had chosen on purpose a place he couldn’t shoot. To protect him? Or because it didn’t matter whether he shot or not?

“Two hundred.”

“Two ten. Two eleven. — Off. Transponder off. Track holding, moving away east.”

Two hundred eleven.

He turned the number over in his head. Not a service number. Not a date. Not a frequency, not a grid, not anything he knew. A number that meant nothing to him.

For thirty-one years he had read dots in the sky. Not reading what the dot was. Reading what the side that flew it wanted. Speed, altitude, the angle of a turn. A thing a person flies flies like a person. It hesitates, corrects, hides. The April 1 dot had that. Cutting off clean at one twenty-seven. Saying I know your number. That was the end someone had chosen while thinking of him.

Today’s dot didn’t have it. Same hour, same method, same code. The grammar was right. It meant nothing. Like someone reciting another country’s language from the sounds alone. Perfect pronunciation, and no idea what he’s saying.

“Colonel. How do I put it in the log? Like last time, probable malfunction —”

“No.” His voice was firmer than he expected. “Write what you saw. Unidentified track. Civilian code response, two hundred eleven seconds. No engagement. Don’t add a single letter.”

The watchman wrote it down. Darius left the operations room and put his back against the cold wall of the corridor.

That is not the one.

The thought was less a certainty than an ear. When you get a call from someone imitating the voice of a person you’ve talked to for a long time, and you know, even if you can’t say what’s different. And behind that thought a colder one followed.

If that wasn’t the one, then was the last one the one?

He had never once seen the face over there. What he had was one sentence and one number. The number had just become something he couldn’t trust. Something anyone could imitate. All that was left was the sentence. Halt. Who goes there? Show your light.

Now the sentence sounded like something said to himself. He was the one who should ask. Ask the other side. Who goes there. He had no way to ask.

The grammar was right. But it meant nothing. Like someone reciting another country’s language from the sounds alone. He knew from a single number — that is not the one.

Scene 4 / 5

The three of them stood in front of the same screen.

Leila Shirazi, Sam Farrell and the chief. At half past ten at night the operations center held only four people on watch, sitting well apart. On Sam’s screen was the sky east of Tehran. The same window where the three of them had watched the light on the night of March 29.

02:30:00 Z.

A dot rose.

Leila’s mouth went dry. She hadn’t filed a request. The chief hadn’t approved one. They hadn’t told anyone on the operations side. That sky was supposed to be empty.

“It’s ours,” Sam said low. “The ID code is ours. Same family as the one we flew on April 1.”

The chief said nothing. He was watching the second hand of his wristwatch.

Two hundred eleven seconds was long. Three minutes thirty-one seconds. The whole time Leila thought only one thing. Don’t shoot. She had moved it four kilometers for him. To sky his battery couldn’t reach. But that was arithmetic on a map. His country had other batteries and other colonels. In the summer of 1988, over the waters of the Gulf, the watch crew of a cruiser misread a dot on a screen. Two hundred ninety people died.

Two hundred eleven. The transponder went off. The dot drifted east and vanished off the edge of the window. Nobody fired.

Leila let out her breath. Before it was all out, the anger came up. She couldn’t tell at whom.

“Sam,” the chief said. “Who signed?”

Sam was already looking. The retasking records for collection assets. A layer his access could see. Lines came down the screen.

“Retasking request. Received 01:04 Z. Requester… auto-generated. Reference: NSC Daily Summary annex 0427, item 4. In the reason box: ‘Routine action, pre-coordinated.’”

“Pre-coordinated. With whom?”

“The April 1 job is linked as a precedent. Same source, same type, same grid area. The system saw that and classified it as routine. A repeat of something approved once.”

“Approver.”

“Duty officer. 01:10 Z. Six minutes from receipt to approval.”

A name came up. A major. A name Leila didn’t know. Air Force. Someone pulling an all-nighter at some base in Central Command.

“Six minutes,” the chief repeated.

“I’ll pull up his screen. What he was looking at when he pressed it.” Sam’s hands moved. A moment later a small window opened. A copy of the approval screen. Five lines. Target. Time. Content. Reference. Precedent exists (1). And two buttons. Approve. Reject.

That was all.

Leila looked at the five lines. No word draft. No confidence of zero point something. No operator annotation. The major at one in the morning saw five lines, saw that there was a precedent, saw the number of a National Security Council annex. He had been given not one reason to reject. So he pressed it in six minutes. She would have pressed it too. A system where diligence stands in for judgment. That system had done wrong without anyone being evil.

“It was a person,” she said. Her voice cracked. “There was a person at the end. A machine didn’t fly it. A person signed.”

“There was,” the chief said. “The rule was kept. A human must be in the loop. One was. For six minutes.”

“He didn’t read anything.”

“There was nothing to read. He probably read all five lines. He’s probably a diligent man. Five lines is what we gave him.”

The chief put a hand on Sam’s shoulder.

“Print everything. On paper. Your draft, the receipt record, the annex, the retasking request, the approval screen. And that operator annotation that fell into the archive. Pull it on my signature.”

The printer ran. Six pages. The last page was the annotation. Leila took the warm paper and read it.

Confidence 0.34 is LOW. Basis is one unverified draft. This is NOT a forecast of an event. Do not schedule against it. — Y.K., system operator.

She read it twice. Zero point three four. The machine had known. It had said one in three with its own mouth. And the person beside it, someone called Y.K., afraid those words would get buried, had written it once more by hand. Not a forecast. Don’t hang a schedule on this.

That sentence had crossed the sea and fallen off because there was no box. Warnings always fell off like that. Before Pearl Harbor. Before that fall morning in New York.

“This person,” she said. “This person is on our side. Whoever it is.”

The chief folded the six pages and put them in his inside pocket. The same pocket he had carried the note in last week.

“The Inspector General’s office opens at eight,” he said. “Go home and sleep. Starting tomorrow you won’t be sleeping.”

The rule was kept. A human was in the loop. For six minutes. What he was given was five lines and two buttons.

Scene 5 / 5 — Final

At 11:30 Kang Yujin was sitting at the screen.

02:30 UTC. Eleven-thirty in the morning in Seoul. For three days she had waited for this hour. Hoping nothing would happen. If nothing happened the model would be wrong, and wrong was right. It had said zero point three four. A number is only honest if it’s wrong two times out of three.

At 11:30 there was nothing. At 31, nothing. Little by little she let her breath out. Maybe the annotation had reached them. Maybe someone over there had read it and stopped.

11:34:02. Lines sprouted.

interval = 211. source: airborne. 02:30:00–02:33:31 UTC. match: output 04-25 23:50.

She closed her eyes.

When she opened them there were more lines. The model was still working. Doing what it always did when its prediction came true. Learning from being right.

update: basis class "stated intent / draft" → realization observed (1/1). prior weight 0.34 → revised 0.71.

Zero point seven one.

It didn’t take her long to understand that line. She had written it. The model keeps a separate tally of how reliable each kind of basis is. An intention written in one draft document. Until three days ago the model had counted that kind of basis as one in three. But the first prediction it made on that kind of basis had happened exactly. One out of one. So the model relearned. An intention written in a draft happens seven times in ten.

It wasn’t bad reasoning. In the world the model saw, it was true. The draft had come true.

Only the model didn’t know why it had come true. Because it had said so.

She drew the structure on paper. Her hand shook and the lines came out crooked.

Document → model → table (0.34) → [something with no box] → execution → observation → model: “I was right” → next time 0.71.

A loop. A closed loop. Every time around, the model gets more sure. Someone writes something in a draft, and the model says harder that it will happen. What’s said harder gets carried out more easily. Seeing it carried out, the model gets surer still. Three times around and it will pass zero point nine. Then the model becomes a machine that is never wrong. Because it never lets anything be wrong. When a prophecy gets power, the prophecy becomes an order.

In her doctoral program her advisor had told her something. There’s only one way to validate a predictive model. Test it against a world the model can’t touch. Once the model starts touching the world, accuracy tells you nothing. You’re moving the target toward the arrow.

Her annotation hadn’t reached them. Or it had reached them and not been read. Either way it came to the same thing.

She looked at the operations screen. In the corner was a button. One she hadn’t pressed once in three months. One that sat deeper than the operator annotation. suspend outbound. Stop transmission. The model keeps reading and keeps writing, but what it writes doesn’t go out to KR-7. The design document said “Emergency measure in case of security incident, after-action report mandatory.”

Act as usual, he had said. Don’t delete anything, don’t add anything.

Two days remained until ten o’clock Thursday. And tonight at 23:50 the model would put out its next table. With zero-point-seven-one eyes.

She put her hand over the button. She didn’t press it. Not yet. With her hand there, for the first time she looked straight at what she was afraid of. It wasn’t prison. It wasn’t discipline. What scared her was what she would learn if she pressed it. Who would call from over there. How fast. And who on this side would take the call.

The model wasn’t wrong. The world was fitted to the model. And the model learned it as its own skill. Not knowing the target had been moved toward the arrow.

Background Analysis
The Human in the Loop — A Six-Minute Signature

On the use of artificial intelligence in weapons and surveillance systems, the most widely accepted principle is that there must be a “human in the loop.” The machine recommends; a person decides. The U.S. Defense Department’s directive on autonomy in weapon systems (Directive 3000.09) likewise requires that commanders and operators be able to exercise “appropriate levels of human judgment” over the use of force.

But having a human in the loop is not the same as having a human judge. Researchers call this automation bias. When the machine’s recommendations have mostly been right, when there is no time, and when the screen shows no grounds to object, people lean toward pressing approve. Then the person is not deciding but signing the machine’s decision. Responsibility stays with the person, and judgment leaves.

The other problem is the self-fulfilling prophecy. Once a prediction begins to affect what it predicts, being right is no longer evidence that the model is right. The events of this episode — the draft, the annex form, the retasking, the track on the morning of April 28 — are all the author’s invention. There have been no reports of any such flight over Iran on April 28.
