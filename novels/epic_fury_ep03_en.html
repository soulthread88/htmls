Pakistan — Islamabad, a private villa near the Foreign Ministry, third-floor reception room
March 4, 2026 (Wed.) · 2:15 p.m. (Pakistan local time) — War Day 5
Scene 1 / 8

The air conditioner in the reception room was broken. Islamabad was still cool in March. Even so the three men in the room were sweating. Whether it was the heat or the pressure, none of them could have said.

On one side of the table sat the man from Tehran. He had no title. Ten-odd years ago he had worked the nuclear talks; now his name was on no ministry’s roster. Across from him sat a senior official of Pakistan’s Foreign Ministry and his aide. There were no Americans. Officially this room did not exist. Officially nothing said here was said.

Pakistan shares a border of more than 900 kilometers with Iran. Since the break in relations in 1980, the Pakistani embassy in Washington has served as Iran’s interests section inside the United States. It is one of the few countries on speaking terms with the United States, China, Saudi Arabia, and Iran alike, and it is a nuclear power besides. Where formal diplomacy is blocked, the world tends to go looking for a corridor. Islamabad was one of those corridors.

The man from Tehran spoke first.

“Seventy-two hours. That’s all I’ve brought. If the bombing stops for seventy-two hours, the people on our side who can speak can get into one room. Right now there is no room to get into. Every building we meet in gets a bomb.”

The Pakistani lifted his teacup and set it down again. That was his way. There and not there; listening and not listening. The mediator’s craft goes by many names. Tact, some call it. Face, others. Sometimes just silence.

“In whose name do these words come?” he asked.

The man did not answer. The silence was the answer. The Supreme Leader was dead and his successor had not yet been named. The president was alive, but the keys to the missiles were not in his hands. In the summer of 1988 there had at least been a man to lift the poisoned cup. A man who compared the ceasefire to drinking poison and drained the cup anyway. Who in Tehran could now say “we,” not even Tehran knew.

“Washington will ask,” the Pakistani official said, “what will be different after seventy-two hours. And it will ask about Fordow.”

“Fordow,” the man said, “is not in the words I was given to carry.”

“Then I shall have to make that call empty-handed.”

Silence again. In a room with no air conditioning the silence weighed more. The Pakistani wrote nothing in his notebook. Writing nothing was the rule of this room. He memorized one number. Seventy-two. Thirty years had taught him how much lighter that number would get on its way to Washington. Three days for one country to catch its breath becomes one line on another country’s desk.

A war begins with gunfire, but its weight shows in the rooms where nothing gets decided.

Langley, Virginia — CIA Iran Operations Center
March 4, 2026 (Wed.) · 5:08 a.m. EST — War Day 5
Scene 2 / 8

Dr. Leila Shirazi checked the signal three times. Then three more. The first time she put it down to instrument error. The second time, to interference. The third time she could not deny it any longer.

The signal from Fordow was getting stronger.

Three days ago what she first caught was a pulse that broke and came back. A 5.7-second period. Yesterday morning the seven people in the conference room had sat in front of that pulse and reached no conclusion. This morning before dawn the pulse was clearer. The gaps were shorter. The amplitude was up.

The enrichment facility at Fordow is buried in the mountains north of Qom. In June 2025 the U.S. Air Force dropped twelve GBU-57 MOPs on it — a bomb that weighs 13,600 kilograms and is designed to punch through about 18 meters of reinforced concrete or about 60 meters of earth. Satellite photographs showed penetration holes above the enrichment hall. But the initial assessment by the U.S. Defense Intelligence Agency was that the strike had set Iran’s program back only a few months, and the confidence attached to that assessment was “low.” No one since had looked directly under the mountain.

Leila worked the pattern again. A broken system running on what power it has left gives an irregular signal. This one had a rule to it. Something completely dead cannot make a rule.

It meant one thing.

Under the mountain, someone — or something — was doing more work.

She began writing the report. Her fingers stopped over the keys. There was the sentence she had written seven months ago. “The only intervention capable of changing the trajectory is kinetic intervention.” She knew what that sentence meant in front of this signal. Kinetic intervention had happened once last year and had been going on now for five days. If that was not enough, what came next?

She did not write the next sentence. It was not hers to write. But she knew. In 2003 a few lines of reports that went out of this building had paved the road to Baghdad. Somebody would write that sentence. And what came of it would be a column of smoke somewhere on that screen too.

In the windowless room she filed the report. 0551. Seven fifty-one in the evening in Seoul, 2:21 in the afternoon in Tehran, 3:51 in Islamabad. The world lived the same moment at different hours. Through all of them the Fordow signal blinked.

Technical Background
The Fordow Signal — What It Could Mean

The June 2025 strike was aimed at the roof of the enrichment hall. What was left underneath has been argued over since the day after. Centrifuges are fragile under vibration; a nearby blast is enough to shatter the rotors. The control room and the power systems, on the other hand, and whatever spare machines may have been stored in the mountain’s other tunnels, were a different matter.

What Leila Shirazi picked up is a low-frequency electromagnetic emission with a 5.7-second period. It overlaps with the signature of the frequency converters that drive a centrifuge cascade. It is not a pattern that can come out of a facility that has been completely destroyed. Either the surviving machines have been strung back together and are running, or new machines have been brought in — either way, it means someone has been at work under the mountain for nine months.

There is only one way to confirm it. Inspectors go in. And there was one more clue, a quiet one. Three days before the war began, an internal IAEA technical report compiling remote-monitoring data carried a footnote. “SCADA stability figures abnormally high — possibility of external interference cannot be ruled out.” Someone must have read it. And turned the page.

Over Tehran — a formation of Israeli Air Force F-35I Adir jets
March 4, 2026 (Wed.) — War Day 5
Scene 3 / 8

The pilot’s call sign was Saba. A major in the Israeli Air Force, thirty-five, from Tel Aviv. Her real name was withheld for security. Her fifth sortie in five days. The target was a military complex east of Tehran. Every pilot in the Israeli Air Force knows the route the men before them flew in 1981 to hit the reactor outside Baghdad and come home. She was much farther east than that now.

Then something came up on her radar.

The Yak-130 is a Russian-built advanced trainer that doubles as a light attack aircraft. In an Iranian air force whose fighter fleet had mostly gone obsolete under sanctions and age, it was one of the few new airframes. It was not an airplane to send against a stealth aircraft. On March 4 the Israeli military announced that an F-35I Adir had shot down an Iranian Yak-130 over Tehran. It was the first time in history that a stealth fighter had downed a crewed military aircraft in combat, and the first time since 1985 that the Israeli Air Force had brought down an enemy plane in the air.

The fight was short. All Saba saw was a symbol on a screen. The symbol turned. Not the way you turn to run. She went by the book. The missile came off the rail. A few dozen seconds later the symbol was gone from the screen.

The other pilot probably never saw her. She knew that. A man who had come up to fight something he could not see. A man who could not have failed to know that once he was up he would not come back. Who ran down the runway anyway. The machine had reduced him to a symbol. She could not erase the man behind it.

History is written at the moment someone is just doing their job.

Heading home, Saba reported the engagement over the radio. Her voice was level. What had been in those few dozen seconds—the other pilot’s will, his choice—she left out. That was not something you put in a radio report.

When she turned west the clouds were low. Somewhere under them was Tel Aviv. Under the clouds behind her was Tehran. The two cities could not see each other. She was between them.

Suez Canal, Egypt — bridge of the U.S. aircraft carrier USS Gerald R. Ford (CVN-78), southbound
March 5, 2026 (Thu.) · 7:52 a.m. (Egypt local time) — War Day 6
Scene 4 / 8

Captain Marcus Webb was passing between deserts. Sand showed over the rail on both sides. A hundred-thousand-ton ship went down a channel little more than two hundred meters wide at a walking pace. In the canal even a carrier waits in line.

The orders had come two days ago. Leave the eastern Mediterranean for the Red Sea. He did not ask why. The map was the answer. The war’s center of gravity was shifting south and east.

USS Gerald R. Ford transited the Suez Canal on March 5 and entered the Red Sea the next day. The day before, March 4, the war had spread beyond the Gulf. Off Galle, Sri Lanka, a U.S. Navy submarine sank the Iranian frigate IRIS Dena. She had been on her way home from the International Fleet Review at Visakhapatnam, India. Eighty-seven of her crew died; 32 were rescued by the Sri Lankan navy. The same day, in port at Bandar Abbas, a Shahid Soleimani-class corvette went down in an airstrike. U.S. Central Command said that since the start of the war it had destroyed 19 Iranian vessels and 1 submarine and struck about 2,000 targets. The IRGC Navy claimed to have blown up 10 merchant vessels that tried to pass through the Strait of Hormuz. The strait did not open.

Sarah Callahan came up to the bridge. She had no camera in her hands. It was the first time since the war began that she had come up to the bridge without one.

“I heard about the Dena,” she said. “Eighty-seven.”

Webb nodded. He had started out as a pilot, but he had spent half of his twenty-five years on ships. He knew what a frigate’s engine room looked like. He knew how a ship folds when a torpedo goes off under the keel. He also knew the name of the Iranian frigate Sahand, sunk by the U.S. Navy in the Gulf in the spring of 1988. They were the enemy. And they were sailors. Neither sentence canceled the other.

“She was headed home,” Sarah said.

“She was a warship of a country at war,” he said. After a long while he added, “Both are true.”

For a moment they looked the same way. The canal. The low morning sun. The stern of the destroyer ahead.

“I’m getting off,” she said. “By helicopter, once we’re in the Red Sea. The desk wants me in Bahrain. They say the strait story has to be written from there.”

He was not surprised. Reporters go where the story is. This ship was heading for the edge of the story now. A fleet moves on orders. A reporter moves toward people.

“Bahrain is taking hits,” he said.

“I know,” she said. “That’s why I’m going.”

There was nothing more to say. The silence between them was not the silence that pooled in that room in Islamabad. It was a silence that held more than words.

She went down first. He stayed and watched the channel. The desert was quiet. The ship was passing through a quiet place on her way to a loud one.

He knew it. You do not open a sea by sinking ships. Then what does open it?

Bahrain — Mina Salman Port, the quarters area at the headquarters of the U.S. Fifth Fleet
March 5, 2026 (Thu.) · 9:15 p.m. (Bahrain local time) — evening of War Day 6
Scene 5 / 8

Lieutenant Kim Junho had been sitting with the phone in his hand for twenty minutes.

In those twenty minutes he had tried to call nine times. All nine times he stopped at the call button. He did not know what to say. Or, to be exact, what to say first. If he began with Mother, I’m alive, she would cry. If he heard her cry, he would cry too. That was what he was afraid of.

Day six of the war. He still had not talked to his parents once. He knew it was not right. He knew an officer’s duty did not require silence toward his family. It was not duty. He felt one phone call would make his mother’s voice and his father’s silence real.

He made the call.

It rang. Before the second ring was over his mother picked up. It was 3:15 in the morning in Seoul. She had been waiting. For six days. Every night. Beside the phone. In the winter of 1950 the mothers of this country waited for the mailman. The mother of 2026 waited for the phone.

“Junho.”

Three syllables. Those three were enough. He did not say anything. He was just there. The phone to his ear, under the night sky of Bahrain, hearing his mother’s voice for the first time in six days.

“I’m alive,” he said. His voice did not shake. That surprised him more.

“I know,” his mother said. “We’re watching the news. Your father too.”

A short silence. His mother’s voice got smaller. Almost a whisper.

“Sleep well.”

That was all. She did not ask anything more. When are you coming, isn’t it dangerous, are you eating. She asked nothing. That made it bigger. By not asking she was saying it: I know everything, I am waiting, that is enough.

He hung up. He looked at the sky. There were no stars in the night sky over Bahrain. Even under blackout the city’s lights did not all go out. Still he looked at that sky a long time.

Steady hands took something like twenty-five years to make, he had guessed, watching the senior officers. He was living the first week of those twenty-five years now. This was what the first week was. That calling your mother was hard. That her sleep well sounded like the longest sentence in the world.

Some wars are fought with guns, and some by picking up a phone.

Tehran — residential streets south of Sharif University of Technology, rooftop of a five-story building
March 6, 2026 (Fri.) · 6:10 p.m. (Tehran local time) — War Day 7 · two weeks before Nowruz
Scene 6 / 8

The sun was going down. Three columns of smoke stood in the sky west of Tehran. Mehrabad Airport had been hit during the day. The day before yesterday it was Azadi Square, yesterday the twelve-thousand-seat Azadi Indoor Stadium. A square named Freedom. It was no longer a place to gather.

Soraya Tahir was on a roof. The roof of a friend’s boardinghouse, between the water tank and the satellite dishes. The internet had been dead for six days and the phones came and went. The city had lost its ways of passing news to itself. What was left was the oldest way. The voice.

Nowruz is the Persian New Year. It begins at the spring equinox, which in 2026 fell on March 20. With a history of more than 3,000 years, it is Iran’s greatest holiday, handed down from before Islam. Even after the revolution the holiday was not erased. Iranians have an old practice of shouting slogans at night from rooftops and windows. They did it in 1979, in 2009, in 2022. In March 2026, in a city where security forces were firing on people in the streets and the internet had been cut, the practice came back.

Soraya did not shout slogans. She sang. An old spring song. A song about winter ending and the flowers coming out. A song every Iranian knows. A song not banned even after the revolution. A song you could not ban without no longer being Iranian.

At first she was alone. On the second verse a window opened in the building across the way. On the third, someone on the next roof sang along. In the summer of 2009 the rooftops of this city had called to each other like this too. Then it was slogans; now it was a song. It did not take the whole song to reach the end of the alley.

Two Basij motorcycles came into the alley. The militiaman on the back of one looked up. Soraya saw his face. He was young. Her age. He looked up at the rooftops and did not reach for the gun on his shoulder. The man in front shouted something. The motorcycle went out of the alley without slowing. The orders were in the front seat. The hesitation was in the back.

Nobody filmed it. Even if they had, there was nowhere to send it. That song came up on no screen anywhere in the world. It was only in that alley.

The war had broken the city. But spring did not stop. Spring is older than war. Could this city cross the two weeks left before Nowruz on a song?

Tehran — University of Tehran, Department of Philosophy office, Building 16
March 7, 2026 (Sat.) · 10:15 a.m. (Tehran local time) — War Day 8
Scene 7 / 8

Professor Azad Moradi took out the notebook he had bought in 1973 again. Four days ago he had filled its last page. A page he had left empty for fifty-three years. There was no room left to write. But last night’s singing was still in his ears. A song that had come across from the rooftops while he sat up all night in his office, from the alleys south of the university.

He turned the notebook over. After the last page came the back cover. The inside of the back cover was blank. Yellowed board. In fifty-three years he had never thought of it as a page.

New smoke rose to the west outside the window. From the direction of Mehrabad Airport. It had been hit yesterday and again this morning. Yesterday the president had come on television. Masoud Pezeshkian. He apologized for the attacks on the neighboring countries. Azad watched the broadcast to the end. He had not seen power in this country that knew how to apologize in a long time. But after the apology the missiles still flew toward the Gulf. The hand that spoke and the hand that fired were not attached to the same body.

He picked up his pen. On the inside of the back cover he wrote.

March 7, 2026. Day eight of the war. New smoke stands in the western sky. Last night the students sang on the rooftops. An old spring song. The men with guns passed under them and did not fire. I heard it. Fifty-three years ago on the first page of this notebook I wrote: the truth is uncomfortable, a lie hurts longer. Today on the inside of the cover I write this. Freedom is uncomfortable too. But oppression hurts longer. The bombs did not teach that. The people who sing under the bombs are teaching it.

He closed the notebook and put it on the desk. A notebook with truly no place left to write.

The campus was quiet. The song came only at night.

He remembered 1979. The year of the revolution. There had been singing in the streets then too. The singing then and the singing now were different. Then it was a song going toward something; now it was a song going back to something. No—whether it was going back or going somewhere for the first time, he could not tell yet. In Iran’s history freedom had always been complicated. The freedom of the Pahlavis was the king’s freedom. The freedom of the Islamic Republic was God’s freedom. Human freedom had always been waiting somewhere in between.

He had taught that to his students for close to half a century. And now those students—young people he could not be sure had ever been taught by him, but who had grown up in the same city under the same sky—were singing on the roofs.

Was this enough? He did not know yet. A beginning or an end? He did not know yet. Only that today he had written something on the inside of the back cover. For now that was enough.

Langley, Virginia — CIA Iran Operations Center
March 7, 2026 (Sat.) · 11:44 a.m. EST — War Day 8
Scene 8 / 8 — Final

The Fordow signal had changed again. Leila Shirazi looked at the screen. The amplitude was twice what it had been when she first caught it.

She wrote down three possibilities. One, an overload from worn-out equipment just before it fails. The way a candle burns brighter just before it goes out. Two, the technicians were bringing more cascades online one at a time. Three, the one she feared most. That when the war began someone had ordered them to speed up.

There was no way now to tell which. Inspectors had to go in. For inspectors to go in, Iran had to open the door. For Iran to open the door, talks had to start with somebody. For talks to start, the bombing had to stop. The bombing did not stop. This morning Tehran and Isfahan were hit again.

She knew how weak each link of that chain was. An analyst’s job is to put numbers on uncertainty. Multiply those numbers together and the result was not hopeful.

There was another road. Bombs. Somebody in the conference room had already said the word. But last June’s twelve bombs had shown where that road ended. The mountain was hit, and nine months later the mountain was sending a signal again. Leila thought of 1953. The fast road this agency had chosen in Tehran. The bill came in 1979.

She wrote one more report. Its last sentence was different from the one seven months ago.

“Kinetic intervention alone is not sufficient. The next step is diplomatic intervention. Without it, the Fordow signal cannot be stopped.”

She wrote the sentence. Saved it. Filed it.

The sentence seven months ago had given this war a reason. Today’s sentence said war was not enough. The same analyst, in front of the same screen, looking at the same country, had come to a different conclusion. The more data piled up, the less certain she was. But what rode the chain of approval upward was always the sentence that sounded certain.

In the room with no windows she drank a glass of water. It was the first water since the war began that she had tasted as she drank it. It was cold. That was good.

A war is harder to end than to start. A bomb breaks a building, but a signal does not stop for bombs.

At the same moment it was 1:44 Sunday morning in Seoul. Kang Yujin could not fall asleep on the cot in her lab. On the screen she had left on was a map of ship positions in Hormuz. Dots that did not move. She did not know yet why those dots kept catching her eye. When would they move again?

Diplomatic Context
The Apology and the Missiles — What Happened on March 6–7

On March 6, Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian went on state television and apologized for the attacks on neighboring countries. Iran, he said, had not attacked any friendly neighboring country. On March 7 he apologized again in a video message and ordered a halt to attacks that units in the field were launching at their own discretion.

The attacks did not stop. On March 6 alone, three missiles flew at Prince Sultan Air Base in Saudi Arabia, Qatar intercepted 13, and in Bahrain a hotel and two residential buildings were targeted. On March 7 the Malta-flagged tanker Prima was hit by a drone near Hormuz, and Kuwait Petroleum Corp. declared force majeure and began cutting production.

The gap between the president’s words and the Revolutionary Guards’ missiles — that was what the country called Iran actually looked like in those days. The Supreme Leader was dead and no successor had been announced. To the question of whom to negotiate with, Tehran itself had no answer. On that same March 7, the United States sent a third aircraft carrier, USS George H.W. Bush, to the Middle East.

Soraya Tahir sang a spring song on a rooftop, and a man with a gun went by without shooting. Professor Azad wrote one more line inside the back cover of a notebook that was already full. The world was moving toward spring — very slowly, very uncertainly.

Author’s Note
The Room in IslamabadFictional
The back-channel contact in Scene 1 of Episode 3 and the “seventy-two hours” proposal are the author’s invention. There is no public record of any such contact in early March 2026. Pakistan in fact became the venue only later, for the Islamabad talks of April 11–12, after the ceasefire of April 8. It is true that the Pakistani embassy in Washington serves as Iran’s interests section in the United States.
The F-35I’s Downing of a Yak-130Real event / March 4
On March 4 the Israeli military announced that an F-35I Adir had shot down an Iranian Yak-130 over Tehran. It is the first case of a stealth fighter downing a crewed military aircraft in combat, and the Israeli Air Force’s first air-to-air kill since 1985. The pilot “Saba” and the details of the engagement are the author’s invention.
The Sinking of the Dena (IRIS Dena)Real event / March 4
The Iranian frigate Dena, returning from the International Fleet Review 2026 at Visakhapatnam, India, was attacked by a U.S. Navy submarine and sank off Galle, Sri Lanka. Eighty-seven died and 32 were rescued by the Sri Lankan navy. It is reported to be the first time since World War II that a torpedo from a U.S. submarine has sunk an enemy warship. On March 6 the Iranian replenishment ship Bushehr was interned in Sri Lanka with her 208 crew members.
The Ford’s MovementsReal timeline
USS Gerald R. Ford was in the Eastern Mediterranean off Israel from February 27, passed through the Suez Canal on March 5, and entered the Red Sea on March 6. This novel follows that actual track.
Pezeshkian’s ApologyReal event / March 6–7
Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian apologized for the attacks on neighboring countries and ordered them halted, on state television on March 6 and in a video message on March 7. The attacks went on immediately afterward.
Azadi Square and Azadi Indoor StadiumReal place / Tehran
Azadi (آزادی) is Persian for “freedom.” The square around the Azadi Tower, built in 1971, is where the 1979 revolution and the protests that came after it gathered. On March 4, military installations and a metro station in the area of the square came under airstrike; on March 5, so did the 12,000-seat Azadi Indoor Stadium.
NowruzReal holiday / Iranian culture
Persian for “new day.” Iran’s New Year, rooted in Zoroastrianism, begins at the spring equinox. It was inscribed on UNESCO’s list of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2009. The 2026 war began three weeks before Nowruz.
GBU-57 MOPReal weapon / United States
A bunker-penetrating bomb weighing about 13,600 kilograms, designed to break through about 18 meters of reinforced concrete or about 60 meters of earth. Twelve were dropped on Fordow in June 2025. The “Fordow signal” of this novel is a fiction built on the real uncertainty surrounding the effect of that strike.
IAEA — International Atomic Energy AgencyReal organization
Founded in 1957 and headquartered in Vienna. Its inspections of Iran’s nuclear facilities depend on the cooperation of the Iranian government. The “SCADA footnote” in the story is the author’s invention.
Soraya TahirFictional character
Returning from Episode 2: a third-year electrical engineering student at Sharif University of Technology, age 22. The scene in which she sings a spring song on a rooftop is paired with Professor Azad’s point of view — a structure in which a generation that has lived seventy-two years and a generation that has lived twenty-two listen to the same night in the same city.
