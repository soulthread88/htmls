Scene 1 / 5

All weekend Leila Shirazi couldn’t write a single sentence. The first time in sixteen years.

She couldn’t write the real reason for objecting. She had to find a reason she could write. Something not false, something that didn’t touch the note, something the woman on seven couldn’t read and let slide. Telling the truth inside a bureaucracy was always translation.

Monday morning she found it while rereading a letter from Ameh. Ameh always closed her letters with the same words. I kiss your eyes. Last month’s letter didn’t have them. When it came, Leila worried for three days. Was Ameh sick? Had someone written it for her? It turned out she had only run out of paper and folded the end over. But for those three days Leila hadn’t believed a single other word in the letter.

When what always comes comes differently, people suspect it. A machine doesn’t know that.

She wrote. In a document she would save. For that room to read, and for it to read.

Channel opinion: Object.

1. The PC-ASSESS confidence of 0.83 rests on the premise that “the source accepted the 4.28 signal as contact from our side.” The only basis for this premise is the fact that the source did not engage the track.

2. But non-engagement does not mean acceptance. On 3.30 the source proved his identity with a 127 seconds of his own choosing, and on 4.1 we answered with the same 127 seconds. This number is the only means of authentication between the two sides. The 4.28 signal was 211 seconds. From the source’s position, 211 is a wrong code. A trained officer does not accept a wrong code as contact. He takes it as an attempted penetration, or as imitation by a third party. Non-engagement is fully explained by the rules of engagement during a ceasefire.

3. The source may therefore currently judge that “someone is imitating our side’s format.” If in this state 127 seconds reappears at the same grid, the source may read it as “the imitator has corrected its code.” This could lead the source to abandon the channel entirely.

4. In addition, given the possibility that the source is under counterintelligence investigation (since the 4.7 incident), inducing a response poses a direct risk to the source’s personal safety.

5. Recommendation: do not execute the 5.14 signal. Await source-initiated contact. The channel requests access to the underlying data used to calculate the PC-ASSESS 0.83.

When she finished she reread it line by line. Nothing false. Rowell already knew April 28 had been her bait. The note was nowhere. And item three. That wasn’t her guess; it was her hope. That the man on the mountain had recognized two hundred eleven as a fake. She had translated a hope into the language of analysis, and knew it wasn’t wrong as analysis either. Good analysis sometimes took the same shape as a good prayer.

At 11:40 on Tuesday she submitted it. The repository blinked once. The sound of swallowing. This time it was written to be swallowed. What would the machine learn if it read this? The channel objects. Would it then write up what the channel would do next?

At 12:05 an answer came. Three lines.

Channel opinion received. Items 2 and 3 are valid. But if that logic is right, the source already suspects the channel, and the only way to clear that suspicion is to send the right code. If 127 is not sent, 211 remains our last word. — 5.14 signal approved. Channel dissent recorded. Request to view underlying data denied. C.R.

Leila looked at the answer a long time. That woman had taken her logic, turned it over, and used it to prop up her own conclusion. Nothing in it was wrong. That woman doesn’t say things that are wrong. Right sentences had come together to make a wrong decision.

Sam Farrell read it over her shoulder and said low:

“You lost.”

“It’s on record.”

“What good is that?”

“There was an operator at the other end who blocked the channel. That person lost too. Opened it again in less than two days. But the record that she pressed it stays.” She turned off the screen. “Later someone will ask. Was there anyone who objected? Being able to answer yes is different from answering no.” The papers from Vietnam, the papers from Iraq, had stayed that way too.

As she said it she knew she was talking like the chief. And she thought about where the chief was now. In front of six tomato seedlings. The people who objected mostly grew old in front of soil.

Thursday before dawn, 0230Z. Six o’clock in Tehran. Two days away. One twenty-seven would rise in that sky. And she still didn’t know what the man on the mountain would read it as.

“Later someone will ask. Was there anyone who objected? Being able to answer yes is different from answering no.”

Tehran — nurses’ station, Imam Khomeini Hospital

Scene 2 / 5

The landline in the nurses’ station rang. A hospital phone always carried someone’s fate.

Maryam was writing a chart. The colleague beside her answered. Listened, pressed the receiver to her chest, and looked at Maryam. Maryam read something in that face, and before she knew what it was, she was on her feet.

“It’s an army line. From the south. For you.”

The receiver was heavy. The line crackled. Far off, many voices mixed together. The sound of people standing in line.

“Hello.”

“It’s me.”

Seventy days. Seventy days of silence ended with two words.

She felt her knees give. Her colleague pushed a chair under her. She didn’t sit. If she sat, she thought, her voice wouldn’t come.

“Davood.”

“They give us forty seconds. The line behind me is long. Listen. I’m all right. The shoulder’s healed. Last week this place got hit, but not our company. I’m fine. Nariman?”

“She’s good. School… she studies in the hospital basement. The professor teaches them. She drew you. Standing by the sea. Holding a shell.”

On the other end the breathing stopped. Then the sound of him laughing. Whether he was laughing or crying, the line was too bad to tell.

“No shells here. Just the smell of oil.”

“Pick one up. Even one.”

“I’ll pick one up.”

“The house —” She started to say it, then stopped. That the house was gone. She couldn’t say that in forty seconds. “We’re at the hospital. In the dorm. When you come, come to the hospital.”

“The hospital. Got it.”

“Why couldn’t you call? Two months.”

“They wouldn’t let us. Because of location. Today’s the first time it’s lifted, just for a day. Maryam, time’s up. They’re pushing behind me. I lo —”

Cut off.

Only the hum was left. She stood with the receiver to her ear. The line had taken the word that came next. It didn’t matter. She knew the rest of it. War cut off sentences. It couldn’t cut off what they meant.

Her colleague took the receiver and set it down. There were four of them in the nurses’ station. Nobody spoke. They had all seen it, all heard it. The head nurse brought a glass of water and put it in her hand. Only then did Maryam sit. None of the four knew whose phone would ring next.

The crying came a long while later. Without a sound. In seventy days she hadn’t cried. Afraid that if she cried, he would become dead. Now she could cry.

Forty seconds. In them he had said three things. That he was all right. That what was hit last week wasn’t his company. And their daughter’s name. She held on to the second. Not our company. So another company had been hit. Next to him. And he was still there. On the coast. In a place that smelled of oil. In a place where nobody knew which company would be hit next.

A minute after learning he was alive, she began to be afraid in a new way. Not knowing had been one lump of darkness. Knowing was a daily count. Today he’s alive. Tomorrow. In the 1980s the wives of this country kept that count for eight years.

That evening in the basement she sat her daughter on her lap.

“Baba called.”

Nariman looked at her mother’s face a long time. A seven-year-old doesn’t take long to tell whether a grown-up is lying.

“Really?”

“Really. I heard his voice. He says he’ll bring you a shell.”

“Did you ask his name?”

“I didn’t ask. He said ‘It’s me’ and I knew.”

The girl weighed that answer carefully. Then she nodded. Grandpa’s rule now had one exception. Someone whose voice you know, you don’t have to ask their name. On the other side of the world, two people were recognizing each other not by name but by one book.

Not knowing had been one lump of darkness. Knowing was a daily count. Today he’s alive.

Pyeongtaek, Gyeonggi Province, South Korea — Camp Humphreys, a windowless conference room
May 13, 2026 (Wed.) · 2:00 p.m. (Korea time) — Ceasefire Day 36 · War Day 75
Scene 3 / 5

It was exactly as Park Sejun had said. In a windowless room, the terms of an alliance were about to be rewritten.

Across the table sat two people. A man in his forties and a woman in her thirties. The man wore a suit and gave his name only as Cole. The moment she heard his voice, Kang Yujin knew. The voice that didn’t get angry. The one Park Sejun said he had heard for the first time. The woman wore jeans and a gray cardigan, and her laptop was covered with stickers. Priya Narayan. In charge of operations on their side, she said.

Yoon Jaeha sat on Kang Yujin’s left. His name card said Interpreter. He didn’t interpret once.

Cole slid two sheets of paper across the table.

“Let’s settle the procedural matter first. On April 28 your operator suspended outbound transmission without notice. About thirty-six hours. The memorandum has no unilateral suspension clause. To prevent a recurrence, we’ve prepared a supplementary agreement changing control of outbound transmission to joint management. Suspension will require simultaneous approval by both sides’ operators. Two pages. If you’d review and sign, we can move to the next item.”

Kang Yujin looked down at the paper. Two pages. Park Sejun had signed four. Looking only at the number. A small country’s signature always went on top of a big country’s draft.

She read. Slowly. Not skipping a line. Cole didn’t look at his watch. A man used to waiting. It was in Article 3, paragraph 2. Execution of the outbound suspension function (suspend outbound) requires simultaneous authentication by the designated operators of both sides.

Simultaneous authentication. A button you can’t press unless the other side agrees. That wasn’t a button. It was a picture of a button. Sovereignty sometimes became a picture like that too.

“I won’t sign.” Her voice was small. The room heard it loud.

Cole’s eyebrows moved slightly.

“May I ask why?”

“Because the function was designed as an emergency measure in case of a security incident. An emergency measure has to be executable by one person immediately. An emergency stop that needs two people’s consent isn’t an emergency stop. It’s like not putting two padlocks on a factory’s emergency stop button.”

“The reason your side pressed that button wasn’t a security incident.”

“It was a security incident.” She was surprised her voice didn’t shake. “The model’s output was coming back into the model’s input. What the model predicted was being carried out because of the prediction, and the model was seeing that and raising its confidence. From zero point three four to zero point seven one. That’s an integrity incident in the system. As the operator, I had a duty to stop it.”

Cole didn’t answer. Beside him, Priya Narayan looked up from her laptop for the first time.

“From zero point seven one, where is it now?”

“Zero point five two. The May 2 table didn’t come true.”

“I saw. And format v2,” Priya said. Her English was fast. “Putting UNLIKELY in front of the content box. You built that, right? For six months I wrote fourteen emails asking them to add a confidence box to our form. Not one answer. A week after you changed the format, the box appeared.”

“Ms. Narayan,” Cole said quietly.

“It’s true, Cole.” Priya looked at Kang Yujin. “One more thing. The annotation you sent on April 25. Nobody read it. There was no box to receive it, so it fell into a dead-letter folder. I found it on May 1. After everything had happened. I read it and didn’t sleep for two days. I’m sorry. I volunteered for this trip to say that.”

The conference room was quiet. Kang Yujin looked at the woman’s face. The laptop with stickers. The shadows under her eyes. Someone in an operations room on the far side of the earth who had been looking at the same screen. For five months she had thought of the far end as a wall. Then as a voice that didn’t get angry. She hadn’t known someone like this was sitting there. Even inside a big institution there was a small conscience. Usually in the lowest seat.

“Thank you,” she said. Then she took out what she had brought. One sheet of paper. Handwritten. “I came to make two requests too. One. Send back to this side a record of the actions your side carried out on the basis of the model’s output. What, and when. The content can be redacted. Only whether it was executed and the time. With that, the model can learn to tell what happened because of what it said from what just happened.”

“That’s operational information,” Cole said.

“Two. Take the analytic products coming in through LNO-INGEST out of the input. Documents written by your analysts. Three or four a day in November, two hundred a day now. The model was built to read Iran. Now it reads what your people intend to do and puts that out as a prediction. That’s where the loop comes from.”

Priya started to say something. Cole raised a hand to stop her.

“Both requests are beyond my authority. I’ll pass them to headquarters.”

“Please pass them on. In writing. And please put in the minutes that I made the requests.”

That was the sentence Yoon Jaeha had taught her. Make sure it stays on paper that someone asked. For the first time Cole looked at Kang Yujin a long time. Then he looked at Yoon Jaeha. The man with the interpreter card who hadn’t said a word.

“It will go in the minutes,” Cole said. “So will the refusal to sign the supplementary agreement.”

“Please put it in.”

The meeting ended after forty minutes. In the corridor Priya Narayan caught up with Kang Yujin. Cole was walking well ahead.

“The second request,” Priya said quickly. “I can’t take them out. I don’t even know who said to put them in. But the tags are in my hands. I can put a separate mark on analytic products when I send them. Then you could block the model from putting out predictions based on input with that mark, right? Read it, but no tables.”

“I can. In a day.”

“Then I’ll start tagging next week. Without telling anyone.” The woman smiled. Mischief crossed her tired face. “You didn’t change the form, you changed how to fill it. I’m going to do the same.” For the first time the two operators stood on the same side. The names of the two countries weren’t in that place.

An emergency stop that needs simultaneous authentication. That wasn’t a button. It was a picture of a button.

May 13, 2026 (Wed.) · Evening (Bahrain local time) — Ceasefire Day 36
Scene 4 / 5

Off Oman, a small ship went down. A big war swallowed the small ships first.

On May 13 the Indian-flagged small cargo ship Haji Ali reported being hit by a drone or missile off the coast of Oman and then sank. Omani authorities rescued all 14 crew. The day before, on May 12, ahead of a state visit to China, President Trump was asked by a reporter how much Americans’ financial situations factored into negotiations with Iran. He answered, “Not even a little bit.” The only thing that mattered, he said, was that Iran can’t have a nuclear weapon.

Lieutenant Kim Junho read the ship’s particulars on the screen. Small. Not a tenth the tonnage of the ships on his list. A ship that went from port to port in the Gulf carrying rice and cement and used cars. Fourteen crew. Before the war nobody knew the names of ships like that. Descendants of the dhows that had crossed this sea for a thousand years.

All fourteen were pulled out. He read the line twice. Oman’s coast guard had done it. Not the U.S. Navy, not the Iranian navy.

Lieutenant Commander Mark Henderson was quiet that day. Only toward evening did he speak.

“You hear what the president said yesterday?”

“I heard.”

“My wife texted me. A picture of the price sign at the gas station in our neighborhood. No words, just the picture. She drives three kids around.”

Kim Junho didn’t know what to say. There was a line to what an American officer could say about his own president’s words in front of an allied liaison officer. Henderson didn’t cross it. He only talked about the gas station picture. That was enough. In the end the price of a war got written on the number board at the neighborhood gas station.

“Sir. That ship.” Kim Junho pointed at the place on the screen where it sank. “What hit that ship — is that unidentified too?”

“Drone or missile. That’s all.”

“It’s an Indian ship. India isn’t on anyone’s side in this war.”

“Neither is your country.”

There was no barb in it. It was a fact. Kim Junho nodded. Korea wasn’t on anyone’s side. It had only sent a liaison officer to this building, kept twenty-five ships on that sea, and one of them had been hit. Not being on a side didn’t mean not getting hit. Neutrality was a declaration, and the sea didn’t read declarations.

He wrote in his notebook. 5.13 Indian-flagged small cargo ship Haji Ali hit and sunk off Oman. All 14 rescued (Omani authorities). Launching party unidentified.

Unidentified. He counted how many times he had written that word this month. Five.

He drew a line under it. Then he turned to the list and added up the crew of all twenty-five ships. A sum he had never done before. One hundred thirty-six. Fewer than the government’s “about one hundred sixty.” It was the number left after crossing off, ship by ship, the men who had rotated off or moved to other vessels. He wrote that number at the very top of the list. Not the sum of tonnage. Not the sum of cargo value. For the first time a trading nation’s ledger began with a number of people.

136.

From that day he changed the first line of the daily report home. Not 25 of our ships in the Gulf but 136 of our sailors in the Gulf (25 ships). Nobody asked why. Three days later the first line of the reply from the Joint Chiefs had changed the same way.

Not being on a side didn’t mean not getting hit. From that day he wrote the number of people, not ships, on the report’s first line.

Arlington, Virginia — the chief’s backyard
May 13, 2026 (Wed.) · Evening EDT — Ceasefire Day 36
Scene 5 / 5 — Final

The chief said he’d had lunch that day. It seemed to have been a long lunch.

When Leila Shirazi came in through the side gate he was setting up tomato stakes. The seedlings had grown a hand’s span. He brushed off his hands and without a word took a paper from his shirt pocket and handed it to her.

A palm-sized paper. A little bigger than the one she had sent. Fountain pen. A man’s hand. Persian in a neat, old-fashioned script.

She read it.

’Tis bitter cold, and I am sick at heart.

The letters shook. It was the paper shaking. Her hand.

She knew that sentence. Her father’s study. A book with its cover gone. Her father always lowered his voice at that passage. What the sentry says as he leaves. Thanks for the relief, it’s bitter cold, and he is sick at heart for no reason. As a little girl she asked: Why is he sick? Her father said, Who knows. He doesn’t know either. But there are nights like that. Nights when something is about to happen.

It got there. The note got there, and he read it, and he recognized it. The book she had hidden in the question. And he answered with the next line of the same book. Twenty days ago she hadn’t even known whether he’d read that play. She had bet on something implausible. And he had answered with something even more implausible. Not code, not intelligence. That it’s cold. That he is sick at heart. Between two countries that had called each other devils for forty-seven years, a play had become a bridge.

A machine wouldn’t pick an answer like this. That’s why this answer was a person’s.

Below it, after a skipped line, there were three more lines in the same hand.

From the one who carried it: He asked. His hand shook. He is alive, and sitting in his own chair.

Leila read those three lines and sat down in the worn chair in the yard. Her legs gave out. Her body was relieved before her mind.

Alive. Sitting in his own chair. Since the night of April 7 she hadn’t been able to shake, for a single day, the thought that he might have been dragged into some room underground. For more than a month. And the person who wrote this paper — the mailman in Islamabad — had known. What the woman across the sea would most want to know. So he added three lines to someone else’s letter.

“It’s an acknowledgment,” she said. Her throat was tight. “You said the mailman doesn’t send one.”

“This mailman does, it seems.” The chief tied twine around a stake as he spoke. He didn’t look at her. Not looking when there was no need to look was his courtesy. “The lunch was worth it.”

After a long while she laid the paper on her knee and spoke.

“Tomorrow at six in the morning, one twenty-seven goes up in that sky. Rowell approved it. I objected.”

The chief’s hand stopped. The twine went slack between his fingers.

“He got the words not to turn anything on at the top of the hour. And he even sent an answer.”

“Yes.”

“Then tomorrow he’ll know it isn’t you. The person who told him not to turn anything on wouldn’t turn something on. He won’t respond.”

“He won’t respond. Then the machine will learn zero point eight three was wrong. That’s fine.” She picked up the paper again. “What I’m worried about is after that. How will Rowell read it? The source didn’t answer even the right code. Then that room will conclude one of two things. The source has been caught, or the source has turned. Either way ZARRIN stops being a valuable source.”

“And a source that’s lost its value —”

“Doesn’t get protected.”

The chief finished tying the twine. He pulled the knot tight twice.

“There’s one thing you have now that room doesn’t. That paper. That he’s alive, not caught, not turned. You know it and that room doesn’t. And you can’t tell that room. The heaviest load in intelligence is always that kind of thing. Knowing and not being able to say.”

“Yes.”

“Then there’s one thing for you to do. Before that room reaches the wrong conclusion, get it to know he’s alive by a road it can accept. A road that isn’t paper.”

“Is there a road like that?”

“I don’t know. I’m on leave.” For the first time he looked at her and smiled. “But for the first time in sixteen years you’ve heard that man’s voice, haven’t you? That it’s cold and he’s sick at heart. An analyst who’s heard that isn’t the same analyst as before.”

The sun went down. Leila folded the paper and put it inside her wallet, behind her mother’s photo. In Tehran it would be past three in the morning now. In three hours a light would come on in that sky. A light she wasn’t turning on. The man on the mountain would see it and think: That is not the one.

That there was someone who would think that. That on the other side of the world there was one person who would tell her apart from the machine. She couldn’t find a name for what that was. It wasn’t love. It was someone whose face she didn’t know. But it was closer to that than anything she had received in sixteen years in this building. What two countries had cut, two people were joining.

A machine wouldn’t pick an answer like this. Neither code nor intelligence. That it’s cold, and that he is sick at heart for no reason.

Background Analysis
Ships With No Names — the Haji Ali

On May 13, off Oman, the Indian-flagged small cargo ship Haji Ali was hit by a drone or missile and sank. Omani authorities rescued all 14 crew. By then the list of civilian ships attacked or seized in this war had already grown long. The Al Salmi and Aqua 1 at the end of March; the Touska, Epaminondas, MSC Francesca and Euphoria in April; the Namu, San Antonio, Hasna, Ocean Koi, Sea Star III and Sevda in May.

Sometimes the attacker was the United States, sometimes Iran, and sometimes it was never established. The flags were Panama, India, a French company, a Korean company, Iran, and the crews’ nationalities were more varied still. Most of them were not citizens of the countries at war. On May 14, 38 nautical miles off Fujairah, the Honduran-flagged Huichuan would be seized by people who boarded without authorization and taken toward Iran.

On May 12 President Trump said the only thing that mattered in the negotiations was that Iran can’t have a nuclear weapon. When transit through the strait would resume, when the sailors inside the Gulf would get out — those weren’t in that sentence. The five scenes in this episode — the channel opinion, the forty-second call, the meeting in Pyeongtaek, Kim Junho’s count, the note that came back — are all the author’s invention.
