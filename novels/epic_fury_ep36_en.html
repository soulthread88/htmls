Scene 1 / 5

It was Memorial Day. The building was half empty. Even on the day for honoring the dead, someone had to read the numbers of the living.

Leila Shirazi came in. Because it was Monday. In Tehran the weekly checklist went up every Monday morning. What Rowell had told her to watch. The number written by the new hand.

Sam Farrell was in too. Without asking, he brought up the raw-collection window on her screen. Then he went back to his desk and unwrapped a sandwich.

The form came up. She read it.

Eastern Sector 3 signals room weekly check. May 25. Items 1–14 normal. Item 15, dormant line Q counter: 419. Entered by: Acting Signals Officer Second Lieutenant Jahangiri. Confirmed: sector commander Colonel Sepahvand.

Four hundred nineteen. One number turned over seven weeks.

She brought up the last seven weeks side by side. April 6, 417. April 13, 417. The 20th, the 27th, May 4, the 11th, the 18th. All 417. Under the dead captain’s name.

And today, 419.

Up by two. A line recorded for seven weeks as unused was suddenly recorded as used twice. She knew when those two times were. Before dawn on March 28 and before dawn on March 30. The two times the man on the mountain had spoken to the other side.

The new hand had written the truth. The truth had become the most dangerous document.

It took her a long time to think through what it meant. There were three possibilities.

One. The new signals officer, the second lieutenant, knew nothing and wrote down the counter as he saw it. The colonel, confirming it, couldn’t correct it. If he told him to change it, the second lieutenant would ask why. In that case this was an accident. The cover had fallen away along with the man.

Two. The colonel had pulled the cover off himself. Because he didn’t want the lie left under a dead man’s name anymore. In that case this was a decision. Toward what, she didn’t know.

Three. The number had been written in advance by the dead man. It could be. She held that thought, then let it go. It was something there was no way to know.

Either way, the number was now in a file at that country’s higher headquarters. Seven sheets of four hundred seventeen, then one sheet of four hundred nineteen. The day someone in counterintelligence put them side by side, the questions would come. Why was it wrong for seven weeks? Who wrote it wrong? Why? A bureaucracy did not forget. It only remembered late.

He had signed it as confirmed knowing that. Under his own name.

On May 25 U.S. forces struck southern Iran. U.S. Central Command said it had targeted missile launch sites and Iranian boats attempting to lay mines, acting defensively and with restraint. The U.S. military called it self-defense and said the ceasefire remained in place. Several Iranian service members were reported killed. The same day President Trump said the talks were going well.

Around eleven the wall screen changed. Bandar Abbas. Missile launch sites. Boats that had tried to lay mines. Self-defense. The people left in the building looked up, then looked down again. It had been one day since word came that a deal was close.

Leila looked at that screen, then at the four hundred nineteen on her own. Two things had come the same morning. Her country had hit that country’s southern coast, and on a mountain in that country’s north a man had put the truth into his own paperwork with his own hand. A big country’s bombs and one man’s honesty carried the same date.

She wrote a memo for Rowell. Short.

5.25 weekly checklist: dormant line counter 417 → 419. New author (second lieutenant); source signed as confirming. Cannot determine among three interpretations (exposure through the author’s ignorance / deliberate correction by the source / advance entry by the predecessor). Common conclusion: line use on March 28 and 30 is now in Iran’s official record. Source’s risk of exposure is higher than last week. No probability given.

No probability given. She didn’t delete that line. Three months ago she could not have written it.

After she sent it she looked at the ceiling of the windowless room. The second note had been on its way for seven days. It had two more weeks to go. When it comes, may you be underground. What that man needed to worry about now might not be the sky. The threat came from the sky, and from the files.

Seven sheets of four hundred seventeen, then one sheet of four hundred nineteen. The new hand wrote the truth. From across the ocean there was no telling whether it was an accident or a decision.

Scene 2 / 5

The name came up in a news alert for the second time. The same city, hit twice.

Bandar Abbas.

This time Maryam wasn’t holding a medication tray. She had finished the night shift and was drinking tea in the nurses’ station. Words ran along the bottom of the television. U.S. forces. Southern coast. Missile launch sites. And one more line. Several of our servicemen martyred.

Several.

The word wasn’t a number. It could be three or thirty. There were no names, no units. Something on the coast had been hit, and some of the people there had died. That was all. The state announced deaths in words, and families had to find their own people inside the words.

Thirteen days ago he had said, This place got hit last week, but it wasn’t our company. That time it wasn’t. This time.

She set down her teacup. Not because her hand was shaking. Her hand was strangely still. She had held on for seventy days without knowing, heard forty seconds of his voice, and since then had lived thirteen days counting every day. Alive today. Tomorrow? Tonight the counting stopped. Until there was an answer.

No call came. They said military phones had been opened for one day only. The internet had been cut for nearly three months. The family support office opened at nine in the morning. A country was keeping its own people cut off from news.

She didn’t go down to the basement. She couldn’t face her daughter. The girl read her mother’s face. Instead she went back to the ward and helped with the next shift’s work. She changed IV bags, took temperatures, held the hand of an old man who couldn’t sleep. Work didn’t keep the thoughts away. It only gave her hands something to do. That was how this country’s nurses had held on through eight years of war.

At four in the morning the head nurse sat her down in a chair.

“Come on. Even an hour.”

“When I close my eyes I see it.”

“See what?”

“His shoulder. The left one. Where they stitched it in March. I still haven’t seen the scar.”

The head nurse said nothing and put a blanket over her. In this hospital comfort was not words but a blanket.

On May 25 Iranian President Pezeshkian ordered an end to the internet shutdown. Since the war began, Iran had allowed access only to government-approved users, and the restrictions lasted until May 26. On May 26 Iran’s foreign ministry condemned the previous day’s U.S. attack as a ceasefire violation and a sign of bad faith and unreliability, and said no act of aggression would go unanswered.

It was half past twelve on Tuesday afternoon.

Someone in the nurses’ station shouted. Not a scream. It works! It’s getting through! A young nurse was holding her phone up. Green speech bubbles were pouring down the screen. Three months’ worth. A whole country’s blocked voices burst out at once.

Maryam took her phone from the pocket of her gown. For three months she had used it only as a clock. She switched it on. It found a signal. A number came up. Four hundred twelve unread. Relatives. Friends. A cousin in Canada. She pushed them up. To the top. To the most recent.

Today. 12:14. An unknown number.

A single photo. It came before any words.

It was a palm. A man’s palm, callused. She knew those lines. On it lay a seashell. The size of a thumbnail, white with brown stripes. Behind it, a little sand and some cloudy water. A seven-year-old’s request had crossed the coast of a war and come back.

One line beneath it.

Walked till I couldn’t smell oil and found it. For Nariman. I’m okay. What got hit yesterday was the west pier. We’re east.

Maryam slid down the wall to the floor. Phone held to her chest. Someone called her name. She shook her head. It meant she was okay. She couldn’t get the word out.

In the basement classroom Nariman looked at the photo a long time. She spread the screen with two fingers and made the shell bigger.

“It’s a real shell.”

“It’s a real shell.”

“Is that Baba’s hand?”

“That’s Baba’s hand.”

“There’s something on his hand. Black stuff.”

“It’s oil. Baba says it smells like oil where he works.”

The girl shrank the screen again. So the whole palm showed. Then she held her own palm up beside the screen. She stayed like that a long time. After three months, she was measuring her father’s hand.

“Maman. What about the men on the west pier?”

Maryam couldn’t answer. The seven-year-old had read the back half of the sentence. What got hit yesterday was the west pier. We’re east. Then who was on the west side? One family’s relief sat in the same sentence as another family’s death notice.

Professor Azad Moradi heard the question at the blackboard. He was a man who had buried his nephew five days before. He came and sat down beside the girl.

“Somebody was waiting for a shell from the men on the west pier too.”

“Then those kids don’t get one?”

“No. They don’t.”

Nariman gave the phone back to her mother. Then she took out her colored pencils and began drawing seashells on a sheet of paper. Not one. Many. Azad didn’t ask. He knew who she meant them for. A seven-year-old was counting the people the grown-ups’ war had forgotten.

“What about the men on the west pier?” The seven-year-old read the back half of the sentence. We’re east. Then who was on the west side?

Lieutenant Kim Junho wrote the word in his notebook and put it in quotation marks. Quotation marks were the doubt he had learned.

“Self-defense.”

On May 26 Secretary of State Rubio said negotiations to extend the ceasefire and reopen the Strait of Hormuz would take a few more days, with a lot of back-and-forth over the specific wording of an initial document. He said the strait had to be open without tolls and without interference. Iran’s Parliament Speaker Qalibaf went to Qatar for the talks. Iran’s Revolutionary Guards claimed they had shot down or driven off drones and fighter jets that entered Iranian airspace. That morning an explosion was reported aboard a tanker in the Gulf of Oman. No one was hurt and the cause was not determined. On May 27 U.S. forces attacked a base on Iran’s southern coast again, and Iran launched several drones.

A liaison officer’s job was carrying words across. When the U.S. military wrote self-defense, he put down the Korean word for self-defense. When it wrote the ceasefire remains in place, he put down that the ceasefire was in effect. His job was to set those two sentences side by side in one report. The contradiction between them was not his to resolve. The language of war was always two-layered like that.

But as three months went by he learned that carrying words across was not enough. The people in Seoul who read his reports wanted to know what the words meant. Was an attack with the word self-defense on it a resumption of the war or not? Could the ships leave or not? The translator had to become an interpreter.

He asked Lieutenant Commander Mark Henderson.

“Sir. What was that yesterday? In your words.”

“Self-defense.”

“In your words, sir.”

Henderson leaned back in his chair and looked at the ceiling a long while. The silence of an officer who has to put his own country’s word into his own words.

“They were going to lay mines. Really. I saw that on the screen myself. In the water our ships use. Leave it and next week one of our destroyers runs over one. So we hit them. That’s self-defense, sure.” He let out a breath. “But hitting the launch sites too, well. You don’t need to hit launchers on land to stop a boat laying mines. That was while we were at it. Because the chance was there.”

“Is the ceasefire still in effect?”

“As long as they don’t hit back big. They’ve got people in Doha now too, so they can’t hit back big. Neither side wants to flip the table. And both sides want to take a little more. Before the signing.”

“So the last few days before the signing are the most dangerous.”

“Always. My father said the twelve days a month before they signed the Paris accords were when the most bombs fell on Hanoi. At Christmas.”

Kim Junho didn’t write that down. He wrote in his own words instead.

Assessment 5.25–26: Both sides jockeying for position just before a deal. The “self-defense” strike is not a collapse of the ceasefire but pressure before signing. However, the risk that pressure turns into miscalculation grows as the signing nears. Continue consultations on our ships’ transit, but choose transit times when U.S. and Iranian activity at sea is low. Note the Gulf of Oman tanker explosion (5.26, cause unknown).

He read it over. Sentences he couldn’t have written three months ago. There was judgment in them. Judgment that could be wrong. And he was ready to write that he would correct it if it was. A small country’s officer had learned how to turn a big country’s words into his own country’s judgment.

In the evening a text came. The captain whose daughter was taking the Suneung. Lieutenant. The company has a date. First week of June. This time it came on paper.

Kim Junho checked with the embassy. It was right. Consultations with Iran had finished for one more ship. He wrote back. Confirmed. It’s right. I’ll congratulate you after you’re out of the strait. Until then add lookouts, and dawn would be a good time to go.

The last few days before the signing are the most dangerous. Because neither side wants to flip the table, and both sides want to take a little more.

Scene 4 / 5

The counterintelligence lieutenant colonel brought the forensics results. One sheet.

One sheet. Colonel Darius Sepahvand read it. Left front brake hose ruptured. Findings consistent with age-related deterioration. Signs of external tampering: undetermined. Secondary damage from vehicle rollover prevents detailed examination.

“Undetermined.” A man’s death was about to be closed with one word.

“I expected that.” The lieutenant colonel didn’t sit. “And I found the four men. General Staff Signals Directorate. Listed as a technical audit team. Their coming to this command post was an order that came down a different chain from my investigation. Who gave it, that’s where it stops. My authority doesn’t let me see above that.”

“So.”

“So this is as far as it goes. The back door in the switch, we leave alone and watch. The captain’s death gets closed as an accident. The four men are out of my reach. Tomorrow I go back to Tehran and take another case.” The lieutenant colonel’s voice was flat. He was working to keep it flat. “I came to say goodbye, Colonel. I’ve been a burden for nearly two months.”

Darius looked at him. The man who had taken the second lieutenant down on March 30. The man who had brought the second lieutenant back on April 19. The man who had laid four sheets side by side. Who had asked where the seven seconds came from. And who last week had put his head under the car hauled up from the valley and shone a flashlight. Even the hunter had a conscience.

There was no paper in his drawer. The paper was with the old man in Evin. What he had was one question.

“Lieutenant Colonel. In two months I never once asked your name.”

“You did not, sir.”

“What is it?”

Something passed over the lieutenant colonel’s face. Counterintelligence people don’t give their names. That was their armor. To ask a name was to ask a man to take off his armor.

“Taheri. Naser Taheri.”

“Lieutenant Colonel Taheri. Sit down. There’s one thing I want to ask you. Something you should think about a long time before you answer.”

Naser Taheri sat. For the first time in two months, when asked.

“That place above, where you said it stops. There is a sheet of paper that says what is there. I didn’t write it. Captain Moradi wrote it. Four days before he died. I read it three days ago.”

Taheri didn’t move. Only his eyes moved.

“The captain didn’t tell me.”

“Why he didn’t tell you is written there too. It says this: ‘He is a good man, but if he knew this, he would walk toward the General Staff and not come back.’”

The room was quiet. Outside the window the sun hung on the ridge. A sentence in which a dead man had tried to protect a living one stayed in the room.

After a long while Taheri passed a hand once over his face. It was the biggest movement he had made in front of Darius in two months.

“So that’s how he saw me.”

“That’s why I’m asking. The paper isn’t in my hands now. It’s somewhere safe. If you say you’ll look, you can see it. If you say you won’t, you go back to Tehran tomorrow and take another case. The captain will have died in an accident, and you’ll stay a man who knows nothing. There’s no shame in that. You must have a family.”

“Two daughters.”

“So think a long time. There are things that, once you know them, you can’t turn back from. I can’t decide that for you. The captain didn’t tell you so as not to decide it for you either. Only, I see it a little differently from the captain. I think keeping a man from deciding is different from asking him.”

Taheri looked out the window. A long time. The sun went down behind the ridge. Nineteen years of a career sat on the scales in front of that window.

“Colonel. I’ve done this work for nineteen years. I’ve sat people down in rooms underground and asked them. Tell me what you know. If they didn’t, I asked more. I have never once asked anyone this. Whether they want to know.”

“Three months ago I didn’t know either. That you could ask.”

“How many people have seen that paper?”

“The man who wrote it. And two.”

“The man who wrote it is dead.”

“Yes.”

Taheri stood up. Darius thought he would go. That would be the right choice. For a man with two daughters.

The lieutenant colonel didn’t go to the door. He went to the window. He spoke with his back turned.

“My older girl is fourteen. Last month she asked me what my job was. I said it was finding bad people. She said, what if the bad person is higher up than you, Dad? I couldn’t answer her.” Children’s questions were always more exact than grown-ups’ institutions.

He turned around.

“I’ll look. Tomorrow, on my way to Tehran. Where do I go?”

“I’ve done this work for nineteen years. I have never once asked anyone this. Whether they want to know.”

Northern Tehran, Evin — Professor Azad’s home

Scene 5 / 5 — Final

That day was the first and last time the three of them sat in one room. The one who hunted, the one who was hunted, and the one who read.

Professor Azad Moradi made tea. There were sugar cubes. A neighbor who came to his nephew’s funeral had left a bag. He set them out in front of the three of them. The colonel was in civilian clothes. So was the lieutenant colonel. Two thousand books looked down on them from the walls of the study. A thousand years of sentences watched three men’s single day.

Coming into the room, Naser Taheri swept the walls once with his eyes. A professional’s eyes. Then his gaze stopped on one spine.

“We have your book in our archive too, Professor. As confiscated material. The 1988 one.”

“Did you read it?”

“It isn’t the kind of archive that’s there for reading.” A moment later he added, “I read it. Quietly.” Banned books were always read first by the men who guarded them.

Azad took out the Shahnameh. He drew the envelope from behind the drawing. He set it in front of the lieutenant colonel. Before touching it, Taheri glanced once at the colonel. Darius nodded.

The lieutenant colonel read. The front. The back. The back once more.

His face didn’t change while he read. Nineteen years of training. What changed was his hand. The thumb holding the paper was pressing the corner. White.

When he finished he put the paper down. He was silent a long time. The three men in the room now knew the same thing.

“03:41:52.”

“Yes.”

“After I found the back door, I only looked at what came in through it. When it came in. The shape of the order that came in. I didn’t look at whether the door was opened from inside first. It never occurred to me. The enemy got into our house, and I never asked who opened the door for them. Nineteen years in this work.” He laughed low. It wasn’t a laugh. “He was a signals officer. He saw it in three days.”

“What will you do now?” Azad asked.

“What do you think I’ll do, Professor?”

“My nephew said you would walk to the General Staff.”

“Three weeks ago I would have.” Taheri lifted his teacup. He put it down without drinking. “I’d have walked there and knocked on the door and asked who was sitting at the third terminal before dawn on April 7. And as he said, I wouldn’t have come back. He saw me well. But there was something he didn’t know. Last week, in the valley, I looked under his car. A man who has seen that doesn’t knock on the door.”

“Then what?”

“There’s a duty roster. At the General Staff Signals Directorate. The night watch of April 6. That’s inside the directorate, but the mess records are with logistics, the entry logs are with the guard unit, the vehicle dispatch is with the transport section. Which room a man was in at three-forty in the morning isn’t written on just one piece of paper. I won’t knock on the door. I’ll go around through the rooms next to it. It takes time. Months. Meanwhile I’m a man working another case.”

Listening, Darius remembered how this man had circled him for two months. He never came straight on. The generator room’s fuel log, the signals room’s entry log, the counter’s checklist. He went around through the rooms next door. That skill was now pointed the other way. The hunter had turned toward a different part of the forest.

“And if you find him,” Darius asked. “If you find the man who sat at the third terminal. What will you do with him?”

Before answering, Taheri looked down again at the back of the paper. It may have been selling out the country. Or it may have been to show the people who wanted to fire the missiles that night how naked we were.

“I don’t know. He wrote that he couldn’t tell, either. I can’t tell now. After I find him, after I know what he wanted that night, I’ll decide. But one thing I’ve already decided. Whatever his reason, if to cover it up he pushed a signals officer off a mountain road, that gets counted separately. Even a man who opened the door to save the country — murder is murder.”

Azad nodded. The nod of a teacher who has heard a good answer. That ends do not wash means clean. He had taught that all his life.

“I’ll leave this paper here,” Taheri said. “If I carry it around, it disappears when I die. Please keep it, Professor. And another copy has to be made. Something that exists in only one copy in the world disappears too easily.” Copying by hand was the oldest way of keeping the truth in this land.

“You mean copy it out?”

“In your handwriting, Professor. And keep it outside this country. I hear your daughter is in Canada.”

Azad wasn’t surprised. There was no way a counterintelligence man wouldn’t know.

“You’re telling me to put my daughter into this.”

“Don’t put her into it. Give her a sealed envelope to keep. Tell her not to open it. The way your nephew did with you.” Taheri stood. “The decision is yours, Professor. I’m only advising.”

At the door he turned to the colonel. The two men looked at each other for a moment. For two months one had hunted and one had been hunted.

“Colonel. About the seven seconds.”

Darius’s back went stiff. The seven seconds had come back.

“I’ll put that question in a drawer. The way the captain kept that number in a drawer for seven weeks. I saw Monday’s checklist. Four hundred nineteen. He wrote that in advance, didn’t he. I know the handwriting. Before he died he left your drawer open, Colonel. I’ll keep it shut. As long as I’m alive.”

“Why.”

“Whether that man at the General Staff and you did the same thing, I don’t know. I don’t want to know. This time I decided. Not to know.” He took hold of the door handle. “But now I can answer what my older girl asked. If the bad person is higher up than Dad, you start with the room next door.”

After he left, two men remained in the study. The tea had gone cold. Azad put the paper back in the envelope.

“Colonel. Do you know what that man just did?”

“He let me go.”

“No. He answered what the boy asked before he went. How choosing not to know is different from knowing and keeping silent. That man chose to know one thing and chose not to know another. On the same day. Both to protect someone else.” The old man slipped the envelope between the pages. “For seventy-two years I looked for that in books. I never thought I’d learn it from a counterintelligence lieutenant colonel.”

Background Analysis
“Self-Defense” — Strikes Inside a Ceasefire

On May 25 U.S. forces struck missile launch sites around Bandar Abbas in southern Iran and boats that were attempting to lay mines. A U.S. Central Command spokesman described it as a self-defense measure to protect U.S. forces from threats by the Iranian military, and the U.S. military said the ceasefire remained in place. Several Iranian service members were reported killed. Al Jazeera, citing an anonymous Iranian source, reported that before the U.S. attack the Revolutionary Guards had targeted a vessel at sea.

The day before, President Trump had said the memorandum of understanding was being finalized, and on the day of the strikes he said negotiations were going well. Iran’s foreign ministry condemned it as a ceasefire violation and a sign of bad faith and unreliability. On May 27 U.S. forces hit a base on Iran’s southern coast again, and on May 28 Kuwait intercepted a ballistic missile aimed at it. Iran said it had targeted the U.S. base in Kuwait used in the previous day’s attack.

The ceasefire began on April 8 and on paper runs until July 8. But through the shelling of the Touska on April 19, the destruction of small boats on May 4, and the strikes on the mainland on May 7, 25 and 27, “ceasefire,” “self-defense” and “terminated” had become words that each did a different job. The scenes in this episode — the number on the checklist, the photo of the seashell, the three men in the study in Evin — are all the author’s invention.
