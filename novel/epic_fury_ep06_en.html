Washington, D.C. — Eisenhower Executive Office Building, National Security Council (NSC) Middle East directorate
March 16, 2026 (Mon.) · 9:40 p.m. EDT — War Day 17
Scene 1 / 9

There was a map on the wall. There were supposed to be flag pins in it.

Brian Collins was senior director for the Middle East at the National Security Council. Fifty-two. His job was to turn what the president had said two days ago into reality. A list of countries that would send warships to Hormuz. In two days he had made twenty-three phone calls. In the winter of 1991 there had not been enough pins for walls like this. This year the box of pins sat on the desk unopened.

On March 16, China and European NATO members refused President Trump’s demand for military support to reopen the Strait of Hormuz. The next day, March 17, Trump posted on Truth Social that he did not need NATO’s help, and criticized Japan, South Korea, and Australia, U.S. allies in the Indo-Pacific that had not joined the U.S.-led attack on Iran. He wrote that the United States did not need anyone’s help in this war.

The sentences of refusal looked alike. Constitutional procedure. Parliamentary consent. Deep concern for regional stability. Collins had been reading sentences like that for thirty years. He had written them to other countries himself. Translated, they meant one thing. You started this.

“Europe’s out. Beijing never meant to come,” the deputy director said from the doorway. “Tokyo, Seoul, and Canberra—same answer.”

Collins did not nod. It was what he had expected. An alliance is not a friendship. It is arithmetic. And the arithmetic always starts in your own country’s ports. Those countries had tankers trapped in the Gulf right now. The moment they sent warships, those ships became the next targets. India had gotten two ships out with one phone call. Everyone had seen it.

He put the box of pins in the drawer. Then he wrote the title of the one-page memo that would be on the president’s desk tomorrow morning. Coalition Status — None.

A war you start alone, you have to end alone. It sounds like a declaration of strength, but it is the loneliest arithmetic there is.

Kuwait City — Salmiya residential district
March 16, 2026 (Mon.) · 11:47 p.m. local time — night of War Day 17
Scene 2 / 9

The children were asleep.

Fatima al-Rashid had put her three children to bed and was sitting in the living room. A siren went off outside. At first she thought it was an ambulance. Since the war started every sound had sounded different. An engine became a missile, a door closing became an explosion.

For seventeen nights the cities of the Gulf had heard sirens. In Kuwait a drone set a high-rise in Kuwait City on fire on March 8, and on March 14 the radar at the international airport was damaged and three soldiers were wounded at Ahmad al-Jaber Air Base. Before dawn on March 16 a drone hit a fuel tank at Dubai International Airport and set it on fire, a fire broke out at the port of Fujairah, and a rocket killed one person near Abu Dhabi.

There was a dull sound far off. The windows shook. She got up and ran to the children’s room. The youngest was crying. Five years old. He did not know why he was crying. Only that something was frightening. That was enough.

Fatima held him and looked out the window. Lights came on in every window of the building across the street. In every window someone stood the same way. Holding a child, looking up at the sky. It was not one family’s night. It was a whole city’s.

This country was on no side in this war. Her husband worked at a bank and she was a pharmacist. They had no grudge against Tehran or Washington. But there were American bases on this country’s soil. That one fact made this country’s night sky a target. In the summer of 1990 she was seven. Things had come out of the sky then too. She had lived her whole life on one thought: she would not hand that summer down to her children.

War happens between soldiers. But the ones who cry in its dark are always the children.

Langley, Virginia — CIA Iran Operations Center
March 16, 2026 (Mon.) · 10:50 p.m. EDT / 6:20 a.m. March 17 in Tehran — predawn, War Day 18
Scene 3 / 9

A name moved from one list on the screen to the other.

Dr. Leila Shirazi watched it move. The list on the left was headed Active. The list on the right, Removal Pending Confirmation. The name that moved was Ali Larijani.

Before dawn on March 17, an Israeli Air Force night strike killed Ali Larijani, secretary of Iran’s Supreme National Security Council, and Basij commander Gholamreza Soleimani. Three hundred Basij officers were reported killed with them. Iranian state media later confirmed both deaths. Larijani had been speaker of parliament (2008–2020) and chief nuclear negotiator, and on the day after Khamenei’s death he was the one who announced the formation of the interim leadership council.

Leila had been reading that man for sixteen years. Speeches, interviews, parliamentary transcripts. A doctorate in philosophy. A politician who took his degree on Kant. A man from a hard-line family who had ended up in the negotiator’s chair. She had never liked him. But his sentences were always worth reading to the end. There was calculation in them. You can bargain with someone who calculates.

“Got a big one,” Sam Farrell said behind her. There was no joy in his voice. He was only stating the size.

“Sam,” Leila said. “Of the people who’ve gone to the right, how many have ever sat at a negotiating table?”

Sam did not count. He did not need to.

“Who’s left on the left?”

“The people who shoot,” she said. “And one leader who won’t show his face.”

She thought of her father. The study in Shiraz. Every evening her father read Rumi’s Masnavi aloud. At twelve she had learned the first lines by heart too. Listen to the reed and the tale it tells, how it sings of separation since it was cut from the reed bed. Her father said the reed was everyone who had ever left home. The year they packed their bags to emigrate, that was not a joke anymore.

Now she read another meaning in it. A reed has to be cut before it makes a sound. Cut a man down and he goes silent. But from the place where he stood, another sound comes. Whether it is the sound of bargaining or a scream, the one who did the cutting does not get to decide. In 1953 this agency erased one prime minister in Tehran. This country heard the sound that came from that place twenty-six years later.

She wrote the first line of the report. Larijani removal—tactical success confirmed. Strategic assessment: pool of authorized interlocutors on the other side for war-termination talks effectively exhausted. In a war that bombing cannot end, we are erasing the people who could say end it.

On the right-hand screen Fordow had been turning for seventeen days. The number of people who could have talked about that mountain had just dropped by one.

Erasing an enemy’s leaders is easy. The hard part is leaving someone to sign the papers on the day the war ends.

Bahrain — Mina Salman Port, the coalition liaison cell at the headquarters of the U.S. Fifth Fleet
March 17, 2026 (Tue.) · 9:15 a.m. local time — War Day 18
Scene 4 / 9

Lieutenant Kim Junho saw the name on the morning status board.

Larijani. A name he knew. The man who had been Iran’s negotiator. A name he had heard in an international-relations class at the naval academy. Now the name was in the box marked Presumed Dead.

The U.S. Navy liaison officer next to him, Lieutenant Commander Mark Henderson, handed him a coffee. Close to forty. The man in this room who talked to Kim more than anyone.

“Hear your country’s not coming,” Henderson said. It was not an accusation. He said it the way you talk about the weather.

“I’m not in a position to know that decision.”

“I know. I find out what Washington decides from the news too.” Henderson pointed his chin at the board. “Our president’ll post something soon. Korea, Japan, Australia. Ungrateful. So what do you think your president says?”

“Probably nothing.”

Henderson laughed low. It was a bitter laugh.

“That’s diplomacy. Saying nothing is saying something.”

Kim did not nod. He knew that was diplomacy. In the summer of 1950 sixteen countries had sent troops for his country. He had learned it in school. But on this morning, in front of this board, with names moving from box to box, he was not sure that saying nothing was right. Two days ago he had sent Seoul three paragraphs. No answer had come. That no answer is an answer too, he was learning.

He went back to his desk and started calling out ship names. This morning one ship had reported six days of water left.

Watching is not the same as staying out. Watching is a choice too, and choices come with a price.

Ramat Gan, Israel — residential neighborhood
March 17, 2026 (Tue.) · morning, local time — War Day 18
Scene 5 / 9

The alarm went off.

David Levy was seventy-two. Thirteen in 1967, a tank crewman in 1973, in 2006 a father who sent his son to the northern front. He had heard too many alarms. In the winter of 1991 Scuds had fallen on this neighborhood. Still, in this war he went down every time. These missiles were not like the old rockets. They took whole buildings.

He lived on the fourth floor. The shelter was in the basement. Sixty-eight steps. His knees were not what they had been. On the second-floor landing he caught up with the couple from downstairs. They were going down slowly too. Both past eighty. The husband was holding his wife by the elbow.

“Go on ahead, David,” the old man said. “We’ll go at our own pace.”

He went on ahead. For the rest of his life he would remember that sentence.

On March 17 Iran rained missiles down. In Ramat Gan an elderly couple was killed on the way to a shelter. A platform at Tel Aviv’s Savidor Central Station was damaged too. The same day Israel began a ground invasion of southern Lebanon.

The shock came before the sound. The whole stairwell lifted once and settled. Dust. Then the sound went out of his ears. In a world where he could hear nothing, David lay on his face with a hand on the basement threshold. He was alive. He looked back. He could not see the top of the stairs. The dust stood like a wall.

This missile was the answer to the dawn in Tehran. Later he heard the name on the news. Larijani. He did not know exactly who that was. But he came to know this much. Every time this country erased someone in Tehran, within a few days someone on a stairway in this city did not make it down.

Civilians do not choose the war. The war chooses civilians. Starting with the slowest walkers.

South Lebanon — an observation post in the border zone
March 17, 2026 (Tue.) · 2:20 p.m. local time — War Day 18
Scene 6 / 9

The sound of a Merkava’s tracks was much lower than people think.

It did not come raising dust like a sandstorm. It was quiet. Ominously quiet. That quiet froze Mohammad Qasir at the observation post. Twenty-six. The worst was when you could not see the tanks. When you could see them, at least you knew which way to run.

Israel approved ground operations in Lebanon on March 3 and from March 4 fought Hezbollah on the ground along the border. On March 17 it began a ground invasion of southern Lebanon. According to the Lebanese Health Ministry, Israeli attacks from March 2 to 16 killed 886 people and wounded 2,141, among them 38 health workers killed.

Smoke rose along the ridgeline. It was not an airstrike. Airstrikes come down from the sky. This came pushing across the ground. Armor. Infantry. Artillery. The oldest form of war.

Mohammad picked up the radio. The leader whose speeches he had grown up on since he was twelve had died the autumn before last. Since then the orders had gotten shorter, and later. Today’s order was one line. Hold your position.

“They’re coming in. Tanks. Heading north.”

He picked up his rifle. It was everything he had in this war. He could not shoot at the sky. He could not stop a drone. Last week his mother had left for Tyre. One of seven hundred thousand. As she went she pressed the key to the olive grove into his hand. A grove does not have a key. It was the key to the shed. His mother called it the key to the grove.

Was he here for Tehran or for that key? He had never asked himself. Today there was no time to ask.

A border is a line on a map. A tank’s tracks roll over it. The line is erased. Beyond it, a young man was holding the key to a storeroom.

Tehran — an alley in the neighborhood south of Sharif University of Technology
March 17, 2026 (Tue.) · 8 p.m. local time — night of War Day 18 · Chaharshanbe Suri
Scene 7 / 9

The fire caught.

Soraya Tahir dragged the door out of the basement storeroom. It was what she had stacked there six days ago. She set a crate in the middle of the alley, laid chair legs on it, and leaned the door against them. The match lit on the third try.

Before dawn today big men had died in this city. During the day word went around that the Basij headquarters had been hit. The checkpoint at the end of the alley had been empty for six days. Nobody would be coming tonight to stop the fires in this city. Whether that was good or frightening, Soraya could not decide.

Chaharshanbe Suri is the custom of lighting fires and jumping over them on the night before the last Wednesday of the Persian calendar year. In 2026 it fell on the night of Tuesday, March 17. That day in Tehran the Basij headquarters, the Revolutionary Guard garrison in Saadat Abad, and several sports halls were hit in airstrikes, and historic buildings in the Sa’dabad palace complex were damaged. Drone attacks aimed at police stations and checkpoints increased even in densely populated parts of the city.

The fire climbed the door. The alley, its streetlights out, went orange. Windows opened one by one. People came down. A woman holding a child. A man in his undershirt. A grandmother on a cane. Somebody brought another crate. Somebody else dragged over a broken stroller.

Soraya jumped first.

“My yellow to you, your red to me!”

In the half second over the fire the heat touched her legs. When she landed somebody clapped. The next person jumped. Then the next. The grandmother could not jump, so she walked once around the fire. They counted that as a jump.

There was an explosion to the north. One side of the sky flashed. Everyone turned that way at once. That fire and this one. One falling, one lit. After a short silence the man in the undershirt shouted, “That’s their fire!” And he jumped. People laughed. It was a kind of laugh nobody had heard in eighteen days.

Soraya sat by the fire and caught her breath. She thought of the boy. The one cracking sunflower seeds. That checkpoint had not been hit, she decided to believe. The boy was jumping a fire in some alley right now. Believing did not need proof. Tonight was that kind of night.

She jumped once more. Gave the yellow and took the red. Gave the sickness and fear and took the strength of the fire. It was a bargain the people of this land had been making for three thousand years. No king, no cleric, no bomber had been able to break it.

Spring does not get permission. The fire did not ask the government, or the missiles, or eighteen days of war. It just caught.

Seoul — Foreign Ministry, African and Middle Eastern Affairs Bureau
March 18, 2026 (Wed.) · 8:30 a.m. KST / 7:30 p.m. March 17 in Washington — morning of War Day 19
Scene 8 / 9

First Secretary Han Jiwon was looking out the window at Seoul. People on their way to work crossed the Gwanghwamun intersection. Faces that knew nothing. She envied those faces.

Thirty-four. Her third year in the Middle East division. For two days the same question had landed on her desk in twenty different shapes. Send them or not. She was not the one who decided the answer. She was the one who made the papers the deciders would read.

On her monitor was the post that had come in from Washington overnight. The American president’s post. That he did not need NATO. And the names Japan, South Korea, Australia. The division director said as he went by:

“It’ll be settled that we don’t issue an official response. Just rough out a position paper. We won’t be using it.”

Instead of the draft she opened another document. The report from Bahrain three days ago. Three paragraphs a naval lieutenant had written. A paper that had come up the chain and stopped on her desk. She knew the third paragraph by heart. Warships can escort ships, but cannot get out ships that are already trapped.

It was not a sentence for a lieutenant to write. So it was a sentence nobody upstairs would read. Han Jiwon copied the paragraph and pasted it at the very top of her own report. For the source she wrote only “judgment of liaison officer on the scene.” A chain of approval reads rank before it reads the sentence. A sentence that could not go up under the name of a twenty-nine-year-old lieutenant might climb one more floor riding a first secretary’s paper.

She hit save. Outside the window people were still crossing the intersection. Six or seven tenths of the oil they burned came through that strait. Not one face knew it. In the winter of 1997 this intersection had looked the same. Right up to the day before everything fell apart.

Refusal always comes nicely wrapped. The way one country leaves another alone on a battlefield is always a formal silence.

In Tehran a negotiator died.

In Washington the president wrote that he needed no one’s help.

And somewhere on earth millions of people were watching the news and turned off the screen. Not because they could not bear it. Because they had seen so much that they had gone numb.

The cruelest thing about war is not the bombs. A bomb is a moment. War is cruelest—when we get used to it.

The same night, a lab in Eunpyeong-gu, Seoul. Kang Yujin could not turn the variable “negotiation” into a number. She started by counting the people who could talk. The people on both sides who could make a call, take a call, and make a promise stick. Today one dropped out of that number. The model’s output moved a little. Toward more days until the end of the war. On the day that number reached zero, what would this model output?

War Situation
March 16–17 — The People Being Erased

For eighteen days Israel and the United States stripped Iran’s command from the top down. On the first day of the war the Supreme Leader, the defense minister, the chief of staff of the armed forces, and the commander in chief of the Revolutionary Guards were killed. On March 14 two acting heads of intelligence at Khatam al-Anbiya Central Headquarters were killed, and on March 17 Larijani, secretary of the Supreme National Security Council, and Basij commander Soleimani. On March 18 Intelligence Minister Esmail Khatib would follow.

The strikes were concentrated on the capital. On March 16, 65 percent of the day’s strikes were aimed at Tehran, and eleven residential buildings collapsed, including an eight-story building on Zarafshan Street. On March 17 the area near the Bushehr nuclear power plant was hit without damage, and U.S. forces used GBU-72 bunker busters on a site believed to hide antiship missiles.

And the first crack showed inside the United States. On March 17 Joe Kent, director of the National Counterterrorism Center, resigned, saying Iran had not been an imminent threat to the United States. The count of children was rising too. UNICEF said that by March 12 the war had killed 300 children and wounded thousands, that hundreds of thousands had left their homes, and that millions could not go to school.
