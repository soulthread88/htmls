Tehran — Imam Khomeini Hospital, makeshift classroom in the basement shelter
March 27, 2026 (Fri.) · 6:45 a.m. Tehran time — War Day 28
Scene 1 / 6

Five in the morning. Professor Azad Moradi was already awake.

In the shelter everyone woke early. It was not that they did not need sleep. The old man next to him had a radio that pushed sleep away. Only after the war came did Azad learn that the old man could not sleep either. A city that had lived forty years under one roof learned of each other’s sleeplessness only after it went underground.

The radio read numbers. The numbers of the past month. The dead. The wounded. Schools destroyed. He took out a number he had heard two weeks ago. Fifty-six.

On March 14 Iran’s Ministry of Cultural Heritage, Tourism and Handicrafts said that since the war began at least 56 museums, historic monuments, and cultural sites across the country had been damaged. On March 2 Tehran’s Golestan Palace was damaged by debris and the shock wave of a missile that fell in nearby Arg Square, and UNESCO confirmed it. Part of Naqsh-e Jahan Square in Isfahan was damaged too. Both are UNESCO World Heritage sites.

Golestan. As a student he had stood an hour in that palace’s hall of mirrors. Tens of thousands of pieces of mirror set into the ceiling and the walls. A room where one person walks in and becomes tens of thousands. In that room the kings multiplied themselves past counting. A shock wave is cruelest to a room like that. The walls stay standing. Only the mirrors fall.

And Persepolis. He had gone there first at twenty-two. The stone columns of the Achaemenids. The palace Alexander set on fire in 330 BC. The cedar beams turned to ash and only the stone was left. That stone had stood two thousand three hundred fifty-five years. The conqueror went, and the stone stayed. There had been no news of it being hit in this war. Not yet. He knew how thin a word that “yet” was.

Nariman came dragging a blanket. There were two reasons a seven-year-old woke at this hour. She was hungry, or there had been a sound.

“Grandpa, the radio woke me up. What did you say before? Fifty-six?”

“Did I say it out loud?” Azad smiled. “It’s about old houses. Very old. Hundreds of years, thousands of years old.”

“How old?”

“Houses built by people you’d find if you went back from your father’s father’s father… a hundred more times or so.”

The child started counting on her fingers and gave up. Ten fingers were not enough.

“Why did those people build houses?”

“To live in. And to leave behind that they had lived.”

“Then why do they break them?”

Azad could not answer. Three days running, the third time. For seventy-two years he had collected words to explain the logic of war. In lecture halls, in books, through a revolution and a war. In front of a seven-year-old those words had not once moved. The child’s questions were simple. The grown-ups’ answers were complicated. The side that was wrong was the complicated one.

History dies twice. Once in the explosion. And once more when the world forgets it. The first death cannot be stopped. The second can.

He had filled the notebook to the back cover and closed it. He opened a pad of prescription forms he had gotten from the hospital office and turned it over. And he wrote. The hall of mirrors. Fall 1968. One person walked in and became tens of thousands. That was all the protecting of cultural heritage he could do today. When the people who remember are gone, the mirrors break twice.

Tehran — emergency room, Imam Khomeini Hospital
March 27, 2026 (Fri.) · 9:20 a.m. Tehran time — War Day 28
Scene 2 / 6

Maryam had started her shift at midnight. She had not finished it yet.

The night shift was supposed to end at eight in the morning. Today it did not end. Before dawn there had been strikes in the west of Tehran, and from six o’clock the stretchers came in. The hallway filled. Bloody clothes. People screaming. People not screaming. War had taught her something. The ones not screaming were worse.

On March 27 Iran’s Health Ministry claimed that more than 230 children had been killed by American and Israeli attacks in the first month of the war. The figure was not independently verified. The same day the Israeli military said there had been four missile salvos from Iran since early morning.

Two hundred thirty. She heard the number on the television in the nurses’ station. As soon as she heard it she went back to the pediatric treatment room. When she thought about the number her hands slowed. When her hands slowed the number went up.

She was thirty-one. Before Nariman was born, hospital work had not been like this. After she had a child, everyone carried in looked like somebody’s child. Even a man of sixty. On every stretcher in this country lay somebody’s child. Whether that was a blessing or a punishment, she could not decide.

A little after nine a man was carried in. A Revolutionary Guards uniform. His face was covered with dust and blood. Maryam’s hands stopped. One beat. She wiped the face with wet gauze. It was not Davood. It was the face of someone not yet twenty.

Davood had been called out at dawn on the day the war began. Three days later there had been one phone call. After that, twenty-four days. Every time a uniform was carried in, she wiped the face. Every time she wiped it, it was not him. Every time it was not him, she was grateful. She was ashamed of being grateful. This face too was a face someone was looking for. In the 1980s the women of this city had wiped faces the same way. Hoping it was not a husband, not a son.

A call came up from the basement. It was Professor Azad. Nariman had eaten breakfast. Today she was watering the bean sprouts and learning letters. Maryam said three things.

“Thank you, Professor. I think I’ll be late.”

She hung up. Behind her another stretcher came in.

In a war a nurse does not save people. She makes longer the time it takes death to take one person. If she is lucky, that time becomes a whole life.

Isfahan Province, Iran — Mobarakeh Steel Company, hot strip mill
March 27, 2026 (Fri.) · daytime, Tehran time — War Day 28
Scene 3 / 6

Mehdi Rostami had watched molten steel for twenty-two years.

Forty-four. Shift foreman at the hot strip mill. His father had worked at this plant too. He came in the year the plant first lit its furnaces, put in thirty years, and left. On this plain southwest of Isfahan there had been nothing but the plant. The plant came, and then a town. The town came, and then a school. Mehdi went to that school. He was a son of the years when a country tried to stand on its own by melting iron.

It was the twenty-eighth day of the war. The plant ran. On days the power was cut, it rested, and on days the power came, it ran. Molten steel does not wait. A furnace that cools once takes days to heat again, and cool it wrong and you throw away the whole furnace. So a steelworks cannot put out its fire even in a war. A plant that cannot put out its fire is the easiest target to see in the night sky.

On March 27 Israeli airstrikes hit the Mobarakeh Steel plant in Isfahan and the Khuzestan Steel plant in Ahvaz. Israel said these facilities were part of Iran’s military industry and partly controlled by the Revolutionary Guards. Later the same day the yellowcake plant near Yazd and the IR-40 heavy-water reactor at Arak were attacked, and the Israeli military said it had also struck missile and naval mine production sites in Yazd. Mobarakeh Steel was hit again four days later, on March 31.

The first explosion came from the substation. The lights went out. The emergency lights came on. On the rolling line one red-hot slab stopped dead. The rollers did not turn. A block of steel at twelve hundred degrees shone alone in the dark.

Mehdi drove his crew into the shelter. Eleven men. He counted. Eleven. He counted again. In the doorway he looked back. The slab was cooling from orange to red, from red to a dark red. He had never watched steel cool for so long. Steel was always something that went by. Stopped steel was new to him.

Somebody said this plant was military industry. Maybe it was. Where all of this plant’s steel plate went, he did not know. Some of it he did know. They stamped the doors of Pride cars from this plate. Rolled gas pipe. Made refrigerators. The roof of his house was this plant’s sheet steel. Steel has no tags. The molten steel flowed without knowing if it would become a gun barrel or a spoon. The bombs did not tell the two apart.

In the shelter the youngest man on the crew asked. Boss, what about the furnace? Mehdi did not answer. He did not know when the power would come back. If it did not, the molten steel in the furnace would set where it was. Set steel becomes one body with the furnace. In twenty-two years what he feared most was not an explosion. It was that.

Iron remembers the fire. Steel that has been melted once keeps the shape of the fire in its grain even after it cools. So do people. So do countries.

Bahrain — shelter and quarters near Mina Salman Port
March 27, 2026 (Fri.) · 7:40 p.m. Bahrain time — War Day 28
Scene 4 / 6

The southern sky lit up once and went dark.

Lieutenant Kim Junho saw it from the landing of the stairs to the roof. The sound came ten-some seconds later. Low and long. He judged the direction. Southeast. Beyond Sitra. Where the smelter was.

Aluminium Bahrain (Alba) said the next day that its facility had been attacked on March 27 and two people wounded. On March 28 the Emirates Global Aluminium plant at Al Taweelah in Abu Dhabi was also damaged in an Iranian attack. The same day two drones hit the port of Salalah in southern Oman, wounding one worker, and the radar at Kuwait International Airport was damaged by drones. Iran allowed twenty more Pakistani-flagged ships through the strait, and Islamabad confirmed it.

Sarah Callahan came up the stairs from below. Notebook in hand. Since the afternoon she had not let go of that notebook. Since the call from the Ford.

“Alba?”

“That direction,” Kim Junho said. “A smelter is a plant that can’t have its power cut. If the pots freeze, you throw away a whole line. So they keep the lights on at night too.”

“How do you know that?”

“My father worked in Pohang. A steelworks. Steel or aluminum, every plant that handles hot metal is afraid of the same thing. Cooling.”

Sarah wrote something in her notebook. Then she closed it. He thought of the blast furnaces of Pohang across the sea. The story of a poor country that stood up by melting iron. His father had stood in the middle of that story.

“Today Israel hit two Iranian steelworks. Isfahan and Ahvaz. And in the evening the smelter here got hit.” She looked south. “Steel for steel, aluminum for aluminum. This isn’t a war between armies anymore, Lieutenant. It’s a war between factories.”

Instead of answering Kim Junho held out his phone. A text that had come in the afternoon. The captain of a chemical tanker registered in Busan. Word is twenty more Pakistani ships are getting out. When is it our turn?

“What did you tell him?”

“I haven’t yet,” he said. “Pakistan has somewhere to call. It’s the country that carried the papers back and forth. We’re the country that got asked to send warships. If we send them we can’t get out, and if we don’t send them we can’t get out.”

Sarah did not write that sentence down. There are sentences you keep without writing them down. In her notebook were the six pages she had taken down that afternoon. The words of a senior U.S. Navy officer. The laundry room that caught fire. A hundred thousand tons sixteen days away from the fight. And one line. We have never been told how this war ends. When to put those six pages out, she had not decided. A story you don’t write changes nothing. A story you write hurts someone.

At first a war aims at soldiers. Then it aims at what feeds the soldiers. In the end it aims at the places people go to work tomorrow.

Alborz Mountains, Iran — Air Defense Force command post, communications room
March 28, 2026 (Sat.) · 3:10 a.m. Tehran time — before dawn, War Day 29
Scene 5 / 6

Tonight the duty officer in the signals room was not Farhan.

Colonel Darius Sepahvand checked that on the duty roster and came up. The duty officer was a second lieutenant of twenty-three. Darius told him to go count the fuel stocks in the generator room below the command post. It was a strange order for a colonel to give a second lieutenant at three in the morning. A second lieutenant was not of a rank to think it strange. That was how the chain of command worked. Because there were people below who did not ask, the people above could do anything. Twenty minutes there and back.

An hour ago fire to the east of Tehran had come up on the status board. The University of Science and Technology. He knew that school. Narges had gone there. His daughter had learned to code there. On graduation day he went in uniform, and in front of her friends she was a little embarrassed. Where she was tonight, he did not know. Baba, I’m okay. That one line was all he had.

On the night of March 27 into the 28th, buildings of Iran University of Science and Technology in Tehran were damaged in airstrikes. The Revolutionary Guards responded by threatening to attack American universities in the region. Israeli strikes then reportedly hit a water storage facility at Haftkel, and in Borujerd, Ali Fouladvand, head of a research division of the Organization of Defensive Innovation and Research (SPND), was killed along with his family.

He sat down at the console. The line no one had used and no one had cut for twenty-nine days. The relay station in Quetta. Who was sitting on the other side of it, he did not know. Before the war, during the joint anti-smuggling work, the man on the other end had said once: We’re not the only ones listening on this line, Colonel. Then it had been a warning. Tonight it was the only hope.

He typed the three lines he had been working over for a long time. The command believed the longer the report, the more it looked like certainty. The truth was usually three lines.

One. Fordow is not empty. Part of Hall B has been restored since last summer, and six IR-6 cascades have been running since March 1.

Two. Air defense forces were ordered to protect this mountain even if everything else is given up. For twenty-nine days not one round has come to this mountain.

Three. I do not know why. If you know, this cable is useless. If you do not know, you are destroying the wrong places.

It needed a signature. He could not write his name. He remembered the drawer where his mother had put his brother’s key. A drawer with a brass handle, its gold paint worn off. The drawer where she kept the key of the son who went to war. Zarrin. Golden. He added one line.

I will open again at the same time in forty-eight hours. — ZARRIN

His hand stopped over the send key. If he pressed it, thirty-one years would get another name. They would call it treason. He could not decide what he would call it. But he knew what to call standing twelve-year-olds at checkpoints. In 1953, in 1979, the soldiers of this country had stood in front of this key. To whom do you owe loyalty? The country, or the people holding the country’s name?

He pressed it.

A line came up on the screen. SENT 03:14:07. He erased the log. The console’s log was erased. But the call counter on the relay would have gone up by one. Only one man at this command post read that counter every week. A man who never forgot a frequency once he heard it.

The second lieutenant came back. Four days of fuel, he said. Darius nodded and stood up. His legs shook a little. For the first time in thirty-one years.

Betrayal is not abandoning your country. It is taking your hands away from what the people who abandoned the country keep doing in its name.

Langley, Virginia — CIA Iran Operations Center
March 27, 2026 (Fri.) · 11:50 p.m. EDT / 7:20 a.m. on the 28th in Tehran — War Day 28
Scene 6 / 6 — Final

Leila Shirazi put dots on the map.

The places hit today. The heavy-water reactor at Arak. The yellowcake plant at Yazd. Six days ago, Natanz. Before that, around the conversion facility at Isfahan. Connect the dots and you got a chain. From ore to powder, from powder to gas, from gas to enrichment. And the other road, the one to plutonium. The bombs were cutting that chain one link at a time.

In the middle of the chain was one mountain with no dot on it.

For twenty-eight days she had watched the signal coming out of that mountain. 5.7 seconds. For the last six days, 5.2. In a meeting three months ago someone had said Fordow was finished last summer. She had written that mountain’s name into the annex by hand. Either no one read it, or they read it and said nothing. The chain of approval sent up only the sentences people wanted to hear. The sentences no one heard were buried in annexes.

Ten minutes before midnight, the corner of the screen blinked.

Via Islamabad station. Precedence FLASH. With a header from the chief of station. Source unknown. Came in over the Quetta relay line. ISI liaison handed it over saying “it’s not ours.” Reliability cannot be assessed. Judged by content to be your center’s responsibility; forwarded in the original.

She read the original. It was Persian. A soldier’s Persian. No ornament, the verbs driven hard into the ends of the sentences.

Fordow is not empty.

She forgot to breathe.

Six IR-6 cascades have been running since March 1.

March 1. The day she first saw that signal. Three people in this building knew that date. She, her branch chief, and the technician who caught the signal. Now that date had come back from the direction of Tehran, across the deserts of Pakistan, written in the hand of someone she did not know.

For twenty-nine days not one round has come to this mountain. I do not know why.

I don’t know either, she said without making a sound. I’ve been asking that for twenty-eight days too.

It could be a trap. She had been trained to think of that first. A lure thrown by IRGC counterintelligence. To make America hit Fordow and get something out of it. Or to make it not hit Fordow. But a lure is usually made of words the other side wants to hear. This cable was words no one wanted to hear. And the last sentence.

If you do not know, you are destroying the wrong places.

The signature was one word. Zarrin. Golden. A word Persian grandmothers use when they call their granddaughters. Her aunt had called her that once. When she was very small. A word from the country she had left had come back onto a desk in the country she worked for.

It said it would open again in forty-eight hours. She looked at the clock. Then she dialed her branch chief’s number. It was going past midnight.

For twenty-eight days she had thought she was asking alone. On the other side of the mountain, one more person was asking the same question.

Background Analysis
The Nuclear Chain — What Had Been Hit by March 27

A nuclear program is not one building. It is a chain. Uranium ore mined from the ground is refined into a powder called yellowcake, which is turned into gas and fed into centrifuges to be enriched. There is another road too. Burn uranium in a heavy-water reactor and you get plutonium. On March 27 Israel hit the beginning of that chain and its side road. The yellowcake plant near Yazd, and the IR-40 heavy-water reactor at Arak. Two days later, on March 29, the International Atomic Energy Agency said the IR-40 had been seriously damaged and could no longer operate.

Of the enrichment facilities in the middle of the chain, Iran said Natanz had been attacked on March 21. The Fordow enrichment facility, in the mountains near Qom, was struck by the United States in June 2025. The premise in this novel that Fordow stayed off the target list throughout the 2026 war, and the signal from that mountain and ZARRIN’s cable, are all the author’s invention.

The same day Israel hit two steelworks. Mobarakeh Steel in Isfahan is among the largest steel companies in the Middle East and North Africa, and Khuzestan Steel in Ahvaz is also one of Iran’s major steelmakers. Israel claimed both were part of military industry. By the next day aluminum smelters in Bahrain and Abu Dhabi had been hit by Iranian attacks. At the end of the war’s fourth week, the targets were moving from military facilities to the skeleton of industry.
