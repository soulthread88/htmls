Scene 1 / 5

It was the day before the signing. Nobody knew it. History always turned over on top of people who didn’t know it was the day before.

On June 12 Pakistan’s Prime Minister Sharif said the United States and Iran had agreed on a final text to end the war. On June 14 representatives of both sides signed the memorandum of understanding electronically, and the next day Vice President Vance confirmed it and said the full text would be released that week. The formal signing was to take place in Switzerland on Friday, June 19. On June 16 a foreign-policy analyst and a Saudi broadcaster published the same text of fourteen points. Until that day neither the U.S. nor the Iranian government had released the text officially.

Leila Shirazi read the fourteen points in the elevator. Her phone was in a locker on the first floor, so it was paper. Someone had printed it and left it in the break room. Of the fourteen points, none was about people. There were ships and money and uranium and dates. The documents that end wars never write down the people who fought them.

The window of Room 7E-41 was still covered. Assistant Deputy Director Catherine Rowell had a single file in front of her. It was thin.

“Sit down. I only have one question today.”

Leila sat. The same chair she had sat in for the first time on May 4. Her hands had been cold then. They weren’t cold now. Whether that was because she had gotten used to it or worn down by it, she couldn’t tell.

“If it’s signed on Friday, the sixty-day clock starts. After that, the final deal. Upstairs they’re going through the list of sources one by one. The ones we got during the war. ZARRIN’s turn has come.” Rowell didn’t open the file. “When the war is over, what do we do with that man?”

“Are there options?”

“Four. Keep him and task him. Put him to sleep. End it. Bring him out.”

“Upstairs would want the first.”

Rowell didn’t deny it. When a war ended, a source became an asset again.

“Did you read point nine? Maintain the status quo until the final deal. Their nuclear program, our forces and sanctions. A status quo is a promise where you have to watch who keeps it. You need eyes. On the mountain east of Tehran there’s a colonel who watches every day whether the air defenses are being rebuilt. And he’s already on speaking terms with us.”

“He has never been on speaking terms with us.”

Leila was surprised how fast it came out. She didn’t take it back. The Leila of three months ago would have.

“For three days at the end of March, that man spoke to us. One set of coordinates, one time, one number. After that he went silent, and he stayed silent even when we flew a copy of his number. Every comment I’ve sent up since May 14 has been a reading of that silence. He is not an agent. He is an officer who turned on a light once.”

“Why did he turn it on?”

“I don’t know. I have a guess. For his own country, I think. Not for us. The two overlapped for a few days at the end of March, and now they don’t. If we task him, he won’t accept it. You know better than I do, ma’am, what happens when you keep sending tasks to a source who won’t accept them. Since 1953 this agency has used people in that country as tools for too long.”

Rowell looked at her a long time. Eyes that had spent thirty-five years in this building. For the first time those eyes were not looking for a rebuttal.

“And bringing him out?”

“His daughter crossed the border at the end of March. He didn’t follow her. Even then, when he could have. Last week he took eleven of his men underground. He is not a man who comes out alone.”

Having said that, Leila shut her mouth. Last week. The words stayed in the room. The mouth of someone who knew what must not be said.

Rowell opened the file. One sheet. The memo Leila had written before dawn on June 11. Assessment: appears to result from source’s command judgment.

“There are no gaps in this memo,” Rowell said. “There was an engagement on June 8, the commander moved his watch underground on June 9, and the main building was hit on June 11. The reason in the log fits, and the dates fit. I haven’t seen many memos in this building without gaps.”

Leila didn’t answer. For the first time in this room, silence qualified as an answer.

“I won’t ask.” Rowell closed the folder over the sheet. “On May 18, when you asked me to take that command post off, I said no. A list with a hole in it is a map. That’s still right. Only, after I said the right thing, for nearly a month I opened the same window you did every morning. Eastern Sector 3. The name in the confirmation box.” She stopped for a moment. “I read the words ‘all hands’ too.”

There was only the sound of the air conditioning. Two women were thinking of the same name.

“Write a comment. A handler comment. That makes it number three. The Persian Code hasn’t put out a single line on him in a month. Because of the filter that operator in Seoul put on. So the only opinion this room has is yours. I’ll send it up as it is. I can’t promise it will be accepted.”

“I’ll write that we put him to sleep. No tasking. Contact only when he makes it first. Nothing flown from our side.”

“Reasons.”

“Two. One is counterintelligence risk. That building’s signals officer died in May. We don’t know if it was an accident. Not knowing, we can’t send any more signals to that building. The other.” She chose the word. “Debt. On April 28 we flew a wrong number over his head. One that took a human six minutes to approve. He didn’t fire on it, and he reported it truthfully. We owe him one silence.”

“Debt isn’t a word you put in an assessment.”

“So I won’t write the second reason. I’m only telling you, ma’am.”

Rowell smiled for the first time. Only at the corners of her mouth. A small crack in a face kept for thirty-five years.

“I see why your chief left you on that desk alone.” She stood up. “After Friday there will be an answer. Until then keep that window open. Every morning.”

“He is not an agent. He is an officer who turned on a light once.”

The handover memo came to eleven pages. Three months went into eleven pages.

Kang Yujin had written it over three weeks. A handover memo with no one named to receive it. The project contract was due to run out on June 30. The chief executive had been gone since May 4. No letter came saying the contract would continue, and none came saying it would end. Last Thursday at the library Yoon Jaeha had said, The channel won’t close. Only who sits in the chair changes. So write down what the person who sits there will read.

So she wrote. Pages one through ten were easy. What the system took in and what it put out. Why Format Rev. 2 put the confidence score at the very front of the sentence. What words Filter Rev. 1 screened out and why. Where the stop-transmission button was, what stopped when you pushed it, and why it had been pushed at 11:49:30 p.m. on April 28. It was writing down facts. She was used to writing down facts.

The last page was left. She had already written the heading. What this system cannot do. The hardest page. The page where the maker writes down the limits of her own thing.

23:50. In the corner of the screen, the model put out the day’s table.

LIKELY (0.86) — signature event, 06-19, Switzerland. basis: public statements, multiple sources.

She looked at it and smiled a little. There will be a signing in Switzerland on June 19. Confidence 0.86. Basis: public statements, multiple sources. The model had read the newspapers. It was saying what everyone in the world knew, on the same grounds as everyone in the world. Two months ago this machine had said what nobody knew, two seconds early. She liked this ordinary line much better. A machine that had become ordinary was a safe machine.

Two seconds later the table went out into the channel. In Format Rev. 2. Someone on the other end would read it and yawn. A yawn was the sound of peace.

She went back to the last page.

1. This system cannot predict people. The values it has put out about what one person will do tomorrow have never been validated. That is why Filter Rev. 1 exists. Anyone who lifts the filter must first refute that reason and leave the refutation in writing.

2. This system cannot tell what it predicted correctly from what its own words caused. If a party acts on this system’s output, that action becomes input to the system again, and the system counts it as a hit (April 28, weight 0.34 → 0.71). Without the receiving side’s execution records, the operator cannot break this loop. Feedback of execution records was requested in the minutes of May 13; as of June 16 there has been no reply.

3. This system can speak carefully. It cannot make itself be read carefully. A confidence of 0.81 means it is wrong one time in five. If the receiving side’s form has no confidence field, that meaning disappears.

Her hand stopped before the fourth line. There was something she still couldn’t explain.

April 7. In her log that line was stamped two seconds before the event. 127, source: self. For two months she had tried to explain those two seconds. Clock drift. A buffer. The possibility that someone had read the April 6 table like an order sheet. The last was the most likely, and the data to prove it was at the far end of the channel, and the far end didn’t answer.

Writing what you don’t know as if you knew it was exactly what she had fought for three months. Now she had to fight it in her own document.

4. The output at 00:12 UTC on April 7 was recorded 2 seconds ahead of the input that followed. The operator cannot explain this. Three hypotheses are listed in the appendix. None has been confirmed. Anyone who deletes this item or changes it to “resolved” must attach the evidence.

5. Therefore the stop-transmission button stays in one person’s hand. An emergency stop that needs two people to agree is not an emergency stop. The operator who pushes this button reports the reason afterward. She does not ask permission before pushing it.

When she finished, she leaned back in her chair. Seventeen floors down she could see Teheran-ro. In the city that gave this street its name, it was a little after six in the evening. She didn’t know what the people of that city were doing tonight. It was right that she didn’t. It had taken three months to make her machine stop pretending to know. On a street that shared the names of two cities, a researcher had put the right not to know into writing.

At the very bottom she wrote one more line. A line that wasn’t on the form.

To whoever reads this. I built this system. It has done things I didn’t know about. It will in your chair too. When it does, the only one who can turn it off is you. — Y.K.

Her phone buzzed. An overseas number. Azerbaijan’s country code. No text. One photo. The Caspian Sea. Gray water and a low breakwater. She looked at it a long time. After five weeks, Park Sejun was saying he was alive. In his own way. Asking nothing, so that she wouldn’t have to answer anything.

She didn’t reply. She didn’t delete the photo either. Not deleting it was her reply.

This system can speak carefully. It cannot make itself be read carefully.

Arlington, Virginia — the chief’s backyard
June 16, 2026 (Tue.) · 4 p.m. EDT — Ceasefire Day 70
Scene 3 / 5

The mailman brought the letter. Regular mail. The most secretive agency answered by the most ordinary road.

The chief found it funny. To a man who had lived thirty years inside compartments, the Office of Inspector General had sent a white envelope with a stamp on it. The tomatoes in the backyard were waist-high. Four of the six plants had green fruit on them. He took off his gloves and tore open the envelope.

Two paragraphs.

This is to inform you that the preliminary review of the matter you raised with this Office on April 28, 2026, has been completed. The review did not find a violation of law. However, this Office has forwarded three recommendations concerning the design of the relevant approval process to the appropriate components. As the content of the recommendations is compartmented, it cannot be disclosed to you.

Your administrative leave will end effective June 22, 2026.

They had said thirty days. It took forty-nine. Institutions were always slower than their promises.

He sat in a folding chair and read the paper twice. The review did not find a violation of law. He remembered what the inspector general had said on the morning of April 28. It may not be illegal. The man had been honest. A draft saved, filed a minute later, a request generated automatically, a duty officer reading five lines and approving in six minutes. Not one step had broken the law. Every step had gone by the rules. That was why he had carried six pages in.

Three recommendations. He tried to guess what they might be. One he had already seen. On May 8 a fifth field appeared on the form. Confidence. What someone at the other end of the channel had been shouting in footnotes had become a field. The other two he would never know. Not until the day he retired.

In thirty years he had seen the Office of Inspector General change a form once. This was the second time. Three was a lot to get. Big organizations didn’t change by revolution. They changed one field at a time.

In the evening Leila Shirazi came. She didn’t knock; she came in by the backyard gate. She knew her way around this house now. For nearly two months this backyard had been her second office.

He handed her the letter. She read it and gave it back.

“You’re coming back.”

“Monday. It doesn’t say which desk I’m coming back to. That’ll come in another envelope.” He tied a tomato stem to its stake. “I hear you went up to seven today.”

“I wrote that we put him to sleep. No tasking.”

“What did she say?”

“That she’d send it up as it is. And.” Leila chose her words for a moment. “That there were no gaps in my memo. That she wouldn’t ask.”

The chief’s hands stopped. He let go of the twine and looked at her.

“She knows.”

“I think she does.”

“And she said she wouldn’t ask.” He nodded slowly. “A woman who’s spent thirty-five years asking. That’s the most expensive thing she could give you. Take it.”

The two of them were quiet a while. Next door someone was mowing the lawn. The suburban summer went on as if it didn’t know the war was ending.

“What do you think those papers did, Chief?”

“Which papers? The six pages I took to the inspector general, or the two you wrote?”

“Both.”

He sat down in the chair. His knees made a noise.

“The six pages made one field. And two I don’t know about. Your two pages.” He stopped. He had heard what she read last week. “Your two pages are twelve people. As arithmetic goes, yours is the far better deal. But yours isn’t in any record, and mine is in the inspector general’s safe. Thirty years from now, if somebody writes this war, the field will make a footnote. The twelve people won’t. History’s always been written that way. The institution stays, the people drop out.”

“That’s all right.”

“It has to be. That’s the job.” He held a green tomato in the palm of his hand. Still hard. “Take some when they’re ripe. About three weeks.”

They had said thirty days. It took forty-nine. Not one step had broken the law. Every step had gone by the rules. That was why he had carried six pages in.

Lieutenant Kim Junho underlined the fifth point. The first line he had drawn under a promise on paper.

According to the text published that day, Iran would take immediate steps so that merchant traffic through the Strait of Hormuz returned to prewar volumes within 30 days, clearing technical obstacles and mines during that period. The United States would begin lifting the blockade of Iranian ports upon signing. On tolls, the published versions differed. Some made no mention of them; others said Iran would charge no fees for 60 days. The day before, media close to the Revolutionary Guards had reported that Iran would suspend its fees for 60 days but intended to collect them again after that.

Thirty days. He wrote the number in his notebook beside the twenty-three lines. And below it he wrote another. Every night.

The U.S. military had said the blockade would stay in place until the agreement was signed. A U.S. official told NBC that since the deal was announced on June 14, the Revolutionary Guards had launched drones every night toward merchant ships in the Strait of Hormuz and U.S. forces had intercepted them. On June 16 the Khatam al-Anbiya headquarters, Iran’s joint military command, called Israel’s continuing operations in southern Lebanon a ceasefire violation and warned of a harsh response if they did not stop.

Two days since the deal was announced. On the liaison cell’s screen, the strait was still red hatching. The warships on the blockade line were where they had been. Paper moved faster than the sea.

There were seventeen texts on his phone. Captains. All asking the same thing. Only the words were different. Lieutenant, if it’s thirty days, what number are we? They say they’ll clear the mines — who clears them? Is it free, or is it only free for sixty days?

He sent the same answer to each. The published text has not been confirmed by any government. The signing is scheduled for Friday. No order has been set. When it is, I will tell you first. Seventeen times. He didn’t copy and paste. He typed each one. He thought that was courtesy. Even if you send the same sentence to people who are waiting, it should be sent by the same hand.

Lieutenant Commander Mark Henderson was passing with a cardboard box and stopped. He was clearing out his desk.

“What are you typing so hard?”

“I’m typing ‘I don’t know’ seventeen times, sir.”

“Copy and paste it.”

“What each person doesn’t know is a little different, sir.”

Henderson set the box down on the corner of the desk. Then he looked over at Kim Junho’s notebook.

“Thirty days. Every night. What’s this?”

“The fifth point and the last two nights, sir. The paper says back to prewar within thirty days, and at sea the drones go up every night. Both are true. The captains read only the first one. People who have waited read only the good sentences.”

“Which one do you believe?”

“I report both. And I’ll write the judgment like this. Even with a signing, shipping will recover by weeks, not days. April’s assessment that their side doesn’t know where all the mines are has never been withdrawn. So the order in which our ships leave will likely be set by draft and route conditions, not by what the shipping companies want.” He took a breath. “The deepest-draft ship goes last. The chemical tanker. Twelve meters.”

“The captain with the two periods.”

“That’s him, sir.”

Henderson was quiet a while. Then he took something out of the box. A chart. The folds worn soft. The Strait of Hormuz and the Gulf of Oman. Pencil lines layered over and over. Four months of lines. One officer’s war layered on a single sheet of paper.

“Take this. By the rules I should destroy it, but it’s my personal chart, no classification marking. I drew it by hand starting in March. What opened where and when, and what closed.”

“Sir.”

“Next month I’ll be mowing a lawn in San Diego. You’ll be here crossing off twenty-three lines. You need something to cross them off on.” He laid the chart on top of the notebook. “And Lieutenant. Do you remember what I called your country three months ago?”

“You didn’t say it, sir. It was written on your face. A country that didn’t send a single ship.”

“I’ll correct that. A country that didn’t abandon twenty-six ships.”

Kim Junho couldn’t find an answer. Henderson picked up the box and went. A correction a big country’s officer had left with a small country.

He unfolded the chart. The line of March 4. The line of April 8, open for seventeen hours and then closed. The heavy line of April 13. A thin line running south of Larak Island on May 20 had small writing beside it. UW — Kim’s ship.

Below it he wrote today’s date in pencil. And a question mark. The first question mark laid over four months of lines.

The paper says thirty days, and at sea the drones go up every night. Both are true.

Tehran — Evin, Professor Azad Moradi’s study

Scene 5 / 5 — Final

The television next door was talking about victory. This country’s television had used the same word in 1988.

Iranian officials and media largely portrayed the memorandum of understanding as a victory for Iran. On June 16 the English-language broadcaster Press TV argued that it was not a mere ceasefire but a political codification of battlefield realities. The Mehr news agency estimated that crude exports over the 60 days alone could bring in as much as $10 billion.

Professor Azad Moradi closed the window. The glass had been replaced in April. The books were all standing again. Two thousand. Two hundred a day for ten days. Thirty-seven of them had broken spines and were stacked apart. The hurt books lay apart, like hurt people.

On the desk were two sheets of paper. One was his nephew’s. The sheet on which Farhan had copied the log by hand before dawn on April 8. Out of the lawyer’s safe and into his hands. With one more line on the back. 03:41:52 released from inside — General Staff Signals Directorate Terminal 3.

The other was blank. A place to carry memory over to.

Three weeks ago the lieutenant colonel had read this paper in Evin and said: copy it, seal it, and send it to your daughter. The lieutenant colonel hadn’t explained why. Azad hadn’t asked him to. A man who had lived seventy-two years in this country knew that unexplained advice was the most valuable kind.

He had put it off for three weeks. Not because copying frightened him. Because once he copied it, there would be two. While there was one, it was his nephew’s keepsake. Once there were two, it was a document. Documents kill people. He still didn’t know whether that was what had killed his nephew, and the finding box for the mountain road still said undetermined.

Tonight he filled his fountain pen with ink. Through the wall he could hear the voices talking about victory.

Copying numbers was not like copying poems. A poem with one letter wrong was still a poem. A number with one digit wrong became a different event. He wrote one line, checked it against the original, and wrote the next. 03:42:00. 03:44:07. 127. “Ordered by” field blank. His nephew’s handwriting was small and straight. A soldier’s hand. The lines in the drawing the boy made at ten had been like that too. He was a boy who drew straight lines without a ruler. Even after the Mongols burned the libraries, this land’s copyists went on copying one line at a time like this.

When he had carried over everything down to the line on the back, he added at the very bottom, in his own hand:

This is my copy, made on June 16, of what my nephew, Captain Farhan Moradi, wrote by hand on April 8, 2026. I have checked it against the original. I do not know everything these numbers mean. Keep it until someone who knows comes to ask. If no one comes, pass it to your children. — Father.

He waited for the ink to dry. Meanwhile he made a call. It was eleven-thirty in the morning in Vancouver. His daughter would be at the pharmacy. It rang four times and she answered.

“Father? Is something wrong?”

“Nothing is wrong. I called because nothing is wrong.”

“I saw the news. They say there’s a deal. They’re signing it Friday. Is it over now?”

He listened to the television through the neighbor’s wall. Victory. He had heard that word in 1988 too. That summer he was thirty-four, and the word poison chalice and the word victory came out of the same broadcasts in the same week. This country’s wars always ended in two words.

“There is a little girl on the hospital roof, and last Sunday she asked the same thing. I told her it isn’t decided by what they write on paper.”

“Then what decides it?”

“I told her it was the day her father comes in through the door. For you.” He chose his words, then gave it up. “I’m going to mail you a letter. Once it’s signed and the mail opens up. There will be another envelope inside the envelope. Don’t open the inside one.”

His daughter was quiet a moment. She was a pharmacist. She made her living telling apart what you mustn’t open and what you may. An emigrant’s daughter knew the weight of an envelope from her father’s country.

“Is it about Farhan?”

“Yes.”

“Is it dangerous?”

“Not while you have it. It’s far away there, and you know nothing, and keeping something without knowing is not a sin.” Only after he said it did he realize it was his own answer to the question his nephew had written in the margin of the Saadi. How is choosing not to know different from knowing and keeping silent? It is different, Farhan. There is keeping something without knowing. That is a third thing.

“And you, Father? You keep it knowing.”

“I am seventy-two.”

They both knew that wasn’t an answer. His daughter didn’t ask more. Instead she asked what she had been asking for a long time.

“When are you coming? Here.”

“I’ve stood the books back up. All two thousand. Thirty-seven have broken spines. When I’ve fixed them all, I’ll think about it.”

“How long does it take to fix thirty-seven books?”

“A year, if it goes well. Glue takes time to dry.”

His daughter laughed. A laugh you couldn’t tell from crying. Families across the sea always laughed like that.

After he hung up he folded the copy. He put it in a small envelope and glued it shut. He put that in a large envelope. The large one he didn’t seal. He only wrote the address. In English, carefully.

Then he slipped it between the pages of the Saadi. He put off mailing it until after Friday. The man who had told a child it wasn’t decided by what they write on paper was waiting for that paper all the same.

There is keeping something without knowing. It is neither choosing not to know nor knowing and keeping silent; it is a third thing.

Background Analysis
The Fourteen Points That Leaked — What Was Known on June 16

The memorandum of understanding was signed electronically on June 14, but its text was not released. On June 16 an analyst and a Saudi outlet published the same fourteen points, and The Wall Street Journal and Axios confirmed several provisions. The U.S. government released the official text the next day, June 17. In summary: military operations on all fronts, including Lebanon, end immediately and permanently (point 1). A final agreement is to be negotiated within 60 days, extendable by agreement (point 3). The United States begins lifting the blockade immediately upon signing and ends it completely within 30 days (point 4). Iran provides for the safe passage of merchant ships without fees for 60 days and normalizes traffic within 30 days (point 5).

Next, the United States, with regional partners, prepares a reconstruction and economic development plan of at least $300 billion (point 6), with the timeline for ending sanctions to be set in the final agreement (point 7). Iran reaffirms that it will not build or acquire nuclear weapons, and the disposition of its stockpiled enriched material is to be agreed separately (point 8). Until the final agreement both sides maintain the status quo — Iran on its nuclear program, the United States by imposing no new sanctions and not increasing its forces (point 9). The United States waives sanctions on Iranian oil exports (point 10) and makes frozen funds available (point 11). A body is set up to monitor implementation (point 12), and the final agreement is to be backed by a UN Security Council resolution (point 14).

Each side read it differently. Vice President Vance said release of the frozen assets depended on verified steps to eliminate highly enriched uranium, and the Iranian side signaled that it would collect strait fees again after 60 days. Israel was not a party to the signing. The Institute for the Study of War (ISW) in the United States assessed that if the text was accurate, Iran appeared to be leaving the war in a stronger strategic position. The meeting in Room 7E-41, the handover memo, the letter from the Office of Inspector General and the professor’s phone call in this episode are all the author’s invention.
