Scene 1 / 5

When the name came up on the screen, Lieutenant Kim Junho got to his feet. His body knew first.

Barakah.

On May 17 the Abu Dhabi Government Media Office announced that a drone attack had set fire to a generator outside the inner perimeter of the Barakah nuclear power plant in the Al Dhafra region, and that emergency teams had responded. No one was hurt and there was no radiation leak. The Barakah plant was built by Korean companies led by the Korea Electric Power Corporation (KEPCO); four Korean-designed reactors are in operation, and Korean staff from KEPCO, Korea Hydro & Nuclear Power and others work there. KEPCO said its dispatched staff were unharmed.

He knew the plant only from pictures. Four domes standing in a row in the middle of the desert, at the water’s edge of the Gulf. When he was a cadet the news showed it every day. The first nuclear plant Korea built abroad. His father was eating dinner in Pohang then, and pointed at the screen. Some of the rebar in there came from our mill. A generation ago this country’s workers laid highways on the sands of the Middle East. Their sons built reactors.

Lieutenant Commander Mark Henderson was watching the screen and turned to him.

“Your country built that, didn’t it.”

“Yes. All four.”

“The people there?”

“We have people there. They help run it and do maintenance.” He picked up the phone. The embassy. Busy. He knew the routine now. Wait and call again. “Sir, that fire. What burned?”

“A generator outside the perimeter. Far from the reactors. One drone.”

“One.”

“One. One got through the intercept net.”

Kim Junho brought up a map. Barakah was about two hundred eighty kilometers west of the city of Abu Dhabi. To Iran’s coast across the Gulf, a little over three hundred. Over the past three months the drones and missiles that had flown across the Gulf numbered in the thousands. Refineries had been hit, airports had been hit, water plants, a smelter. A nuclear power plant was a first. Since Chernobyl the world had kept a taboo against fighting a war next to reactors. At Zaporizhzhia that taboo had cracked. Today in the desert, once more.

The edge. Not the reactors. He held on to that. And he knew how thin it was. He didn’t know the distance between one drone and the reactors. That distance might have been someone’s aim, or the wind. Civilization stood on those few hundred meters.

Twenty minutes later the embassy picked up. All Korean staff safe. He took it down, passed it home, and opened his notebook.

5.17 Drone hit and fire at generator outside UAE Barakah nuclear plant. No casualties, no radiation leak. All our nationals on staff safe. Launching party unidentified.

Unidentified. The sixth time. Attacks with no name were piling up.

He sat a long while looking at the word, pen in hand. Three months ago, when he came to this island, his assignment had been ships. Korean ships trapped in the strait. He counted ships, and then he counted people. One hundred thirty-six. Today, outside that number, another group of people appeared. People working at a power plant in the desert. They weren’t on his list. Whose list were they on?

This wasn’t Korea’s war. He had heard that sentence many times in three months and never once thought it was wrong. It wasn’t wrong now. But the things that sentence couldn’t explain kept growing. The twenty-four on the Namu. The one hundred thirty-six on the sea. The engineers at Barakah. The war took people who weren’t its own into itself without asking. A country that lives by selling things to the world had people in every war in the world.

At the end of the report he added a line.

Recommendation: manage the status of our nationals in the Gulf region — ships, power plants, construction sites — on a single sheet. At present each ministry counts separately.

The war took people who weren’t its own into itself without asking. He had counted ships, then people.

Tehran — Enghelab Street, a lawyer’s office
May 17, 2026 (Sun.) · 5:00 p.m. (Tehran local time) — Ceasefire Day 40
Scene 2 / 5

Captain Farhan Moradi walked the street of bookshops in civilian clothes. His body felt strange out of uniform.

Enghelab Street. The road lined with bookshops across from the university. As a student he had bought secondhand books on this street. Looking for books that weren’t on his uncle’s shelves. Some display windows were still boarded with plywood. Still, more shops were open than not. People were leafing through books. On the seventy-ninth day of the war. On a street bearing the name of the revolution, people still bought poetry.

On May 17 Iran’s police chief, Ahmadreza Radan, said more than 6,500 “traitors and spies” had been arrested since the war began.

The number was on the morning news. Six thousand five hundred. Farhan heard it in the command post mess and didn’t put down his spoon. There were eyes that watched for the man who puts down his spoon. A country where even grief was watched.

Over the past eight days his work had changed. After the counterintelligence lieutenant colonel found the back door in the exchange, he needed a signals officer. Someone who could read where that door led, what had gone in and out for nine years. Farhan was called in and spent three nights in front of the system diagram. He found two more things the lieutenant colonel hadn’t. The lieutenant colonel thanked him. He meant it.

And the day before yesterday, people came from Tehran. Four. Civilian clothes. From higher up than the lieutenant colonel. They called themselves a technical committee. They told him to hand over every record of the night of April 7. Originals. Farhan handed them over. Even what he had written by hand that night. They looked at that paper a long time. 03:42:00. 03:44:07. Originator box blank.

Is there a copy of this, Captain?

No.

They nodded. Believing faces. Those faces made him uneasy. People who don’t believe ask again. People who believe don’t ask, and leave. And what they take doesn’t come back.

The lieutenant colonel had said he wanted to close the door and lost. That upstairs they wanted to leave it and watch. Farhan found that strange. A foreign door nine years old in your own country’s exchange, and you watch. Why? After three nights with the system diagram he reached one answer, and told it to no one.

The door might not open only one way. A way in is also a way out. Through the hole the other side used to listen to this side, someone on this side could also speak to the other side. For nine years. And if there was someone upstairs who knew that, that person would want neither for the door to be closed nor for it to be known. Hostility was loud only in front of the door. Behind the door, other calculations passed back and forth.

The record of the command that came in through that door on the night of April 7. With the originator box empty. There was now one copy of it in the world. In his hands.

The lawyer’s office was on the second floor above a bookshop. No sign. The old lawyer was a friend of his father’s. A man who had been a judge before the revolution and was thrown out. After that, he had lived forty years on this second floor, keeping other people’s wills and house deeds. This country’s memory was kept scattered across second floors like that.

Farhan laid the envelope on the desk. It was sealed. Nothing was written on the outside.

“Please keep this. If I don’t come back for it myself, take it to Professor Azad Moradi in Evin. Hand to hand.”

“How long is ‘don’t come back for it’? A month? A year?”

“If you hear I’m dead or arrested. If you hear nothing, a month.”

The old lawyer looked at him. He wasn’t surprised. In forty years he had kept many envelopes like this. Some were collected, some weren’t.

“I won’t ask what’s inside. Just one thing. Will the person who gets this be hurt?”

Farhan had asked himself that question in his uncle’s study. The not-knowing may not be yours, but deciding not to know is, his uncle had said. This envelope would take away his uncle’s freedom to decide not to know. Handing over the truth was handing over a burden.

“He could be,” he said. “That’s why it’s him. All his life he’s been a reader. He’s someone who can decide for himself what to do after he reads. He could burn it, or keep it, or show it to someone. I can’t make that decision for him. I only want the decision to be in his hands.”

“I know your uncle. In 1988 he was the only professor who wrote about my case in the paper. He couldn’t teach for three years because of it.”

The lawyer picked up the envelope, put it in a drawer and locked it. He put the key in his vest pocket.

“A month. Come and take it back before then. I’d prefer that.”

“So would I.”

When he came out on the street the sun was going down. He went into a bookshop and bought a secondhand book. Saadi’s Gulistan, the Rose Garden. For his uncle. He’d said about thirty books had taken water; Saadi must have been one of them. He’d bring it on his next pass. A book written after the Mongols swept this land seven hundred years ago.

His next pass was Wednesday. Three days away.

“He’s someone who can decide for himself what to do after he reads. I can’t make that decision for him. I only want the decision to be in his hands.”

Scene 3 / 5

At eight on Monday morning Rowell called her up. This time she wrote no reason.

Leila Shirazi went up with no paper. The woods outside the window had grown thicker in two weeks. Catherine Rowell didn’t offer coffee. She was standing.

“There’s an attack scheduled for tomorrow morning. Full scale. You’re supposed to not know yet, and as of now you know.”

It took Leila a beat for the sentence to reach her body. She heard the word ceasefire collapse inside her.

“We’re in a ceasefire.”

“The ceasefire is a document. On the sea they’ve been shooting at each other for a month. The negotiations have been stuck for two. The president has decided to stop waiting. That’s the state of things at this hour. It could change. Three Gulf countries have been working the phones all night.”

“Why are you telling me?”

“The air defense command post for Eastern Sector 3 is on the target list.”

The air in the room thinned. The woods outside the window went far away.

“ZARRIN’s command post.”

“Air defense command posts are first-hour targets. All of them. They were on February 28, and in last June’s war too. His building was already hit nearby twice in March. He was lucky.” In Baghdad in 1991, too, the first hour had been the hour for putting out the eyes.

“Take it off the list.”

“I knew you’d come to say that.” Rowell turned toward the window. “That’s why I called you up early. To answer. No.”

“He’s a source. The one you called half the ceasefire.”

“That’s why not. Four hundred people see the target list. If only one of twenty air defense command posts is missing, someone among those four hundred will wonder why. If even four of the four hundred wonder why, he’s finished. Not by our hand, but by their counterintelligence. A list with a hole in it is a map, Analyst. Pointing to where he is.”

Leila knew there was nothing wrong with that logic. That was what she couldn’t bear. Right logic was leaving a man standing on a target.

“Then a warning —”

“With what? You persuaded me last week. That the format was spent. Top of the hour, airborne, one twenty-seven. He doesn’t trust it. You were right and I adopted it. So we have no road to reach him now. You made it that way. Correctly.”

Those words were a knife. And Leila knew she had handed over its handle herself. HC-02. Retire this format. Wait for source-initiated contact. The door she closed to protect him was shutting him in.

There was one more road. Paper. Islamabad. A saucer. A road that takes twenty-one days. The attack was tomorrow morning. Only the slowest road was left.

“He’s known this since March,” Rowell said. Her voice had dropped a little. “What it means for his country’s air defense commander to talk to the enemy. Whose things would come flying over his head. He turned on a light knowing that. I don’t think he imagined you could protect him.”

“You don’t know what he imagined.”

“No. Do you?”

Leila didn’t answer. The paper in her wallet was heavy against her chest. ’Tis bitter cold, and I am sick at heart. That wasn’t asking to be protected. The woman was right. He hadn’t asked for anything.

That made it harder to bear. Protecting someone who doesn’t ask was the hardest thing.

She went back to her desk and did nothing all day. The clock on her screen showed Tehran time. Evening. Night. What was he doing now? Not knowing what was coming over his head tomorrow morning. She got through that day only by the hands of the clock.

At 3:12 p.m. the wall screens in the operations center changed.

On May 18 President Trump posted on social media that he had decided not to carry out an attack on Iran scheduled for tomorrow, saying the Emir of Qatar, the Crown Prince of Saudi Arabia and the President of the UAE had asked him to. He passed on the three leaders’ word that serious negotiations were under way, but added that he had instructed the military to be prepared to go forward with an immediate full-scale, large-scale attack if an acceptable deal was not reached. The same day, Pakistan passed Iran’s revised peace proposal to the United States.

Someone let out a low breath. Someone swore. Sounds that couldn’t be told apart as relief or disappointment. In the building of war, peace too was greeted that way.

Leila sat in her chair and covered her face with her hands. She stayed like that a long time. Sam Farrell came over and without a word set a glass of water on her desk and went.

Put off. Not canceled. Maybe for good, maybe for a while. That was what the president had said, they said. His building was still on the list. As a first-hour target. And she still had no road to reach him. Except the one that took twenty-one days. A man’s life hung on the mood of a single sentence.

She took out her paper notebook. Opened it from the back. To the page after the one where she had written six lines last month.

Seven. Next time it may not be put off. Eight. Before then, he has to be told one thing. By a road where no light comes on. Nine. That road takes twenty-one days. So it has to leave today.

A list with a hole in it is a map. If only one of twenty is missing, it points to where he is.

Alborz Mountains, Iran — Air Defense Force command post
May 18, 2026 (Mon.) · 11:00 p.m. (Tehran local time) — Ceasefire Day 41
Scene 4 / 5

The post reached Tehran near eleven at night. Written by the enemy’s president.

Colonel Darius Sepahvand read it in the operations room. The duty officer had put it up on the screen with a translation. Will not carry out the attack on Iran scheduled for tomorrow.

The operations room went quiet. The silence of people learning for the first time that they had survived.

Scheduled. People read the word. Tomorrow. So, what would have come down when the people in this room fell asleep tonight and woke up. Something first learned from the words saying it wouldn’t happen. This country’s fate was decided first on another country’s president’s screen.

Darius took his eyes from the screen and looked at his sector’s status board. Four batteries. One with missiles. The other three had been empty since April. They had dug out two buried launchers and stood them up, but the missiles to load them never came. Two of five radars were turning. With this he was guarding the eastern sky of Tehran. If guarding was the word.

If it had come tomorrow morning, what could he have done? Aim the one remaining battery at something in the first wave, fire, have the battery’s position exposed, and be erased in the second wave. And this building. Air defense command posts are hit in the first hour. For thirty-one years he had taught that. You put out the enemy’s eyes first.

He remembered when the area near this building was hit twice in March. Then he had thought he might die, and it hadn’t mattered. The calm of a man with nothing left to protect.

Today was different. Since the paper under the saucer, he didn’t want to die. Noticing that, he was a little surprised. One line of writing had shaken the weight of fifty-two years.

And he thought of that person.

Had the person over there known? That tomorrow had been scheduled? If so, what had that person done? He held on to the question a long time. Not to get angry. To try standing where that person stood.

Even if that person knew, there would have been nothing to do. Paper takes twenty days. Lights can’t be trusted anymore. That person would have sat, knowing what their country was sending over his head, in a place where they could neither stop it nor warn him. All day.

It was the same place he had sat all April. Knowing the second lieutenant was in the room underground, and not writing the sixth sentence.

’Tis bitter cold, and I am sick at heart. He had sent those words over there. Tonight it felt as if they were coming from there to here. Two ends of the same scene. Two sentries. One coming to relieve, one leaving. In that play the two sentries were on the same side. That was the difference. And maybe not a difference.

Captain Farhan Moradi came into the operations room. Since getting back from his pass yesterday he had been quieter. He read the post on the screen and came to stand beside the colonel.

“It’s been put off.”

“Put off.”

“We learn tomorrow was scheduled from the other side’s president’s post. What was our command doing?”

“Who knows. Maybe they knew. And if they knew, maybe they saw no reason to tell us.”

The captain laughed low. A laugh that wasn’t one. The laugh of an officer who has to learn about his own country’s sky from the enemy’s writing.

“Colonel. I’m going out on a pass Wednesday. To take my uncle a book.”

“Go. Give the professor my regards.”

“I will.” Farhan started to go, then turned back. “Colonel. If I’m late on Wednesday, I’ve written next week’s checklists in advance; they’re in the third drawer in the signals room. Jahangiri can just submit them as they are.”

“Is there a reason you’d be late?”

“No. It’s a mountain road. I’m just saying.”

He went out. Darius watched his back. Something snagged. That night he didn’t know what. Wednesday’s mountain road was long.

Since the paper under the saucer, he didn’t want to die. The calm of a man with nothing left to protect was over.

Arlington, Virginia, USA — the chief’s house
May 18, 2026 (Mon.) · Night EDT — Ceasefire Day 41
Scene 5 / 5 — Final

This time it wasn’t the backyard but the kitchen. Rain was soaking the seedlings.

It was raining. The chief handed her a towel and made tea. Leila Shirazi opened the paper notebook on the table. Seven, eight, nine.

“So it was tomorrow,” the chief said. He’d learned from the news. People on leave learn from the news. “And his building’s on the list.”

“I asked them to take it off. They said no. A list with a hole in it is a map.”

“She’s right.”

“I know. That’s why I came.”

She turned to the next page of the notebook. It was blank.

“I don’t know what to write. I can send one line. It takes twenty-one days. What do I tell him? That your building is a target? He knows. He’s an air defense commander. He knows better than I do. Then when it’s coming? I don’t know either. It could be put off like today, or it could come within twenty-one days. Before the note gets there.”

The chief set a cup in front of her.

“You’re trying to send intelligence. Because you’re an analyst. But what he sent you last time — was that intelligence?”

“No.”

“He said it was cold. That he was sick at heart. What do you think that was?”

Leila thought a long time.

“…That there’s a person here.”

“Right. So what you send may not be intelligence either. You can’t tell him whether he’ll die tomorrow or twenty-one days from now. What you can tell him is something else.”

She picked up the pen. Put it down. Picked it up again.

What came next in that scene? Her father’s voice came back to her. The sentry leaves. He says it’s cold and he is sick at heart. The relieving sentry asks: has it been quiet? The departing sentry answers: Not a mouse stirring. And they part. Good night. And then the new arrivals call out to each other. Halt, who goes there. The answer wasn’t a password. Her father loved that passage. As a girl she didn’t know why he loved it.

Friends to this ground.

To the question of who. Not giving a name, not giving a side, not giving a king. Friends to this ground. His ground. The ground she left at six. The ground where Ameh still lived, where her mother’s house was number two hundred eleven. The regime had changed. The ground was the same.

She wrote. In Persian. One line.

Friends to this ground. What comes overhead I cannot stop. Only be underground when it comes.

The chief read it over her shoulder. Slowly.

“May you be underground.”

“Air defense command posts are hit in the first hour. If he were underground in that hour, not in the operations room. He won’t be, because he’s a commander. He won’t go down and leave his men. Even so.”

“Even so.”

“Even so, it stays, doesn’t it, that someone said so. Over there. That inside the country sending the bombs there’s one person who wants you to live.”

The chief took the paper. He held it a long time without folding it.

“This time I’d better buy dinner, not lunch. It’s urgent.” He folded the paper. “Leila. This is the first time you’re sending the other side something that isn’t intelligence. In sixteen years.”

“Grounds for firing?”

“Grounds for firing. And maybe the most useful thing you’ve ever done. Intelligence agencies fight wars with intelligence. What’s left after a war is usually the things that weren’t intelligence.”

The rain didn’t stop. She finished her tea and stood. At the door the chief spoke.

“That line. Friends to this ground. Doesn’t someone else answer after that?”

“Yes. ‘And liegemen to the Dane.’”

“You didn’t write that.”

“I can’t write that,” she said. “I’m not sure anymore which king I’m liegeman to.” For the first time an exile’s daughter asked the address of her own loyalty.

To the question of who, there is an answer that gives no name, no side, no king. Friends to this ground.

Background Analysis
May 18 — The Attack Scheduled for Tomorrow

On Monday, May 18, President Trump said on social media that he had decided not to carry out an attack on Iran scheduled for tomorrow. He said Emir Tamim of Qatar, Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman of Saudi Arabia and President Mohamed bin Zayed of the UAE had asked him to hold off, saying serious negotiations were under way. He said the delay might be brief or might be permanent, and that he had instructed the military to be ready to go forward with an immediate full-scale attack if no deal was reached.

The world first learned that an attack had been scheduled from a post saying it wouldn’t happen. It was seventeen days since he notified Congress on May 1 that the hostilities “have terminated.” The same day, Pakistan passed Iran’s revised peace proposal to the United States. The weight of mediation was widening from Pakistan alone to three Gulf states, and a few days later Qatar would formally join the mediation.

The day before, a drone had fallen on the outskirts of the UAE’s Barakah nuclear power plant and started a fire. An event that suggests why the Gulf monarchies were working the phones so urgently. The scenes in this episode — the envelope left with a lawyer, the target list on the seventh floor, the second note in the kitchen — are all the author’s invention. This novel does not know what targets were in the actual attack plan.

Author’s Note
Barakah nuclear power plantReal
The Arab world’s first commercial nuclear plant, in the Al Dhafra region of Abu Dhabi. A consortium led by KEPCO built four Korean-designed reactors (APR-1400). On May 17 a drone fell on a generator outside the inner perimeter and started a fire, but there were no casualties and no radiation leak. Kim Junho’s father and the rebar story are fictional.
The attack scheduled for tomorrowReal / May 18
The gist of President Trump’s social media post. He said he had put it off at the request of the leaders of Qatar, Saudi Arabia and the UAE.
Target listFictional
Which facilities were in the actual attack plan has not been made public. The conversation between Rowell and Leila and the “list with a hole” logic are the author’s invention.
Enghelab StreetReal place
A street dense with bookshops in front of the University of Tehran. “Enghelab” means revolution. The lawyer and the envelope are fictional.
“Friends to this ground”Literature
Hamlet, Act 1, Scene 1. To the sentry’s challenge, Horatio answers “Friends to this ground,” and Marcellus, “And liegemen to the Dane.”
Saadi’s Gulistan (The Rose Garden)Literature
A book of prose and verse by the 13th-century Persian poet Saadi.
