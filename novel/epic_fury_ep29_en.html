Scene 1 / 5

The fire went out on Tuesday. Other fires didn’t.

On May 5, HMM put out the fire aboard the Namu by releasing carbon dioxide. The same day, President Trump referred on social media to Iran opening fire. The UAE Defense Ministry said it was again fending off Iranian missile and drone attacks. That night the French shipping company CMA CGM said its cargo ship San Antonio had been attacked in the Strait of Hormuz and several crew members had been injured and evacuated. On May 6 a tug was secured to take the Namu to Dubai.

Lieutenant Kim Junho slept four hours in two days. News came in pieces: carbon dioxide filling the Namu’s engine room, the temperature coming down, the crew holding out in the living quarters forward. He took it and passed it on, took questions and passed them on, took answers and passed them on. In between, in the strait, a French company’s ship was hit. There were injured sailors. A ship not on his list. For a moment he was relieved at that, and caught himself being relieved. The list made him protect people, and made him forget the people off the list.

On Wednesday afternoon the post came up.

On May 6, President Trump said there had been great progress toward an agreement with Iran and that Project Freedom would be paused for a short period, to see whether a complete and final agreement could be reached. The same day, U.S. Central Command announced that a U.S. aircraft had used its cannon to disable the rudder of the Iranian tanker Hasna, which had been trying to run the blockade to Iran.

Lieutenant Commander Mark Henderson pointed at the screen.

“Three days. Started Monday, stopped Wednesday.”

“Two ships got out. In three days.”

“Two. Only the ones flying our flag.”

“Not one of mine got out, and one got hit.”

Henderson didn’t answer. The silence of a man who knows there is nothing to say. Kim Junho was grateful for that silence. The Henderson of three months ago would have said something clever. War had taught even a big country’s officer when to keep his mouth shut.

“Great progress, he says,” the lieutenant commander said after a while. “You believe it?”

“I want to. The problem is it’s the fourth time in a month.”

He turned back the pages of his notebook. April 7. A whole civilization will die tonight — and two hours before, a ceasefire. April 17. Strait open — closed again a day later. May 1. Hostilities terminated — three days later small boats destroyed. And today. Great progress — the same day, cannon fire into a tanker’s rudder.

The words and the sea were using different calendars. He was a liaison officer, and his job was to copy both calendars onto one report. The capital’s calendar turned by sentences. The sea’s calendar turned only by ship counts.

5.6 U.S. president: “great progress,” Project Freedom paused. Same day, U.S. forces disable Iranian tanker’s steering. Assessment: negotiating progress and maritime coercion proceeding simultaneously. Early transit for our ships to be judged not by announcements but by actual ship counts through the strait. Ships through today: 0.

In the evening word came that a tug had been found. The Namu would go to Dubai. To a repair yard. The twenty-four would soon set foot on land. Beside the fourteenth line he wrote: Under tow — Dubai.

That line would come off the list now. Twenty-six would become twenty-five. He thought a long time about whether that was a good thing. There were two ways a ship came off his list. Getting out through the strait. And getting hit and towed to a yard. The first ship to come off took the second way. Not out of the Gulf but into a dry dock inside it. In the ledger, escape and being hit were the same line struck through.

The words and the sea were using different calendars. On the day the words great progress came out, no ship got through.

Alborz Mountains, Iran — Air Defense Force command post, the colonel’s office
May 6, 2026 (Wed.) · 10:00 a.m. (Tehran local time) — Ceasefire Day 29 · War Day 68
Scene 2 / 5

The attaché arrived five minutes early. The mailman always comes a little early.

A colonel of the Pakistan Army. About fifty. A neat mustache, and Persian spoken short, the way a soldier speaks it. A liaison officer from the General Staff brought him and stepped back at the door. Thirty minutes, he said.

Darius Sepahvand stood to receive him. His body knew protocol. Handshake. Seat. Weather. Second Lieutenant Sohrab Jahangiri came in with a tray, set down two glasses of tea and sugar cubes, and went out. The door closed. He would be standing outside it.

For twenty minutes nothing happened. The attaché explained the ceasefire-monitoring procedures, and Darius confirmed there had been no engagements in his sector since the ceasefire. He didn’t mention the track on the morning of April 28. That was for the General Staff to decide. The attaché wrote a couple of lines in his notebook. The tea went cold. Diplomacy mostly happened over cold tea.

In the twenty-second minute the attaché picked up his glass. Drank, set it down. The sound of the glass touching the saucer. His hand stayed at the edge of the saucer, and when it left, the corner of a sheet of paper was sticking out from under it.

“A friend in Islamabad asked me to give the colonel his regards,” the attaché said, looking out the window. In exactly the voice he had used for the weather. “I don’t know what it says. I haven’t read it.”

Darius didn’t move.

The first thing a soldier of thirty-one years thought was a trap. Counterintelligence. The lieutenant colonel had said he would change the order. Look outside first. This could be that outside. A mediating country’s attaché hands a note to an air defense commander. The commander takes it. The door opens.

But for a trap it was too clumsy. And he had watched this man’s eyes. For twenty minutes. This man was bored. The boredom of a mailman doing his job without knowing what the job means. Not something you can fake.

He reached out and drew the paper out. Half the size of a palm. The bottom folded up once. On the unfolded part, one line. Persian. A woman’s hand.

The one who asked was a person, but the one who answered was not only a person. Turn nothing on at the top of the hour.

The sounds in the room went far away. The crow outside the window, the boots in the corridor.

He read the sentence once, and didn’t need to read it twice. What he had been turning over alone for eight days was written in someone else’s hand. Two hundred eleven. The thing with correct grammar and no meaning. That is not the one. He had been right. There were two over there. A person, and something that wasn’t a person. And the person had folded a sheet of paper and sent it over the mountains to tell him. Not by radio. Radio meant the other thing would hear. In the newest kind of war, the oldest kind of message had survived.

Turn nothing on at the top of the hour. Six o’clock sharp. The hour he had chosen. That wasn’t his anymore either.

But whose paper was this? That question came before the joy.

That was what remained. If a number could be imitated, so could handwriting. What reason was there that the thing that wasn’t a person couldn’t write a paper pretending to be one? He looked at the attaché. The attaché was still looking out the window.

He had to ask. The way the other side had asked him a month ago. He knew the sentence. It was all he had. He spoke low, in Persian.

“Halt. Who goes there?”

The attaché turned his head. The boredom cleared from his eyes. This man had come with instructions to wait for those words. Along with the warning that they might not come.

The attaché didn’t answer. He reached out and unfolded the crease in the paper in Darius’s hand. The fold pressed down with a thumbnail opened. Beneath it were two words.

Long live the king.

Darius drew in a breath. A breath he had been holding a long time.

He knew what those two words meant in this country. Since 1979 they had been not a slogan but a charge. If that paper were found in this room he wouldn’t even get the chance to explain. But that wasn’t the first thing that came into his head.

Isfahan. Summer 1983. He was nine and his brother was thirteen. Their mother’s parents’ house, where they had gone south to get away from the war. On their grandfather’s bookshelf was a book with its cover gone. The story of a Danish prince. Their grandfather taught English at a school, and he treasured an old Persian translation of that book. His brother liked only the very first page. Before the ghost comes, two sentries on a cold night calling out to each other. The brothers made it a game on the roof. His brother would shout from the far end: Halt. Who goes there? And he had to answer from this end. If he got it wrong, his brother counted him as the enemy and fired the water pistol.

Long live the king.

Four years later his brother died at seventeen. Now he was the only person in the world who knew that game. That dawn in March, when he saw the first sentence from the other side, he had thought he’d heard it somewhere. He never could remember where. A rooftop forty-three years ago. A game from one war had come back as the code of another.

The person over there couldn’t have known the game. They had only read the same book. In the same language. A Persian translation with its cover gone. Some grown-up must have read it to someone.

It was a person. And a person raised in his language. Inside the enemy’s intelligence service there was someone who had heard Shakespeare in his country’s tongue.

A machine wouldn’t pick this answer. Writing those two words to a soldier of this country was insane. It wasn’t plausible. That’s why he could believe it. The person over there had chosen words that could kill him, and it was the only proof that person could give him. I am not a calculation.

The attaché took the paper back. Folded it and put it in his cigarette pack. That was probably an instruction too. Leave nothing.

“If you have something to send, I’ll hear it,” the attaché said. “One line only. I don’t write it down. I memorize it.”

Darius thought. He had too much to say, and one line could hold one thing. He remembered what came next in that scene. What the sentry being relieved says as he leaves. His brother had hated that line. It was the line that ended the game.

“Tell them this. ‘’Tis bitter cold, and I am sick at heart.’”

The attaché repeated it once under his breath. He didn’t ask what it meant.

“And one more thing. That I won’t speak in numbers anymore.”

“That’s two lines, Colonel.”

“Then send only the second.” A moment later he corrected himself. “No. Only the first. Anyone who understands the first will know the second without being told.”

The thirty minutes were up. The attaché stood, shook hands, thanked him for cooperating with the monitoring procedures, and left. When the door opened, Sohrab was standing at attention. The second lieutenant walked the attaché to the stairs, came back, and cleared the tea glasses. That was how history went out. On a tray.

“The tea went cold. Neither of you drank much.”

“The talk ran long.”

Sohrab lifted the saucer onto the tray. Nothing was under it. He didn’t ask anything. But before he went out, he spoke.

“Colonel. Outside the door I didn’t hear a sound. If anyone asks, that’s what I’ll say. It’s true. This door is thick.”

The door closed. Darius sat in his chair and looked at his hands a long time. The hand that had held the paper. The hand that had held a water pistol on a roof forty-three years ago. Two wars in between.

For eight years he had thought he was alone. Since his wife died. And this last month he had been more alone. Today he learned that somewhere on the other side of the world there was one person who had read the same book. No name, no face. It wasn’t enough. But it was different from nothing. For forty-seven years the two countries had called each other enemy. All that time, the same book was still being read.

A machine wouldn’t pick this answer. It wasn’t plausible. That’s why he could believe it. The person over there had chosen words that could kill him — I am not a calculation.

Seoul, South Korea, Eunpyeong-gu — district public library, third-floor reading room

Scene 3 / 5

Kang Yujin asked as soon as she sat down.

“LNO-INGEST. Analytic product. Three or four a day in November, two hundred a day in March. Who raised it?”

Yoon Jaeha closed his book. He wasn’t surprised. But for the first time, before answering, he looked around once.

“You really are someone who counts, Doctor.”

“Counting is my job.”

“On January 19 the technical annex was amended once. An amendment raising the daily intake capacity cap. Four pages. They sent the draft and this side signed it. The person who signed was Mr. Park.”

“You mean Mr. Park did it knowing?”

“Mr. Park says he only looked at the capacity number. It was written in gigabytes. It didn’t say what would increase, and he didn’t ask. I tend to believe him. He’s a businessman, not a spy. More capacity means a bigger settlement.”

It took Kang Yujin a while to accept that. She had been looking for a conspiracy. Someone with a purpose who turned on the tap. But the answer that came back was a four-page document, a man who looked only at the number, and a quarterly settlement. Big mistakes usually start with small signatures.

“Then what about their side? Who over there started pouring in people’s documents?”

“I called last week to ask that. The number that rang in seventeen minutes.” He smiled a little. “This time we called. They picked up.”

“What did they say?”

“They want a technical consultation. Operators from both sides sitting across from each other. They noticed the output format changed. And they don’t dislike it. Which means someone over there welcomes that format.”

This person is on our side. Kang Yujin had no way of knowing that sentence had been spoken in a room across the Pacific. But for the first time the far end of the channel felt not like a wall but like a room. With someone sitting in it.

“When, and where?”

“Next Wednesday. They choose the place. You have to go, Doctor. You’re the one who fixed that machine’s mouth.”

“What about Mr. Park?”

Before answering, Yoon Jaeha ran a finger once down the spine of the book.

“Mr. Park hasn’t been answering his phone lately. He didn’t come to the office Monday, or yesterday. When did you last talk to him?”

“Last Tuesday, past midnight. The night I pressed the button.”

“How was he?”

“Tired. And… he said it was the first time he’d heard a voice from over there. That it scared him.”

“I see.” He had the look of someone filing those words away somewhere. “Doctor. If Mr. Park contacts you, let me know. Whatever he says. Especially if he says he’s going away somewhere for a while.”

She nodded. As he started to rise she stopped him.

“One thing. When I go to the consultation, what should I ask for?”

“You said it last week. That to break the loop, this side has to know what they moved. Ask for that. The execution records. And ask them to take people’s documents out of the input.” He put the book on the return counter. “They won’t give them. Not at first. But it stays on record that someone asked. On paper. That gets used later. A lot of history is written from requests that were turned down.”

She had been looking for a conspiracy. The answer that came back was a four-page document, a man who looked only at gigabytes, and a quarterly settlement.

Tehran — Imam Khomeini Hospital
May 7, 2026 (Thu.) · Night (Tehran local time) — Ceasefire Day 30
Scene 4 / 5

The answer from Southern Command came on Tuesday. Twelve days after she had filed the inquiry.

A one-page notice. Maryam read it standing in the nurses’ station. Sergeant Davood. Transferred to a coastal defense battalion in the March 9 unit reorganization. Station: Bandar Abbas, Hormozgan Province. Confirmation of individual status since April in progress by that battalion. Further notice to follow.

Bandar Abbas. She found the name on a map. The very bottom of the country. Where the strait bends. Twelve hundred kilometers from Tehran. On March 9 he had gone there without even having the stitches taken out of his shoulder.

For two days she carried the notice in the pocket of her gown. It didn’t say he was alive. It didn’t say he was dead. In progress. But now there was a place. Her husband wasn’t somewhere; he was there. She told Nariman her father was by the sea. The girl had never seen the sea. She asked if Baba would bring her shells.

And tonight the name came up on the ward television.

On May 7 the United States said it had struck Iranian military sites at Bandar Abbas and on Qeshm Island in response to a combined missile, drone and small-boat attack on U.S. destroyers transiting the Strait of Hormuz. Iran accused the United States of violating the ceasefire by attacking an Iranian tanker and other vessels, and claimed U.S. strikes had fallen on civilian areas on Qeshm and in nearby Bandar Khamir and Sirik. The Iranian military said it had targeted U.S. warships east of the strait and south of the port of Chabahar in response. Iranian authorities said six people were missing and several injured from a fishing boat and a cargo ship attacked by U.S. forces near Khasab in Oman.

Words ran along the bottom of the screen. Bandar Abbas. Military sites. U.S. airstrikes.

Maryam stood holding a medication tray. Someone called her name. She didn’t hear.

Military sites. A coastal defense battalion was a military site. The people defending the coast were on the coast. What the Americans had hit was something on the coast.

They had said ceasefire. A month ago. The day the children saw sunlight on the hospital roof for the first time. That day she had thought: it isn’t over, it’s stopped. She couldn’t be the least bit glad to have been right. After the ceasefire in the summer of 1988, too, the women of this country had waited a long time.

The head nurse took the tray from her hands. Asked nothing. In this hospital it had long since become good manners not to ask anything of someone frozen in front of the television. Everyone had someone somewhere. A whole city was waiting at once.

She went down the stairs. To the basement. The classroom lights were off and the children were asleep. Nariman lay by the wall with the blanket pulled up to her chin. By her head was a sheet of paper. Something she had drawn that afternoon. Water colored in with a blue pencil. A stick figure standing beside it. The round thing in its hand was a shell.

Professor Azad Moradi was reading in a chair by the door and looked up. He hadn’t watched the television, but he saw her face and knew.

“Where did they say?”

“Bandar Abbas. Davood’s there. I found out Tuesday. And tonight they…”

She couldn’t finish. The professor closed his book and pulled over another chair. She sat.

“Bandar Abbas is a big city,” he said. Not in a comforting voice; in the voice of someone stating a fact. “More than half a million people live there. Several ports, several bases. Between being there and being where it hit, there’s a lot of room.”

“I know.”

“Knowing doesn’t help.”

“Yes.”

The two of them sat a long time. They could hear the children breathing. Seventeen breaths. In nine years in the emergency room, Maryam had told people to wait. Until the results came. Until the doctor came. Every time she said it, she hadn’t known how cruel it was. Now it had come back to her.

“Professor. I don’t even know what I hope for anymore. Whether I want news to come or not to come.”

“You want both. That’s what waiting is.” He took off his glasses and wiped them. “As long as there’s no news, he’s alive in you. When news comes, whichever way it goes, that life ends. People say waiting is only suffering, but waiting is also keeping something. Something not yet decided.”

Maryam picked up her daughter’s drawing. Blue water. Stick figure. Shell. She folded it and put it in her gown pocket, next to the notice.

“As long as there’s no news, he’s alive in you. Waiting is also keeping something. Something not yet decided.”

Arlington, Virginia — the chief’s backyard
May 7, 2026 (Thu.) · Evening EDT — Ceasefire Day 30
Scene 5 / 5 — Final

The chief was planting tomato seedlings. The hands that had decided war were pressing down soil.

Leila Shirazi came in through the side gate in the backyard fence. She had left her phone in the car. The chief was on his knees pressing the soil; he saw her and brushed off his hands. Day seven of administrative leave. He hadn’t shaved, he looked more at ease than she had ever seen him, and both of them knew the ease was a lie.

“Did you memorize it?”

“She asked three things. Whether ZARRIN responded to the April 28 signal. Where two hundred eleven came from. Whether you sent anything to ZARRIN outside the channel.”

“What did you say to the third?”

“That you didn’t send anything.”

The chief laughed. Without a sound. He wiped his forehead with a dirty hand.

“You’ll make a good analyst. It only took sixteen years.”

“And there were two things she didn’t ask. The operator on the other end. Y.K. Not one word about the channel being blocked for nearly two days, or the format changing. And the one hundred twenty-seven seconds on the night of April 7. She only said it put out three lines, and didn’t say what came after.”

The chief’s hand stopped. He looked down at the seedlings a long time.

“Sit down.”

There were two worn wooden chairs in the yard. They sat. The sun was going down. From next door came the sound of a lawn mower.

“The first one is easy. She didn’t ask about Y.K. because she doesn’t want you getting in touch with that person. If the two people at either end of the machine get to know each other, the people in the middle get uncomfortable. That isn’t hiding. That’s blocking.”

“And the second?”

“The second is what I’ve been thinking about for a week. The leave is long.” He grabbed a handful of soil and let it go. “On the night of April 7, that country’s air defense command network went dark for one hundred twenty-seven seconds and came back. Our electronic warfare people confirmed they didn’t touch it. You got that confirmation. I saw it too. It’s probably true. Electronic warfare didn’t do it.”

“Then who?”

“Leila. When Persian Code started in 2019, it wasn’t only a listening program. It had two branches. One branch listens and learns. That’s the one you know. The other branch puts a hand in and leaves it there. In that country’s exchanges and switching equipment. In peacetime. Quietly. Hoping it would never be used. What we shut off in 2024 was the first branch. The machine that couldn’t explain itself. The second branch wasn’t under review. It was under a different authority. It’s been that way since Stuxnet. The people who listen and the people who touch have always been in different buildings.”

Leila kept still. The lawn mower stopped. On a peaceful suburban evening, they were talking about a country’s sky going dark.

“So that night.”

“My guess. No proof. On the night of April 6 it must have put out a table. When the next one twenty-seven would be. With some confidence. That table got poured into the four-box form, the confidence fell off, and what was left was a time and a number. And someone read it not as a prediction but as an opportunity. It was the night of the ultimatum. If we could show that country one thing. That we can turn off the lights in your sky. Without a single bomb, for two minutes or so. Then maybe they’d take the door.”

“With that number? With ZARRIN’s number?”

“Because that number was on the table. Whoever pressed it probably didn’t know what one twenty-seven was. Whose brother’s service number. What it would be set beside on a counterintelligence desk over there. What he saw was probably five lines. Target, time, content, reference, precedent.”

Leila closed her eyes. The colonel on the mountain. Someone laying four sheets of paper side by side. She had never seen that scene, but she could see it.

“We burned him. With the number we made to protect him.”

“We don’t know yet whether we burned him. If there was no engagement over there, he’s still sitting in his own chair.” The chief looked at her. “And Rowell didn’t ask about it because it went through that room. She said she sent up three lines. That’s probably true. That woman doesn’t say things that are wrong. Only, three lines probably weren’t the only thing that went up that night.”

The sun went behind the roof. The chief stood and picked up a watering can. He watered the seedlings. Six of them. He looked like a man who had decided to grow only what he could grow.

“Why are you telling me this now?”

“I couldn’t tell the Inspector General. A guess isn’t a complaint. And until last week I wasn’t sure myself. You came back with what she didn’t ask, so now I’m half sure.” He set the watering can down. “Leila. Now you have to go through that room to reach ZARRIN. That room called ZARRIN half the ceasefire. A valuable source gets protected. But what that room protects is the source, not the man. Don’t forget the difference.”

“Do you think the note got there?”

“Who knows,” he said. “The mailman doesn’t send a receipt. That’s the good thing about the mailman, and the bad thing.”

Back in the car, she turned her phone on, and there was a news alert. U.S. forces had struck Bandar Abbas. She read it and sat a long time without starting the engine. Six days since a letter said it had terminated. In that city there might be some nurse’s husband. She had no way of knowing the name.

“What that room protects is the source, not the man. Don’t forget the difference.”

Background Analysis
Between the Pause and the Strike — May 6 and 7

Project Freedom stopped after three days. On May 6 President Trump said there had been great progress toward an agreement with Iran and that the operation would be paused for a short period, to see whether a complete and final agreement was possible. The same day a U.S. aircraft used its cannon to disable the rudder of the Iranian tanker Hasna, which had been trying to run the blockade.

The next day, May 7, the United States said U.S. destroyers transiting the strait had come under a combined missile, drone and small-boat attack, and struck Iranian military sites at Bandar Abbas and on Qeshm Island. Iran claimed civilian areas had been hit and said it had targeted U.S. warships east of the strait. The same day the UAE announced it had intercepted two Iranian ballistic missiles and three drones, and that three people were injured. The United States said it had also attacked two empty tankers bound for Iran.

On paper the ceasefire held. But from the shelling of the Touska on April 19 to the strikes on the mainland on May 7, the clashes at sea were climbing one step at a time onto land. The attaché’s visit, the note, the rooftop in Isfahan, the notice Maryam received and the chief’s guess are all the author’s invention. This novel does not claim that anything like that actually happened to Iran’s air defense network on the night of April 7.
