Bahrain — Mina Salman Port, the coalition liaison cell at the headquarters of the U.S. Fifth Fleet
May 22, 2026 (Fri.) · Afternoon (Bahrain local time) — Ceasefire Day 45 · War Day 84
Scene 1 / 5

Two numbers came up on the screen on the same day. One was big. One was small.

U.S. Central Command said that through May 22 it had turned back 94 ships at the blockade line. The same day, Qatar joined the mediation between the United States and Iran. Qatar was a country Iran had struck in this war. The main mediator was still Pakistan.

Ninety-four. Lieutenant Kim Junho copied the number into his notebook. It meant that in forty days the U.S. Navy had turned around ninety-four ships going to or coming from Iran’s ports. Beside it he wrote another number. One. In the same forty days, the number of Korean ships trapped in the Gulf that had gotten out through the strait.

Ninety-four and one. The two numbers counted different things. One counted what was stopped, one what got out. But both happened on the same water. A big country’s ledger and a small country’s ledger were writing down the same sea differently.

Lieutenant Commander Mark Henderson shook his head at the news from Qatar.

“Qatar got hit in March. Their gas terminal. Now they’re standing with the country that hit them and making calls to us. They’re also the ones who talked the President down four days ago.”

“Isn’t that because they got hit, sir?”

“Meaning what?”

“If the war starts again, they’ll get hit again. Saudi too. The Emirates. You saw Barakah, sir. Those people don’t care who’s right. They only know that if the missiles start flying again, their power plants and water plants get hit. So they’re not taking a side. They’re standing on the other side of the war. Countries that live next door always do that arithmetic.”

Henderson looked at him a long time. His eyes were looking for the lieutenant of three months ago.

“Your country too.”

“We have twenty-four ships, sir. And one power plant.”

He didn’t say it like an excuse. Three months ago he would have. As a lieutenant from a country that had sent one liaison officer to its ally’s war instead of warships. Now he knew it was not a shameful place to stand. Someone had to stand on the other side of the war. In the place where you count ships and count people and watch to see if the road opens. A country that had been through a war knew the weight of that place. Seventy-some years ago this country, too, had been inside the arithmetic while other countries worked out its armistice.

In the evening one of the captains texted. The one whose daughter was taking the Suneung. Lieutenant. Word at the company is we’re next after the Universal Winner. Next week, they say. Can I believe it?

Kim Junho asked the embassy. The embassy asked Seoul. The answer came an hour later. In consultation. Date not set. He passed it on as it was. It is true they are consulting. Nobody can give a date yet. What goes around at the company is what goes around at the company. When a date is set, I will tell you first. Your daughter’s exam is in November. You will be home before then. That is what I believe, not what I know.

He thought of deleting the last sentence and sent it as it was. Writing what you believe apart from what you know. That was the grammar he had learned these three months. An analyst across the ocean and a researcher across the desert were learning the same grammar in the same three months.

Someone had to stand on the other side of the war. Not on a side. In the place where you count ships and count people and watch to see if the road opens.

Langley, Virginia — CIA headquarters, seventh floor, 7E-41
May 22, 2026 (Fri.) · Afternoon EDT — Ceasefire Day 45
Scene 2 / 5

Rowell slid a printout across the desk. Paper had come back to this room.

Paper. That was the first thing Leila Shirazi noticed. From the woman who had said she’d grown tired of paper. Power always came back to paper in the end.

“Eight days’ worth. What it’s put out on ZARRIN.”

It was an empty table. Eight lines of dates and nothing else. The content column was blank. For the first time, the machine was silent.

“Not one line since the night of May 14. Before that it put out two or three a day. What the source would do, how close counterintelligence had gotten, when the next contact should be. Then it just stopped.” Catherine Rowell leaned back in her chair. “I thought it was broken. It wasn’t. The operator on their end blocked it. No forecasts on any input with words like source, handler, asset.”

"Y.K."

Rowell’s eyes narrowed. Of course she knew that Leila knew those two letters. It was only the first time either of them had said them out loud.

“That’s right. She wrote this in the reason field. The model is not validated for predictions about individuals.” Rowell said the sentence once more in English. Slowly. “I looked at that reason for three days. Trying to refute it. I couldn’t.”

Leila kept still. One sentence written by a researcher in Seoul had held a desk on the seventh floor for three days.

“It was built to read radar. The rhythm of air defenses. The patterns in command traffic. That’s validated. Over years, thousands of times. But what one person will do tomorrow has never been validated once. We just figured it was good at the other things, so it would be good at that too. When it said point eight three, I believed the number. I never asked where the number came from.” She looked out the window. “That’s what your chief said in 2024. I thought at the time he was a coward.”

“And now?”

“I still think he’s a coward. I’ve just learned that sometimes the coward is right.”

It was the biggest concession she could make. Leila took it. She didn’t ask for more. That was how a big agency changed course, a little at a time. Without anyone saying they had lost.

“Is that why you called me in?”

“On ZARRIN there’s one assessment that reaches my desk now. Yours. Because the machine has shut its mouth. For three months I read your assessments next to what it put out. When they differed, I went with it. Now there’s nothing to put next to yours.” Rowell leaned forward. “So I’m asking. A person. That signals officer’s death. You wrote ‘monitoring.’ What’s behind that word?”

Leila steadied her breath. This time she didn’t hide that she was doing it. For the first time there was less to hide in this room.

“I’ll start with what I know. That man ran the dormant line at that command post. The line the source used when he first spoke to us, on March 28 and 30. So he was in a position to see what the source did. Whether he saw it, I don’t know.”

“Your guess.”

“I think he saw it and covered it. I have one reason. For seven weeks that command post’s weekly checklist gave the same number. The use count on the dormant line. The source used it twice, and the number didn’t go up. Someone wrote it down as not going up. The only one who could do that is the signals officer.”

“Where did you see that?”

“In the raw intelligence. It’s a form that goes up every Monday. The machine was probably eating it too. But it doesn’t read anything when a number doesn’t change. It takes a person to see that a number not changing is strange.”

Rowell nodded slowly.

“Then the source has lost the only person who knew his secret. Is he safer now, or in more danger?”

“I don’t know. That’s my answer. If it was an accident, he’s safer. If it wasn’t an accident, someone at that command post is erasing the people who know something, and the source is next. I don’t have the data to tell those apart. I can’t give you point-something either. If I made up a number like that, it would be a lie.”

The room was quiet. Leila expected her to be disappointed. This was a woman who had been taking numbers for thirty-five years.

“Look at next Monday’s checklist,” Rowell said. “The signals officer has changed, so the hand writing the numbers has changed. What number the new hand writes. That will tell you what state that command post is in now.”

Leila looked at her again. It was an instruction a machine couldn’t give. And it was a good one. A woman who had trusted people less was telling her to look at a number left by a human hand.

“If I made up a number like that, it would be a lie.” There is a room where “I don’t know” counts as an answer. Only after the machine went quiet.

Seoul, South Korea, Eunpyeong-gu — district public library, third-floor reading room
May 21, 2026 (Thu.) · 10 a.m. (Korea time) — Ceasefire Day 44
Scene 3 / 5

This had happened the day before. On the other side of the world, in another reading room.

Thursday, ten o’clock. Yoon Jaeha had the beacon-fire book open. He said he was about two-thirds through. That day Kang Yujin learned for the first time that he really was reading it. The cover hadn’t been a disguise.

“Cole sent a letter. A formal one. He wants the filter taken down.”

“On what grounds?”

“Breach of the memorandum. Unilateral change of function. He says your filtering out inputs about people narrows the agreed scope of output.”

“Were people in the agreed scope of output? I’ve never seen that memorandum.”

“I have.” He folded down the corner of a page. “They’re not. The memorandum says ‘indications analysis concerning the command-and-control systems of the target state.’ Systems. Not people. Their side crept past that scope a little at a time for five months. The four pages in January opened that door. Your filter didn’t narrow the scope. It put it back where it was. That’s what I wrote in the reply. The strongest thing a small country can say to a big one is the text of the agreement.”

“…You wrote it?”

“Yesterday. Not under my name.”

Kang Yujin looked at him a long time. The man who wore an interpreter’s nameplate and never said a word. The man who never hid that protecting her was a card this country could play. That man had defended her filter by citing the text of a memorandum she had never even seen. For the first time, the state was on her side.

“What about Priya? She said she’d put tags on.”

“They’ve been coming in since Monday, haven’t they?”

“Yes. They’re accurate. Much better than my word filter. Won’t she get hurt? She said she was doing it without telling anyone.”

“That’s her decision. Like you pushing the button.” A moment later he added, “It’s a good thing they have someone like you on their side too. There has to be at least one person like that on each end for a channel to work as a channel.”

Sun came into the reading room. Young people studying for the civil service exam had their heads down. The same faces as a month ago. They didn’t know their futures hung on a machine by some sea.

“They say the talks are in the final stage,” Kang Yujin said. “When the war ends, what happens to KR-7? Does it shut down?”

Yoon Jaeha closed the book before he answered.

“Dr. Kang. That channel didn’t open because of the war. It opened last November. The war started in February. The war made the channel busy. The war didn’t make it.”

“Then it won’t shut down.”

“It won’t. That’s what worries me. During a war, people watch. You watch, I watch, people like Priya on their side watch. If something goes wrong, the phone rings in the middle of the night. When the war ends, everybody goes off to do something else. The channel stays, the machine keeps eating, and only the people watching get fewer. Mr. Park signed the four pages before the war, too. When nobody was watching.”

She thought about that for a long time. She wanted the war to end. Every day. But the day the war ended could be the day her model was most dangerous. The day nobody asked anymore. Peace loosened the watching. In the place where the watching went slack, the next war grew.

“Then I have to keep watching.”

“You have to keep watching. And you have to set it up so it can be watched when you’re not there. The button, the filter, Format Rev. 2. If it’s all in your head, it leaves the day you leave. Put it in writing. Down to why you did it. So the next operator can read it.”

“You’re telling me to put up a signpost by the beacon tower.”

Yoon Jaeha laughed out loud for the first time. The first laugh she’d heard from him since they met.

“Have you read that book?”

“Just the title.”

“Toward the end there’s a passage. It says when the beacons failed, it usually wasn’t because the fire went out. Or because the beacon men ran off. The fire went up, but the document that said what the fire meant didn’t get passed down to the next soldier. Five fires go up, and nobody knows if it means the enemy crossed the border or the fighting has already started. Machines will be the same. The numbers get passed on. The meaning doesn’t.”

During a war, people watch. When the war ends, everybody goes off to do something else. The channel stays, the machine keeps eating, and only the people watching get fewer.

Northern Tehran, Evin — Professor Azad’s home
May 23, 2026 (Sat.) · Dawn (Tehran local time) — Ceasefire Day 46 · War Day 85
Scene 4 / 5

At dawn on the third day, Azad Moradi opened the envelope. After he had finished grieving first.

The memorial was in the afternoon. He had decided to read it before then. When he stood at the grave, he wanted to stand there as a man who already knew. What the boy had left in his keeping.

He slit the seal with a knife. One sheet came out. Military form paper. On the front were a few lines in his nephew’s hand. Neat numbers.

04.07 central switch, upstream connection. Cut 03:42:00. Restored 03:44:07. Duration 127 seconds. Format: maintenance order. “Ordered by” field: blank. Records for the interval: deleted. This record was copied by hand by the officer on watch before the deletion.

He read it three times. He knew all the words. He understood about half the meaning. April 7. He remembered that night. The night he handed out iodine tablets to children in the hospital basement. The night someone posted that a whole civilization would die. At three forty-two that night, something in this country had gone dark for two minutes and seven seconds. It didn’t say who turned it off.

Had the boy died because of this? He couldn’t tell. It was a few numbers. To the eyes of a philosophy professor, it didn’t look as heavy as one man’s life.

He turned the sheet over. The truth was always on the back.

There were more lines on the back. In pencil. A hastier hand than the front. There was a date. May 16. The day before the boy went to see the lawyer.

Uncle. The front is what I wrote down on the night of April 7. The back is what I learned in May.

The order came in from outside. Through the maintenance door of the switch brought in in 2017. That door had been there for nine years. Counterintelligence found it, and they think the enemy broke in.

But that door can’t be opened from outside alone. Someone has to draw the bolt from inside. I looked at the system diagram for three nights, and I found the trace of the bolt being drawn at 03:41:52 on April 7. Eight seconds before the order came in. The terminal that drew the bolt isn’t at this command post. It’s in Tehran. The third terminal at the General Staff Signals Directorate.

So it wasn’t only the enemy that turned off the net over our sky that night. Someone inside opened the door for them. I don’t know who. I don’t know why. It may have been selling out the country. Or it may have been to show the people who wanted to fire the missiles that night how naked we were. If so, that person was trying to stop the war. I can’t tell which.

This is all I know. On May 15 four men came from Tehran and took the original of the front. They asked if there was a copy. I said no. They don’t know that I know what’s on the back. Not yet.

Uncle. I didn’t tell the counterintelligence lieutenant colonel. He is a good man, but if he knew this, he would walk toward the General Staff and not come back. I didn’t tell the colonel either. The colonel is already carrying too much.

So I am giving it to you. You aren’t a soldier, you aren’t on any side, you are a man who reads. What to do is yours to decide. You can burn it. I won’t hold it against you. Only, I thought there should be one copy of this in the world. For when someone asks what happened that night. Even if no one ever asks.

Azad laid the sheet down on the desk. His hand couldn’t hold it.

Now he knew. It was as heavy as one man’s life. Heavier. This paper was the record of a night when a country had covered its own eyes with its own hand. And the boy had died without being able to tell whether the man who did it was a traitor or a peacemaker. In 1953, in this city too, hands from inside and outside had moved together. Then too, people had argued for a long time over who had sold the country and who had saved it.

How is choosing not to know different from knowing and keeping silent?

The boy had gone without hearing the answer. Azad thought he knew it now. To choose not to know is to protect yourself. To know and keep silent is to protect someone else. The boy had kept silent to protect the lieutenant colonel, to protect the colonel. And he had put the weight of that silence on one sheet of paper and sent it to his uncle. So as not to carry it alone and die.

For seventy-two years he had lived as a man who read. What others had written. What Plato wrote, what Saadi wrote, what his students wrote. He read it, explained it, taught it. A man who reads doesn’t get hurt. That was the good thing about reading.

Now he was a man entrusted. From one who reads to one entrusted. One step that had taken seventy-two years.

To choose not to know is to protect yourself. To know and keep silent is to protect someone else.

South of Tehran — Behesht-e Zahra Cemetery
May 23, 2026 (Sat.) · Afternoon (Tehran local time) — Ceasefire Day 46
Scene 5 / 5 — Final

The earth on the grave was still damp. Three-day-old earth.

The third-day memorial was short. A few relatives, a few officers from the command post. Someone passed around dates and halva. Colonel Darius Sepahvand stood at the very back. In civilian clothes, not uniform. Second Lieutenant Sohrab Jahangiri was beside him.

When people drifted off, Azad Moradi went over to the colonel. The second lieutenant understood and stepped back about ten paces.

The two men walked the path between the graves. The headstones in this section were new. The dates started on February 28. Azad read the birth years as he walked. 2003. 1999. 2006. In the very next section the headstones from the 1980s stood faded. One cemetery held the ages of two wars side by side.

“Colonel. There is one thing I taught the children. Before you fight, ask the name.”

“Rostam and Sohrab.”

“Did the boy tell you?”

“The lieutenant told me. That you read that passage in the hospital basement, Professor. My second lieutenant’s name is Sohrab.”

Azad looked back. The young second lieutenant stood at a distance, looking at a headstone.

“Then I will ask. Not the name. Something deeper than that. What was my nephew to you?”

Darius stopped walking. It wasn’t a question you could answer with a lie. That he was a good subordinate. An officer he valued. He had already said those things at the memorial. The old man wasn’t asking to hear them again.

“He was the man who carried my lie for me for seven weeks.”

Azad looked at him. For seventy-two years he had watched thousands of students answer questions. Faces pretending to know, faces hiding what they didn’t know, faces that really knew. This face was none of those. It was the face of a man who answers knowing the answer will hurt him.

“What lie?”

“That I can’t tell you. If you knew, Professor, you would get hurt. But the lieutenant saw what I did, and he wrote down what he saw as something he hadn’t seen. Every week. I didn’t order it. He decided it. And before he died he set it right. He found a way to set it right without hurting me.”

“So the boy was trying to protect you.”

“Yes.”

“He tried to protect you with this too.”

Azad took the paper from his inside pocket. He had brought it in the envelope, unfolded. He held it out to the colonel. Back side up.

“‘I didn’t tell the colonel either. The colonel is already carrying too much.’ That’s what it says. I am going against the boy’s wishes. He wanted you not to know.”

“Then why are you showing me?”

“I can’t read the numbers on this paper. A man who can’t read it can’t keep it. And.” The old man’s voice dropped. “The boy sent this to me so as not to carry it alone and die. I don’t want to carry it alone and die either. I am seventy-two.”

Darius took the paper. He read it. The front. Then the back.

The terminal that drew the bolt isn’t at this command post. It’s in Tehran. The third terminal at the General Staff Signals Directorate.

The cemetery wind lifted the corner of the paper. Among thousands of headstones, one sheet of paper shook.

When he read that line he couldn’t breathe for a while. The night of April 7. He had taken those one hundred twenty-seven seconds for something the other side did. Not for something someone of his own did. Picking up his numbers and using them. The counterintelligence lieutenant colonel had read it the same way. That the enemy had come in through a nine-year-old back door.

The door had opened from inside.

In his country’s General Staff there was someone who had done the same thing he had. Someone who had opened a door to the enemy. Who had that man lit a fire for? Hoping for what? Darius thought back to what he himself had hoped for that dawn in March. That the war would end. That no more seventeen-year-olds would die. That man might have hoped for the same thing. Or the exact opposite. In the summer of 1988 this country’s leader said he accepted the ceasefire as if drinking from a poisoned chalice. Someone was holding that chalice again.

And Farhan had known this. For five days. He had known it and not told him. The colonel is already carrying too much. The subordinate had protected his commander.

“Professor.” His voice didn’t sound like his own. “The lieutenant may not have died in an accident.”

“I have thought that too.”

“Whoever has this paper may be next.”

“I have thought that too. That is why I am asking you. What should be done with this? Should it be burned?”

Darius looked down at the paper. Burn it and it’s over. He had said it was the only one in the world. Burn it, and the old man is safe, and he is safe, and that man at the General Staff is safe. And no one ever knows what happened on the night of April 7. Or why Farhan died. This country’s history was full of paper burned that way.

I thought there should be one copy of this in the world. For when someone asks. Even if no one ever asks.

“Don’t burn it.”

“Then what?”

“There is one man this has to go to. The very man the lieutenant worried about. The counterintelligence lieutenant colonel. He is trying to find out who those four men were. Without this paper, he’ll dig in the wrong place and then give up. With it, he’ll walk toward the General Staff. As the lieutenant said, he may not come back.” Darius handed the paper back to the old man. “I can’t make that decision for him. It’s his life. But I can ask him. Whether he wants to know. Whether he still wants to know, when there are things that, once you know them, you can’t turn back from.”

“So you stand him in front of the door and ask.”

“That’s what you taught the lieutenant, Professor. That choosing not to know is your own.”

Azad put the paper back in the envelope. He put it in his inside pocket. He looked at the headstones for a long time.

“Colonel. One more question. That lie of yours. The one my nephew carried for you. Is it the same kind of thing as what is written on this paper?”

Darius didn’t answer. For a long time. The silence was the answer.

“I see,” the old man said. He wasn’t surprised. “Then you are the man in this country best able to guess why that man at the General Staff did it.”

“I can guess. I can’t believe it. There’s no rule that a man who did the same thing did it for the same reason.”

“That is what the boy couldn’t tell apart.”

“Yes.”

The two men came back to the grave. The second lieutenant was waiting. Someone had left a single rose on the damp earth. Azad stood before it and recited something under his breath. It was not a prayer. It was Saadi. He used to teach his students that the poem hangs on a wall at the international organization’s building in New York.

The children of Adam are limbs of one body. When one limb aches, the other limbs cannot rest.

The door had opened from inside. There is no rule that a man who did the same thing did it for the same reason. That was what he died without being able to tell apart.

The Other Side of the War — Qatar Joins

On May 22 Qatar joined the mediation between the United States and Iran. Qatar was a country Iran had attacked in this war. Four days earlier, on May 18, the emir of Qatar was one of three leaders who had asked President Trump to put off the attack “scheduled for tomorrow.” The Gulf monarchies are America’s allies and Iran’s neighbors, and if the war resumed they would be the first to be hit.

On May 24 there were reports that the United States and Iran were nearing a broader agreement. President Trump said a memorandum of understanding was being finalized and the Strait of Hormuz could reopen soon. The reported draft included a temporary halt to Iran’s uranium enrichment, talks on sanctions relief, and a phased reopening of sea lanes. Around the same time he proposed tying the deal to an expansion of the Abraham Accords, and Pakistan publicly refused.

But at sea the blockade went on through those same days. U.S. Central Command said it had turned back 94 ships through May 22. And the day after reports said a deal was close, U.S. forces would strike Iran’s southern coast again. The scenes in this episode — the back of the envelope, the conversation at the cemetery, the empty table on the seventh floor, the memorandum in the library — are all the author’s invention. The account of Iran’s communications network on the night of April 7 is not a real event.

Author’s Note
Qatar joins the mediationReal / May 22
Qatar stepped in as a mediator though Iran had attacked it. The main mediator was Pakistan. The conversation between Kim Junho and Henderson is fictional.
“Memorandum of understanding being finalized”Real / May 24
The reports of President Trump’s remarks and of the draft’s contents are real. The memorandum of understanding would not be signed until mid-June.
The back of the envelopeFictional
The switch’s back door, the bolt drawn from inside, the General Staff Signals Directorate terminal and the “technical committee” are all the author’s invention. This novel does not claim that any such person existed in the real Iranian military.
The third-day memorialCustom
In Iran, gatherings to remember the dead are held on the third day, the seventh day and the fortieth day after the funeral.
“The children of Adam are limbs of one body”Literature
Lines from Saadi’s Gulistan (“Bani Adam”). They are also woven into a Persian carpet that hangs at United Nations headquarters.
Scope of output in the memorandumFictional
The KR-7 memorandum of understanding, its technical annex and their text are fictional.
