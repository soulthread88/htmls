Lose the port, lose the nation. In EP.11, we examined how Alibaba, Temu, and Miniso are encircling Korea's consumer ecosystem. EP.12 operates on an entirely different scale. Sri Lanka lost 99 years of sovereign control over a single port. Greece surrendered 67% of its largest harbor to China amid a debt crisis. Africa's Djibouti became home to China's first overseas military base. What guarantee is there that this pattern won't repeat itself in Northeast Asia? Busan — the world's 7th-largest container port — and Incheon, the gateway for Korea-China trade: we trace what would happen if Chinese capital entered South Korea's heartland.

01The String of Pearls Strategy — China's Global Port Acquisition Roadmap

🔍 Top Searches: "What is the String of Pearls strategy?" · "China port acquisitions purpose" · "Belt and Road port investments"

China has been strategically acquiring ports around the world as a core pillar of the Belt and Road Initiative (BRI), announced by President Xi Jinping in 2013. The strategy is dubbed the "String of Pearls" because Chinese-funded ports along key Indo-Pacific maritime chokepoints link together like beads on a necklace.

As of 2024, Chinese capital has invested in more than 129 ports worldwide, with over 70 concentrated in the Indo-Pacific. The playbook is consistent: first approach with commercial investment and loans, then acquire operating rights and equity stakes, and finally convert the asset into a military or strategic foothold — a three-phase pattern.

DATA · AidData — Harboring Global Ambitions Report 2023
$2.19B Chinese funds injected into Hambantota Port (Sri Lanka) — the largest single-port investment globally

AidData's comprehensive analysis found that China invested in port projects across more than 129 countries between 2000 and 2021, concentrating over $8 billion in the top 10 ports alone. The first case of a commercial port converting to a military base was Djibouti, where China's first overseas military facility was established in 2017.

Source: AidData, "Harboring Global Ambitions: China's Ports Footprint and Implications for Future Overseas Naval Bases" (2023) — docs.aiddata.org

02Global Cases — The Countries That Lost Their Ports

🔍 Top Searches: "Hambantota port deal explained" · "Piraeus port China COSCO" · "debt-trap diplomacy examples"

China's port acquisition strategy has already materialized in dozens of countries. While circumstances and legal details vary, the common trajectory is economic dependency → transfer of operating rights → geopolitical subordination. The cases below offer critical lessons for South Korea.

🇱🇰
Sri Lanka
Hambantota International Port
Built with loans from the Export-Import Bank of China, Hambantota Port generated far lower cargo volumes than projected, plunging into deficit. In 2017, facing a national foreign exchange crisis, Sri Lanka transferred 70% equity and a 99-year operating lease to Chinese state-owned China Merchants Port Holdings (CMPort) for $1.12 billion.

The legal characterization of this deal is debated. The Diplomat and the Georgetown Journal note it was a separate lease agreement — not a direct result of debt default. However, CSIS and AidData argue that the transfer of port operating rights to China amid deepening economic dependency carries clear strategic implications, regardless of the formal legal structure.
⚠️ 99-Year Operating Rights Transferred
Source: CSIS, "Game of Loans: How China Bought Hambantota" — csis.org
Source: The Diplomat, "The Hambantota Port Deal: Myths and Realities" — thediplomat.com
🇬🇷
Greece
Port of Piraeus — Europe's Largest
Exploiting Greece's 2008 debt crisis, Chinese state shipping giant COSCO acquired the container terminals at Piraeus, expanding its stake from 51% in 2016 to 67% by 2021, effectively controlling Europe's largest port. COSCO recorded record pre-tax profits of €112.9 million in 2024.

The concern extends beyond economics. In January 2025, the U.S. Department of Defense placed COSCO on its list of companies with ties to China's military, raising acute security concerns about a NATO ally's largest port being operated by a Chinese state firm with potential access to U.S. and allied naval intelligence.
🔴 COSCO Holds 67% Controlling Stake
Source: Atlantic Council, "Greece's policy on China" (2025) — atlanticcouncil.org
Source: Asia Times, "China-operated ports raise security fears for US, NATO" — asiatimes.com
🇩🇯
Djibouti
Doraleh Multi-Purpose Port + PLA Military Base
Following massive investment in the Doraleh Multi-Purpose Port (DMP), China constructed its first-ever overseas military base in Djibouti in 2017. The facility is capable of hosting destroyers, amphibious assault ships, and aircraft carriers, and is believed to include electronic warfare systems. It sits just 12 km from Camp Lemonnier — the U.S.'s largest military base in Africa.

AidData warns that the Djibouti pattern is a template that can be replicated at other ports: a textbook "commercial investment → militarization" progression.
🔴 Fully Converted to Military Base
Source: AidData, "Harboring Global Ambitions" (2023) — docs.aiddata.org
🇵🇰
Pakistan
Gwadar Port — CPEC Centerpiece
As part of the $60 billion China-Pakistan Economic Corridor (CPEC), China built Gwadar Port and operates it under a 43-year lease through state-owned COPHC. The port sits just 70 km from the strategically vital Strait of Hormuz.

CSIS has reported ongoing negotiations for China to gain access to a Pakistani naval base nearby. Local Balochistan residents have staged fierce protests, saying "only Chinese workers benefit while we're left with debt and higher electricity bills."
⚠️ 43-Year Lease + Military Negotiations Underway
Source: CSIS, "Pakistan's Gwadar Port: A New Naval Base in China's String of Pearls" — csis.org
🇰🇭
Cambodia
Ream Naval Base
Cambodia's constitution prohibits foreign military bases, yet amid controversy over how that provision applies here, large Chinese-funded facilities were built at Ream Naval Base. Since December 2023, Chinese Type-056A corvettes have been permanently stationed there, and in April 2025, a joint China-Cambodia logistics and training center began official operations.

Equipped with a 300-meter deep-water pier and a 5,000-ton dry dock, the base sits near the Strait of Malacca — a position capable of choking off a key global energy corridor.
🔴 PLA Warships Confirmed (Dec 2023–present)
Source: Pentagon China Military Power Report 2024 — gr8-ideas.com
🇦🇺
Australia
Port of Darwin
In 2015, Darwin Port — a strategic northern hub adjacent to U.S. and Australian military facilities — was leased for 99 years to Chinese private firm Landbridge Group. President Obama immediately expressed concern. As of 2025, the United States is actively pursuing a buyout of the port.

This case drove home for Western allies the lesson that private investment can be just as much a national security threat as state-owned enterprise acquisitions.
⚠️ U.S. Buyout in Progress (2025)
Source: Asia Pacific Foundation of Canada, "Explainer: China's Global Port Play" — asiapacific.ca

03Busan & Incheon — The Real Risk South Korea Faces

🔍 Top Searches: "Busan port Chinese investment" · "Incheon port cargo volume" · "South Korea port security"

99% of South Korea's international trade moves by sea. Over $650 billion in cargo passes through Korean ports annually, and Busan is the world's 7th-largest container port. Energy imports are even more exposed: more than 70% of Korea's crude oil arrives from the Middle East and overseas markets, all traveling through sea lanes China is strategically working to control.

DATA · Busan Port Authority & Ministry of Ocean and Fisheries 2024
23M TEU Busan Port annual container throughput — World's 7th largest (2024)

Busan Port handles over 75% of South Korea's import and export cargo, making it a critical national infrastructure node. Incheon Port — the primary gateway for Korea-China trade — processes roughly 3 million TEU annually and is the economic lifeline of the Seoul metropolitan region. If these two ports were operated by foreign capital, the entire Korean economy could be held hostage in a crisis.

Source: Busan Port Authority Official Statistics 2024 / Tradlinx, "South Korea Port List" — blogs.tradlinx.com

Today, Busan and Incheon ports are operated by Korean public corporations (Busan Port Authority and Incheon Port Authority). However, Chinese capital penetration is possible through three vectors: ① acquiring equity stakes in private terminal operators; ② investing in port-adjacent logistics parks; ③ gaining technical access through port equipment supply (cranes and automated systems). China's ZPMC currently holds roughly 70% of the global market for port cranes, and the U.S. Department of Defense has issued a formal cybersecurity warning about these products.

04Energy Sovereignty — Lose the Port, Lose the Power Grid

Port sovereignty and energy sovereignty are inextricably linked. South Korea ranks among the lowest in the OECD for energy self-sufficiency, importing over 97% of its crude oil, natural gas, and coal. What happens when the routes and ports through which that energy arrives fall under foreign control?

FACT · IEA Energy Statistics / Korea Energy Economics Institute
97%+ South Korea's energy import dependency — Among the lowest self-sufficiency rates in the OECD (2024)

Korea imports 100% of its crude oil, 100% of its natural gas, and 98% of its coal. The energy flows through the Strait of Malacca, the South China Sea, and the East China Sea — sea lanes at the center of China's military expansion. China's military footholds at Djibouti, Gwadar, and Cambodia's Ream base are, in part, positioned to exercise wartime control over these exact energy corridors.

Source: IEA, Korea Energy Profile 2024 / AidData "String of Pearls" Analysis

05Both Sides of the Debate — Is Chinese Port Investment Actually a Threat?

Whether Chinese port investment always constitutes a threat is a genuine point of contention even among academics and strategists. Here is a balanced summary of the strongest arguments on each side.

✅ THE CASE FOR — Economic Benefits & Infrastructure Modernization

1) Infrastructure investment works: COSCO's investment in Piraeus genuinely boosted cargo throughput and contributed to the Greek economy. The port posted record profits in 2024.

2) Alternative financing for developing nations: Chinese capital provides funding for high-risk infrastructure projects that Western institutions decline to touch. Not every BRI country is a victim.

3) The Hambantota myth: According to analyses by the Georgetown Journal and The Diplomat, the Hambantota lease was a separate transaction — not a direct consequence of debt default — and Sri Lanka's formal sovereignty over the port is legally intact.

⚠️ THE CASE AGAINST — Strategic Dependency & Security Threats

1) Official U.S. DoD Warning (2025): The United States placed COSCO on its list of companies with ties to China's military and warned of the potential for intelligence collection on U.S. and NATO naval movements at COSCO-operated ports.

2) The Djibouti Template: AidData documents the "commercial port to military base" transition as an already-proven pattern — now repeating in Cambodia and Pakistan.

3) China's National Intelligence Law (2017): Chinese companies are legally required to cooperate with Chinese government intelligence requests. If a port operator is a Chinese firm, cargo and warship movement data could flow to Beijing.

Source: U.S. DoD COSCO Blacklisting (Jan 2025); Reuters "Greece examines impact of COSCO blacklisting" — usnews.com

For a country like South Korea, which is entirely dependent on maritime trade, this risk is existential in nature."

06What Korea Is Missing — The Invisible Threats

Many Koreans assume, "our ports are state-run, so we're fine." But the real risks materialize in the blind spots.

⚠️ Three Critical Blind Spots

① Chinese-Made Port Cranes (ZPMC): The U.S. Department of Defense issued a formal cybersecurity warning in 2024 about products from China's ZPMC, which supplies roughly 70% of port cranes worldwide. The concern: remote-access capabilities may be embedded in the hardware. Korean ports' use of ZPMC cranes warrants a dedicated security audit.

② Investment in Port-Adjacent Logistics Parks: Even if direct port operating rights are blocked, Chinese capital can find back-channel entry through port-adjacent warehouses, automated logistics systems, and IT infrastructure.

③ Port IT and Data Systems: When Chinese companies participate in port operating systems that concentrate cargo information, ship movements, and shipper data, a data sovereignty problem emerges — regardless of who formally operates the terminal.

Source: U.S. DoD Port Crane Security Alert (2024) / USCC Annual Report 2025

07Conclusion — What Korea Must Do Now

South Korea still holds its port sovereignty. But the experiences of countries around the world offer a consistent warning: the moment when economic temptation is strongest is also the most dangerous moment. Preemptive and systematic defense is essential.

⚓ Defending Port & Energy Sovereignty — 5 Action Strategies
What Korea's government, businesses, and citizens must do right now
STRATEGY 01
🏛️
Legal Protections for Critical Infrastructure
Designate Busan and Incheon ports as national security assets by law, mandate screening of foreign capital seeking port operating rights or equity stakes, and introduce a Golden Share regime.
STRATEGY 02
🔍
Immediate Port Equipment Security Audit
Conduct cybersecurity audits of Chinese-manufactured cranes and automated systems (including ZPMC products), and establish response protocols in line with U.S. DoD warnings.
STRATEGY 03
⚡
Diversify Energy Import Routes
Secure alternative energy corridors to reduce Malacca Strait dependency. Diversify energy supply routes through Australia, Qatar, and Norway, and strengthen strategic energy reserves.
STRATEGY 04
🤝
Strengthen U.S.–Korea Port Security Cooperation
Build a joint Korea-U.S. port security framework aligned with the U.S. "Strategic Ports Reporting Act" (2025), and leverage QUAD cooperation for broader multilateral maritime security.
STRATEGY 05
📡
Secure Port Data Sovereignty
Restrict foreign company participation in port operating IT systems, mandate domestic storage of port operations data, and legislate data residency requirements for all port management systems.

In EP.11, we saw how shopping cart sovereignty is under threat. In EP.12, we've confirmed that the threat extends to ports and energy. Sri Lanka lost 99 years. Greece handed over 67% of a NATO-critical port. Laos gave Beijing the switch to its national power grid.

South Korea still has time to choose. But that window is closing fast. To avoid repeating the mistake of mortgaging long-term sovereignty for short-term economic gains, Korea must now treat its ports and energy infrastructure as a matter of national survival — not just commerce.

"A $1 bargain in your shopping cart is a supply chain problem.
Busan Port's operating rights are a national survival problem.
Both threats share the same root."
