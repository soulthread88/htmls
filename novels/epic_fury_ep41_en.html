There were twenty-six lines in the notebook. Twenty-four had a line drawn through them. Six months were inside those lines.

The memorandum of understanding held for twenty-one days. On June 20 Iran declared the strait closed again, citing Israeli airstrikes on Lebanon, and for three days from June 26 the United States and Iran exchanged attacks. On July 7, when Iran attacked merchant ships in the Strait of Hormuz, including a Qatari liquefied natural gas carrier, U.S. forces struck more than 80 Iranian targets the next day, and President Trump declared the memorandum over. The United States resumed its blockade of Iranian ports on July 14. U.S. forces struck Iran thirteen nights in a row, through July 23. On August 17, the 60-day deadline for a final agreement passed.

Lieutenant Kim Junho ran his fingertip down the twenty-four lines. Each had a date. 5.20. 6.10. And then twenty-two lines in the nine days from 6.22 to 6.30. The ship whose line he had drawn first on the chart on the night of June 18 went out on June 22. The date he had written as “around” turned out right. He knew it was luck and not skill, and so after that he never nailed down a date again.

The ship of the captain with the two periods went out on June 27. From the Gulf of Oman the captain sent this. Lieutenant. There was a last..

Two lines were left. War always left the last lines.

On July 1 the Ministry of Oceans and Fisheries said that of the 26 Korean ships inside the Strait of Hormuz, all 24 that had planned to transit had gotten out. When the war began, those ships had 146 Korean crew aboard. In early September two Korean ships still remained inside the strait. One was the Namu, hit in May; its repairs finished, it lay at anchor off Dubai with seven Korean crew aboard. In early September the presidential office confirmed it was reviewing a troop deployment to Hormuz.

One line was the Namu. The ship hit in the stern at three-thirty in the afternoon on May 4. Four months ago he had written source of fire unconfirmed beside that line. The words still hadn’t been corrected. The ship had been fixed. The sea the fixed ship had to go out into had turned rough again. A ship could be fixed; an attack with no name could not.

The other line was a tanker. Her captain had decided not to go out in June. He said the cargo he was to load was still on the pier. Kim Junho hadn’t been able to talk him out of it then. In June the sea really was open for a while, and the captain had an owner, and the owner had a contract.

On September 9 the U.S. military said it had destroyed five Iranian tankers in retaliation for an attempted missile attack on its warships, and the Revolutionary Guards claimed to have struck two U.S. vessels and eight tankers in the strait. The United States denied any of its ships were hit. The British military said several merchant ships had been hit and disabled in the northern Gulf and the Gulf of Oman. Near Dubai, one crew member on a tanker was killed and one went missing. The International Maritime Organization counted nineteen seafarers killed in the Strait of Hormuz and Middle East waters in this war through late August.

That was yesterday. Near Dubai. He had traded texts all night with the Namu’s chief mate. We’re all right. We saw lights. They were far off. He decided to believe far off. On May 4 too, someone would have said far off. He now wrote what he chose to believe apart from what he knew.

There was a cable from headquarters on his desk. Report the on-site liaison officer’s judgment regarding the deployment review by September 11.

He had been writing it since yesterday. It had come to three pages. On the first page he wrote facts. Two ships left. Their positions, drafts and crew numbers. Seventeen of our nationals inside the strait. The number and locations of merchant ships hit in the past two weeks. The channels the U.S. military escorted and the ones it didn’t. On the second page he wrote what he didn’t know. Who fired on last night’s tanker. Who fired on the Namu in May. Whether one more warship would change the answers to those questions.

The third page was one paragraph. The paragraph that took longest.

This liaison officer submits no judgment on whether to deploy. It is a larger question than can be seen from this post. However, the following is reported. Since May, the 24 ships of ours that left this strait did so not under warship escort but through consultation and application with the Iranian side. For the remaining 2 ships and 17 people, that channel is still alive. Whatever is decided should be done in a way that does not cut that channel.

He looked a long time at the phrase submits no judgment. The man he was in March would have thought it cowardly. What a twenty-nine-year-old lieutenant had learned in six months was that an officer is someone who knows how far his knowledge goes. And that what a small country has to keep, facing a big war, is not a flag but a road that is already alive.

There was a postcard in the drawer. A San Diego postmark. A photo of a lawn. Three lines on the back. Lieutenant. I mow once a week. Still using the chart? I hear two lines are left. Let me know when you close it. — M.H.

The chart was taped to the wall. Henderson’s lines and his lines now overlapped past telling apart. The thin lines of June 18. The three red X’s of July 7. The blockade line, thick again from July 14. Six months of two countries’ officers, layered on one sheet of paper.

He didn’t close the notebook. He left it open on the corner of the desk. So the two lines showed.

He didn’t close the notebook. He left it open on the corner of the desk. So the two lines showed.

Through the window you could see Bukhansan. It was a room with a window.

Kang Yujin had had that window for three months. On July 1 the nameplate changed, and so did her business card. On page four of her appointment papers, one paragraph had gone in, in writing. The operator may halt transmission on her own authority and reports the reason afterward. The other side held out for three weeks, then accepted it. Yoon Jaeha never told her what happened in those three weeks.

There were twelve sheets of paper on her desk. One a week. The tally that came every Friday from the far end of the channel. The form was one line.

Action taken on the basis of this output — Yes: 0 / No: 7.

For twelve weeks the Yes box had been zero. Zero was the number she had kept for four months.

On the morning of July 8 she confirmed that the machine had been wrong. memorandum: terminated (public statement, 07-08). forecast 06-18: holds through 08-16, UNCERTAIN (0.64). realization 0/1. note: no precursor in ingest. No precursor. The machine hadn’t known what would happen in the strait on the night of July 7. The people who decided it hadn’t written it down first in any document.

That day she added one line of operator note. It said one in three. This was the one.

Being wrong hurt. It hurt that the paper collapsed after twenty-one days, and it hurt that the machine had said two times in three it wouldn’t. Only, no one had sent a ship out believing it. No one had flown anything believing it, either. Friday’s sheet told her so. Zero.

This morning a different form had come. Not from the other side but from this one. From above.

Analysis request. 30-day outlook on the probability of our ships and vessels in the strait being hit, if our naval vessels are dispatched. Include confidence. Reply by September 11.

She read it and looked out the window. The mountain had the light of early autumn. The same mountain had looked down on this city in the year of the Imjin War, and in 1950.

One night in May she had realized it. When this machine predicted other people’s actions, it was simply right or wrong. When it predicted its own side’s actions, a loop formed. The side that read the answer moved, the movement changed the answer, and the machine counted the changed world as its own hit. In spring that loop had been across the sea. This morning someone had asked her to make one more of those loops on this side. They wouldn’t have meant any harm. A number makes a decision easier. Decisions made easier had led countries into war many times.

She wrote a reply. In the margin of the request form, by hand.

Reply: Will not perform. Reason — this system has not been validated to predict what we ourselves will do. If we read its answer and act, the answer changes, and the system cannot tell the difference (handover memo, item 2). What this decision needs is not a probability but the judgment of someone on site who knows the ships one by one. I understand there is such a person in Bahrain. — Operator

When she finished she read the last sentence again. She didn’t know the person in Bahrain. Not even his name. In June Yoon Jaeha had mentioned him once in passing. That there was a lieutenant who knew all twenty-six ships by heart.

She didn’t delete that sentence. It was the only greeting she could send a lieutenant whose face she didn’t know.

In the evening the 23:50 table would come out. In Format Rev. 2, through Filter Rev. 1. She would read it, add a note saying it could be wrong, and wait for Friday’s sheet. The machine was still on. The channel hadn’t closed. What had changed was that the person sitting in front of the button had it on paper that the button was hers, and that at the far end there was now a box that counted yes and no.

They were things far too small to stop a war. She knew that.

Only, for three months now, no one had acted on that machine’s words. What the small things kept was not small.

A number makes a decision easier. So she didn’t give one.

Scene 3 / 5

The shell hung from the child’s neck on a string. The medal the war had left a seven-year-old.

Nariman rested her chin on the roof railing and fiddled with it. A white shell, a little chipped at the edge. Baba had made the hole. Spending one of his seventy-two hours on it, with an awl borrowed from the hospital’s maintenance department.

Seventy-two hours was three days. On the evening of the third day he walked out through the same gate. June 21. And seventeen days later the war came back. The peace was as short as a three-day leave.

On July 8 Iranian state media reported that eight service members had been killed in Bandar Abbas and Bushehr by the U.S. attacks. The next day Iran’s health ministry said 14 people had been killed and 78 wounded by U.S. attacks. Over the following two months, explosions were repeatedly reported in Bandar Abbas. On September 1, Iranian state media reported, a U.S. strike hit a place in Kuhestak in Hormozgan province where a wedding was being held, killing five people, including two children, and wounding more than 60.

Maryam stood behind her daughter. The two keys were back in her pocket. The generator room and the pharmacy storeroom. On June 19 she had returned them to the director. On the morning of July 8 the director, without a word, put them back on her desk. The war came back as keys.

On her phone was this morning’s line. Alive. The sea was loud yesterday. How’s the shell.

The lines that used to start with Quiet today had started with Alive since July. He had decided that. Because quiet days had become rare. Of sixty-five mornings, twice that word had not come. Both times it came in the evening.

“Maman. Am I really going to school?”

“In two weeks. On the first of Mehr.”

“Even though it’s war?”

“Even though it’s war. There are times tables you didn’t learn last year.”

“I learned all the times tables from Grandpa. Up to seven.”

Professor Azad Moradi put down his book in the chair by the roof door. It wasn’t the Saadi. A different book, its spine newly glued. It smelled of glue.

“Eight and nine are left. I left those for the school. A teacher needs something to teach.”

“How many books have you fixed, Grandpa?”

“Fourteen. Twenty-three to go.”

“When you fix them all, are you going to Canada?”

The old man put off answering a little. His daughter had called in mid-July. It came. The envelope inside the envelope. I didn’t open it. I put it in the pharmacy safe. And after a long pause. Father, the war has started again, and the books matter that much? He said they did. He couldn’t explain. In August the lieutenant colonel in Evin had sent one line through someone. Peacetime work is slow and wartime work is slower. But I’m doing it.

“I said I’d think about it. I didn’t say I’d go.”

“Grown-ups always say they’ll think about it. Baba too. There might be a rotation, might be.”

Azad laughed. Without sound. A seven-year-old had put her finger exactly on the way grown-ups talk.

The sun was going down. The city’s lights came on one by one. There seemed to be fewer than on that evening in June. Nariman held the shell to her lips and murmured something. Maryam couldn’t tell if it was a prayer or the times tables. In this city the two had long made the same sound.

“Maman. In June Grandpa said the day Baba comes in through the door is the day my war ends.”

“So it was.”

“Baba came in and then went back out. So did my war end and then start again?”

Maryam couldn’t find an answer. She looked at the professor. The old man was silent a long while, then got up from his chair and came to the railing. Leaning on his cane, he stood beside the child.

“I was wrong, Nariman.”

The child looked up.

“The day it ends isn’t one day, it turns out. At seventy-two I learned that twice in seven months. Once in April, once in June. There’s a day someone comes in through the door, and a day he goes back out, and a day he comes in again. Only later do people add them all up and call it the end. The person in the middle of it doesn’t know which day is the day. It was like that in 1988 too. We didn’t know that summer of the ceasefire was the end.”

“Then what do you know?”

“That this morning the word came. And that what hangs around your neck is a real seashell.”

Nariman looked down at the shell. Then she nodded. Too slowly for a child that age.

Maryam’s phone buzzed. Lines rarely came in the evening. She held her breath and looked at the screen.

Forgot. Today somebody put shells in a row where the west pier was. Twenty-some. Nobody knows who. Looked like Nariman’s drawing.

She showed it to her daughter. The child read it, stayed still a long time, then gripped the string around her neck. Someone was counting the west pier’s children for her.

“The day it ends isn’t one day, it turns out. There’s a day someone comes in through the door, and a day he goes back out, and a day he comes in again. Only later do people add them all up and call it the end.”

The sixty days didn’t run out. They stopped at twenty-one. The paper’s clock couldn’t beat the sea’s clock.

Darius Sepahvand met it underground. Before dawn on July 8, when the reports from the southern sectors started pouring in all at once again, he wasn’t surprised. He was only a little surprised at himself for not being surprised. That night he sent his daughter one line. I’ve seen it all. Don’t come. His daughter answered. I know. Because for you it’s never just me, either.

The U.S. military said that over thirteen nights in July it struck Iran’s air defense systems, coastal radars, missile and drone facilities and command posts. The attacks stopped on July 25 and resumed four days later. On August 25 the U.S. secretary of state informed allies that the United States would not resume major combat operations against Iran for the time being. On September 2 the U.S. defense secretary extended the deployment of about 50,000 U.S. troops in the region into 2027. On September 9 Jordan said it had intercepted 18 of 20 ballistic missiles fired from Iran.

The main building wasn’t rebuilt. There was no reason to. Underground the screens went from three to five, the pipes still dripped, and the watch could no longer sleep without the sound. The one battery left became two. One August night a launcher no one asked about came up the mountain on a truck. The war became daily life not on the ground but under it.

Sohrab Jahangiri became a first lieutenant. The scar on his forehead stayed. He was the signals officer now. Not the acting one. The checklist in the third drawer was filled in every Monday in his handwriting. In the box for the dormant line Q counter, the same number had been written for sixteen weeks. 419. It didn’t go up anymore.

The counterintelligence lieutenant colonel came once in August. Alone again. He didn’t talk about drawers. He talked about something else. That the man who had put in for discharge withdrew it on July 8. Because the war came back. That the man still reported every day to the room with Terminal 3. I get his mess sheet every day. He doesn’t eat chicken on Tuesdays. I’ve become a man who knows things like that. And as he stood he added one more thing. Both drawers are shut.

0600 exactly.

Darius stood in front of the screen. At this hour on March 30 he had turned the search radar on for 127 seconds. The last three digits of his brother’s service number. After that the number came back to him three more times. Once as an answer, once from a hand he didn’t know, once as an echo a month late. In between, a wrong number came up once too. And then it stopped. Since May 14 nothing had come up in the sky at those coordinates. For seventeen weeks.

He knew what that silence was. Someone on the other side was keeping it. Keeping from flying anything. He knew how hard that was. He had been doing it himself every day for four months. Not firing, not switching on, not answering. The least visible work in war. History books didn’t record that kind of work.

Farewell, honest soldier. He had sent those words. And he hadn’t asked who had relieved her. In three months he had sometimes thought about that second half. Had that person been relieved? Gone to another post? Was someone else sitting at that desk now, someone who knew his sector only as a number?

There was no way to know. There was one thing he could do.

Sohrab brought the draft of the morning report. Four lines. Darius read.

Eastern Sector 3. As of 0600, September 10. No unidentified tracks. No engagement. Batteries available: 2. Watch, 12, all hands, carrying out duties at alternate command post.

“You don’t need to put in ‘all hands’ anymore, Colonel. The General Staff has flagged it twice as a word that’s not on the form.”

“Put it in.”

“I’ve been putting it in for three months.”

“Put it in for three months more.”

The first lieutenant didn’t ask why. He half knew why. Since the colonel told him to make sure to put that word in, in the red light before dawn on June 11. The other half he chose not to know. That was what the dead signals officer had left him. How to keep something without knowing. This country’s next generation was inheriting that way.

Darius picked up the pen. The confirmation box. For thirty-one years he had written his name in that box. Thousands of times. Most of them nobody read. They went into files and turned yellow.

These four lines, someone read. He believed someone read them. There were no grounds for the belief. Things with grounds are things you know, not things you believe.

He wrote. Carefully. So the one who read it wouldn’t mistake a single letter.

Confirmed: sector commander Colonel Sepahvand.

Not firing, not switching on, not answering. The least visible work in war.

Scene 5 / 5 — Final

Leila Shirazi opened the window. As she did every morning.

It was the hundred and thirteenth morning. She had counted from May 21. From the day after the signals officer died. She opened it on weekends too. On Independence Day, and on July 8. That day the four lines came six hours late, and for those six hours she didn’t move from her desk.

Today it was on time. Collected 0241Z.

Eastern Sector 3. As of 0600, September 10. No unidentified tracks. No engagement. Batteries available: 2. Watch, 12, all hands, carrying out duties at alternate command post. Prepared by: First Lieutenant Jahangiri. Confirmed: sector commander Colonel Sepahvand.

All hands. The words had been there for three months. Words not on the form. She had never asked why they weren’t taken out. The moment she asked, they would become a sentence in an assessment, get a number, go up to someone’s desk. She just read them. Every morning. As the one receiving them.

On August 17 the sixty days ran out. The day of review. The war had already started again, and the question that came down from above was shorter and more urgent than in June. Do we wake the source? She wrote the fourth handler comment. Two pages. She still knew the last paragraph by heart.

On the request for the probability that the source will wake and carry out tasking: none given. A number without grounds is a lie. Instead the following is recorded. For 89 days, without missing one, the source has signed in the same place under the same name. That is all this desk knows, and this desk judges it to be enough. Recommendation: remain dormant.

Catherine Rowell read it and sent it back with one line. Retain. Next review to be set by successor. — C.R. She would leave this building in twenty days. Nobody knew yet who would sit in that room. When Leila thought of the day that person would come, her hands went a little cold. When she thought of the fourth comment sitting in the file, they went a little less cold. In this building paper lives longer than people.

Behind the photo of her mother in her wallet, there were now three slips of paper.

She got the third on the last Friday in June. In the backyard in Arlington. That day the chief picked his first tomato. From the plant of the six that got the most sun. He cut it in half, salted it, gave her one side, and with his other hand passed her something wrapped in foil. The lunch was good. They said it was the last lunch.

After she ate the tomato she unfolded it. Persian. The mailman’s handwriting.

Farewell, honest soldier.

And beneath it, the words of the one who passed it on and the mailman’s words. He read the paper and that evening took all his people down below. Two days later the top came down. Twelve lived. This is the last pouch.

That day in the backyard she cried a long time. The chief pretended to tie up tomato stems. The kindest lie of a man who had spent thirty years in intelligence.

She knew the line. From the first scene of that book. The farewell to a sentry going off watch. She knew there was a question after it too. Who hath relieved you? The colonel hadn’t sent that second half. She thought for many days about why, and finally settled on one answer. By not asking, he had let her go. Told her she could leave. That she had no duty to answer.

So she didn’t answer. She flew nothing. Not on the July nights when the sixty days collapsed, not in August when those above said to wake him. That was what he had asked, and it was the only thing she could pay back for the six minutes of April 28. Inside a great power’s intelligence agency, the only way an analyst could repay a debt to an enemy officer was silence.

Only, there was an answer. An answer she didn’t send.

She opened her notebook. The paper notebook. The one she moved to from the screen in April. She wrote today’s date, and under it one line. The line she had written every morning since that Friday in June.

No one has relieved me. I am still here.

Sam Farrell passed with his coffee and glanced at her screen. He still couldn’t read Persian. He read her face.

“He there?”

“He’s there. All hands.”

He nodded and went on. That exchange happened every morning too. Daily life in wartime held on through small rituals like that.

On the wall screen the news ran. A port on the Red Sea had fallen, it said. Something had hit a Saudi pipeline, it said. The deployment of fifty thousand U.S. troops had been extended into next year, it said. The war was in its seventh month, and no one had heard how it ends. The question a captain had put to a reporter in the spring was still waiting for an answer.

Leila didn’t look at that screen. She read the four lines once more. A small name, not the big news, set the terms of her morning.

On the other side of the world, three floors under a mountain, a man had written his name carefully again this morning. So the one who read it wouldn’t mistake it. He didn’t know who read it. She knew who wrote it. They had never seen each other’s faces, and never would. Between two countries that had called each other enemies for forty-seven years, there was a line held up by one name and one pair of eyes.

Halt. Who goes there?

That dawn in March, she had asked that. He answered with a light. Six months later, each of them had come to know the answer to that question. It wasn’t a name. Not a rank, not a country.

Someone who is there every morning.

She didn’t close the window. She would open it tomorrow morning too.

No one has relieved me. I am still here. How long will the mornings of keeping that post go on? Before that question, this war has not yet ended.

Closing Part One · Afterword
September 10, 2026 — The War in Its Seventh Month

The war that began on February 28, 2026, went through the April 8 ceasefire and the June 17 Islamabad Memorandum of Understanding, but did not end. The memorandum collapsed on July 8, and the United States resumed its blockade of Iranian ports on July 14. On August 17 the deadline for a final agreement passed, and Iran refused to negotiate an extension of the memorandum. In late August U.S. officials confirmed that the U.S. Navy had cleared some 100 suspected mines from the main shipping lane of the Strait of Hormuz. On September 1 the United States said it would respond tanker for tanker and began targeting tankers of Iran’s national oil company.

By around September 10 the war had spread to two seas. In Hormuz, the management body Iran set up stood against a southern route backed by the U.S. military; in the Red Sea, after the Houthis took the port of Mocha, there were reports that they had seized Perim Island in the middle of the Bab el-Mandeb. Saudi Arabia temporarily shut its East-West Pipeline after a drone attack on September 10. The U.S. Congressional Budget Office said the United States spent more than $38 billion on the war from February 28 to August 1. U.S. military deaths stood at 17 as of July 19. Lebanon’s health ministry said on September 7 that 4,362 people had been killed by Israeli attacks since March 2. A week later, on September 17, President Trump said he faced a big decision on whether to resume large-scale attacks or pursue a diplomatic deal.

The war in this novel follows what actually happened. What the author invented are the people. Darius Sepahvand, Leila Shirazi, Kim Junho, Kang Yujin, Maryam and Nariman and Davood, Azad and Farhan Moradi, Sohrab Jahangiri, Naser Taheri, Tariq Mehmood, Catherine Rowell, Mark Henderson — they, the papers they passed to each other, the number 127, the analysis model and its output, and the underground command post do not exist. The novel’s machine was never portrayed as the cause of any real attack or agreement. What it set in motion, the characters of this novel never manage to prove. What they prove is something smaller. That you can choose not to fly something, that you can turn something off, that you can write down that you don’t know.
