Episode 8, “The Nuclear Threshold”

Scene 1. Natanz, Isfahan Province, Iran — uranium enrichment complex

March 21, 2026 (Sat.) · morning, Tehran time — War Day 22

The ground moved first.

Vibration is faster than sound. The people left in the Natanz complex took it through the soles of their feet before they heard the blast. The concrete floor shook like an animal. The fluorescent lights flickered. The alarm went off. The alarm always comes late.

On March 21 the United States hit Iran’s Natanz nuclear facility with bunker-buster bombs. Iran said its uranium enrichment center had come under air attack. The International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) urged military restraint to avoid the risk of a nuclear accident, and Russia condemned the strike as a flagrant violation of international law. The same day the Israeli military said it had struck a nuclear weapons development and research facility at Malek Ashtar University of Technology in Tehran. Natanz had been hit before, in June 2025, and in this war on March 2. Now it was hit again.

The underground halls lay tens of meters under earth and concrete. After the bombing last June, nobody outside knew what was spinning down there, or how much. The people inside knew. In 2010 the centrifuges had gone mad from a code nobody could see and torn themselves apart. Now the mountain shook. Those people were feeling their way up through dark passages with their hands.

The vibration came twice, three times. Then it stopped. The quiet after it stopped was worse. Somebody took out a dosimeter. The needle did not move. This time.

When you bomb a nuclear site, a promise without a name breaks. The promise not to touch the places where no one can say what will happen. This war has broken that promise many times, and every time the needle has stayed still. So far.

Scene 2. Langley, Virginia — CIA Iran Operations Center

March 21, 2026 (Sat.) · 4:30 a.m. EDT / noon in Tehran — War Day 22

A number that had not budged for twenty-one days changed.

At first Dr. Leila Shirazi did not believe her eyes. After her aunt’s call yesterday she had slept three hours. The deepest sleep in twenty-one days. When she got back to her desk the cycle readout on the right-hand screen was not 5.7.

5.2 seconds.

She went back through the log. The time the first bomb fell on Natanz. Forty minutes later Fordow’s pulse sped up. 5.6. 5.5. 5.3. And it stopped at 5.2. The amplitude was up too.

“Sam,” she called. “Come here.”

Sam Farrell stood in front of the screen a long time without speaking.

“Natanz gets hit, so Fordow speeds up?”

“It could be coincidence,” Leila said. An analyst had to say that first. “If it isn’t, the people under that mountain are hearing what’s happening outside. It means somebody gave an order. This is the only place left, so hurry.”

She remembered the three hypotheses she had written down two weeks ago. Overload in old equipment. Step-by-step expansion. An order to raise output. It was the third. The one she had feared most.

A more uncomfortable fact followed. Today Washington had hit Natanz. While it was at it, it could have hit Fordow. It did not. Even with five of her reports pointing at that mountain. Somebody was leaving that mountain alone on purpose. This agency had first laid hands on Iran in 1953. Then too the decision was made outside the reports. Not knowing why was more unsettling than the faster signal.

She wrote the cable.

Fordow emission cycle shortened from 5.7 to 5.2 seconds, amplitude increased. Change began approx. 40 minutes after the Natanz strike. Assessment: order to accelerate operations likely. With Iranian retaliation against Dimona expected, the logic of targeting each other’s nuclear sites is hardening on both sides. Analyst’s view: recommend contact through a third country before this logic goes further. Pakistan is the most realistic channel.

The last sentence was not an analyst’s to write. The chain of approval did not like sentences like that. She did not delete it. She did not know that six days earlier a lieutenant in Bahrain had written the same kind of sentence.

Bombs break buildings. But every time a bomb falls, the people in the places not yet hit work faster.

Scene 3. Dimona, Israel — residential neighborhood

March 21, 2026 (Sat.) · evening, Israel time — War Day 22

Galit Amir believed her home was safe.

The logic was simple. In the desert next to this city there was a facility the country would neither confirm nor deny. Around a facility like that, the air defenses are laid down layer on layer. So this city was the safest. She was fifty and ran a nursing home. Since the war she had spent more nights sleeping in the shelter. Still she had not thought a missile would really fall here.

The siren went off.

Ninety seconds to the shelter. She knew that time in her body. She had practiced. She pushed two wheelchairs in, and the moment she shut the door the shock came. The whole building shook. Dust poured down. The power went out. In the dark somebody began to pray.

On the evening of March 21 an Iranian missile fell on Dimona in southern Israel and a building collapsed. Depending on the count, 64 to more than 78 people were wounded, and one child was in critical condition. Iran said it had aimed at the Shimon Peres Negev Nuclear Research Center in response to attacks on Natanz and Bushehr. Then missiles fell on the nearby city of Arad, wounding 116, seven of them seriously. The same day in Tel Aviv missile debris fell on a kindergarten.

When the door opened the building across the street was gone. Half of it, to be exact. The wall had been cut away as if with a knife. Somebody’s living room was open to the sky. A sofa hung three floors up.

“I thought we were safe here.”

She was not saying it to anyone. She said it to the air. One of the old men from the nursing home took hold of her sleeve. He had come to this desert in 1948. He had built a country once, and grown old believing that country would protect him. The old man was not looking at the fallen building. He was looking at the eastern sky. Out across the desert, toward that facility no one ever called by name.

Without using a nuclear weapon, just by aiming at a nuclear city, you change the language of the war. Today two countries put a finger on each other’s most dangerous place.

Scene 4. Souda Bay, Crete, Greece — the captain’s cabin of the U.S. aircraft carrier USS Gerald R. Ford (CVN-78), under repair

March 22, 2026 (Sun.) · afternoon, Greece time — War Day 23

Captain Marcus Webb read the post aboard a ship tied to a pier.

The sound of welding torches came up through the hull. They were tearing out the burned compartments. His ship was as far from the war as it could be. So he heard the war’s words in the quietest place.

On March 21 President Trump issued an ultimatum: if Iran did not open the Strait of Hormuz within 48 hours, the United States would strike Iran’s power plants. On March 22 U.S. and Israeli strikes hit two power plants in Bandar Abbas. On March 23, with half the deadline gone, President Trump announced he was delaying the strikes on power plants by five days because “productive” talks were under way. Iran denied there were any such talks, and Iran’s foreign ministry said there would be no negotiations before its goals were met.

Power plants. His eyes would not come off that word. A power plant is not a radar site. It is not a missile launcher. When a power plant goes dark, the first thing to stop is not an army. It is a ventilator. A water treatment plant. An incubator.

He called for the staff judge advocate. A young lieutenant commander came.

“Are power plants a lawful target?”

The lieutenant commander’s answer came after a long while.

“It depends on the situation, Captain. It’s a question of proportionality between military advantage and civilian harm. If the grid is supporting military command and control—”

“Is every power plant in a country supporting military command and control?”

The lieutenant commander did not answer. Webb did not blame him. No one on this ship had the rank to answer that question.

His ship could not sortie now. If that order came, it would not come to him. He was relieved, and he was ashamed of the relief. In the Gulf in 1988 the Vincennes had read an airliner as an enemy plane. The Navy has never washed that ship’s name clean. That his hands would stay clean was not because he was right. It was because a fire had started in a laundry room.

He took Sarah’s cable out of the drawer. The sixth interview request. In twenty-five years he had never once told a reporter what he thought. Soldiers do not speak about policy. That was discipline. But when this war was written down, his silence would be written down too. When the words came about striking power plants, what did the captain in that place say? Did he say nothing?

He wrote a reply. One line. Call me the day after tomorrow.

A soldier has the right to refuse an unlawful order. But nobody tells you in advance which orders are unlawful.

Scene 5. Alborz Mountains, Iran — north of Tehran, Iranian Air Defense Force command post

March 22, 2026 (Sun.) · morning, Tehran time — War Day 23

Colonel Darius Sepahvand had lived fifty-two years.

Thirty-one of them he had spent inside a uniform. He knew what this army was. He knew what it could do. On the twenty-third day of the war, for the first time, he thought about what this army must not do.

Natanz had been hit. That was expected. What he had not expected was the answer. Dimona. He was not a missile-forces man. That order did not cross his desk. But the launch warning came up on his screen too. He could calculate where the trajectory ended. When the calculation was done he looked at the screen a long time. Hoping he had read it wrong.

It was the nuclear city. If what they had aimed at was the facility next to it, and if it had been hit, then today the map of this region had changed. Depending on the wind—Jordan, Saudi Arabia, maybe the west of this country. As a boy he had spent a spring in a basement while Iraqi missiles fell on Tehran. That was a war of cities aiming at cities. This time there was a reactor next to the city.

He remembered something from long ago. A public lecture series at the University of Tehran. A philosophy lecture he had heard from the back row, out of uniform. When it was over he went up to argue with the professor. The professor’s name was Azad Moradi. The professor heard him out and then said quietly: Colonel, if you perform a conviction long enough, you forget you are performing. That is when it is most dangerous. He had turned away angry. That was more than ten years ago.

Now he knew what those words meant.

And there were Washington’s words. That it would hit power plants. Bluff or meant, he could not know. He knew one thing. When the power plants go dark, the first to die are not soldiers. They are hospital patients. Children. People like the daughter who carries water up to the old woman downstairs.

His last call with Narges had been eight days ago. Her question was still waiting for an answer. Father, who are you protecting right now?

Something inside him broke. There was no loud sound. The sound of a crack in a beam that had held for thirty-one years was so small only he could hear it. He was afraid to give it a name.

In war the guilt of obeying and the guilt of ordering often stand in the same place. For the first time he saw where that place was.

Scene 6. Tehran — basement machine room, Imam Khomeini Hospital

March 22, 2026 (Sun.) · 11 p.m. Tehran time — night of War Day 23

During the night shift Maryam went down to the basement. It was no place for a nurse.

There was one facilities engineer in the machine room. A man near sixty. He was standing in front of the fuel gauge of the emergency generator.

“How long will it last?” she asked.

“Run everything, thirty hours,” the engineer said. “Run only the ICU, the operating rooms, and the neonatal unit, four days. If more fuel comes in, longer. If it doesn’t, that’s it.”

It had been two weeks since the oil depots burned. The hospital stood in line behind the army and in front of the bakeries. Somebody’s signature decided the order.

Maryam thought about upstairs. Seven incubators in the neonatal unit. Eleven patients on ventilators. People waiting for dialysis. She knew those numbers by heart. She went around the wards counting them every day. In every hospital in this city somebody was counting the same numbers.

“They say the American president will hit the power plants,” the engineer said. “Says he’ll give two days.”

“I heard.”

“I wonder if the man knows what a power plant is. A power plant isn’t a light bulb. It’s the lungs of those eleven people up there.”

On the stairs up to the dormitory Maryam stopped for a moment. Nariman was asleep on the third floor. The child was afraid of the dark. She did not understand the war, but she knew that when the lights went out it got dark. Maryam touched the candle in the pocket of her gown. The candle from the haft-sin table. Half of it was left.

To the people who decide on war, a power plant is a dot on a map. In a hospital, a power plant is a date. Thirty hours. Or four days.

Scene 7. Tehran — Professor Azad Moradi’s apartment

March 23, 2026 (Mon.) · evening, Tehran time — War Day 24

Professor Azad Moradi read the same news three times.

Once on a foreign broadcast on the shortwave radio. That the American president said he was having productive conversations with Iran and was putting off the attack on the power plants for five days. Once on state television. That there were no such conversations, that it was enemy psychological warfare. And once from the mouth of his downstairs neighbor. “Professor, so does that mean the lights won’t go out?”

When he set the three side by side he saw something strange. One side says there are talks. The other says there are none. Both cannot be true. It is hard for both to be complete lies, too. There is no reason to invent talks that do not exist in order to delay five days. There is only one reason to deny talks that do exist so quickly. Because there are ears inside listening.

March 23 was a hard day for Tehran. The rights group HRANA recorded 379 airstrikes in 20 provinces that day and 38 civilians killed, with most of the strikes falling on the capital. That night, witnesses said, Basij patrols and checkpoints lined the streets across the country. On March 24 Iran named Mohammad Bagher Zolghadr secretary of the Supreme National Security Council to replace the dead Larijani.

He had lived seventy-two years. In the winter of 1979 too, two radios had described two different countries. The years had taught him one thing. When two countries speak so exactly opposite about the same event, somewhere between them a table is being set. Even if no one has sat down yet, somebody is carrying chairs.

“It isn’t over yet,” he said. He was talking to himself.

It bothered him that he had not been able to answer his neighbor. Would the lights go out? Philosophers are not taught how to answer questions like that. He took out six candles, set them in front of the door downstairs, and came back up. That was his answer.

Then he opened Rumi. There was not much an old philosopher could do in a war. He could read. That on a night when bombs were choosing a city, somebody was still reading. That was the sign that civilization was still breathing.

When truth and lies collide this plainly, somewhere between them a negotiating table is set. That is the grammar of diplomacy. Who will sit down first, nobody knows yet.

Scene 8. The Ford’s captain’s cabin at Souda Bay, Crete — a hotel room in Manama, Bahrain, on a secure line

March 24, 2026 (Tue.) · 10 a.m. Greece time / 11 a.m. in Bahrain — War Day 25

The call came on the hour.

“I’m not recording.” It was Sarah Callahan’s voice. The line was distant. “I’ve closed my notebook too.”

“Can a reporter do that?” Marcus Webb asked.

“Today I’m not calling as a reporter.”

Webb pulled up a chair and sat down. Through the porthole he could see the water of Souda Bay. It was calm. A hill with olive trees. Here the war came only as the sound of welding. From the other end of the line, now and then, another sound came. Something dull and far off. She did not explain it, and he did not ask.

“It’s been put off five days,” she said. “If that order comes again in five days, what will you do? If your ship is back by then.”

Before he answered Webb looked at the water a long time. Duty and conscience. When they point the same way it is easy. The moment they split, it becomes the oldest question a soldier has.

“I’m a soldier. I carry out lawful orders.”

“I’m asking whether power plants are a lawful target.”

He did not answer. On the other end of the line he could hear her breathing.

“My staff judge advocate said it depends on the situation,” he said finally. “That’s the right answer. I also know you can hide behind that answer. Ms. Callahan, let me tell you one thing I saw. When the fire broke out, the ventilators on my ship stopped for thirty seconds. There were two people in sick bay. Thirty seconds until the emergency power switched over. The doctor pushed air in by hand. I saw those thirty seconds.”

Silence.

“Turning off all the lights in a country is making those thirty seconds a few million times. With nobody there to push the air in by hand.”

“Can I use that?”

“No,” he said. Then he added, “Not yet.”

After the line went dead he held the receiver for a long time. For the first time in twenty-five years he had told someone outside the uniform what he thought. His hand shook a little. He did not mind the shaking now.

In war, silence is sometimes the most honest thing you can say. And the words “not yet” mean the silence is coming to an end.

At the same hour it was five in the afternoon in Seoul. Over the last three days Kang Yujin’s model had swung hard twice. Once at the ultimatum, once at the delay. Watching the output curve, she noticed something strange. The model reacted less to what the announcements said than to the gap between an announcement and its denial. The time between one side speaking and the other side denying it. The shorter that gap, the earlier the predicted end of the war. She had never put that variable in. Where had it come from?

Natanz and Dimona — Two Cities That Pointed at Each Other

Natanz, in Isfahan Province, is Iran’s largest uranium enrichment complex. The main enrichment halls are buried under earth and concrete. It went through the Stuxnet malware in 2010, sabotage in 2020 and 2021, and bombing in June 2025, and in this war it was hit on March 2 and March 21. The GBU-57 bunker buster the United States uses weighs about 13,600 kilograms; it was first dropped in combat on Fordow and Natanz in June 2025.

Dimona is a city in the Negev Desert in southern Israel. Nearby is the Shimon Peres Negev Nuclear Research Center, built in the late 1950s with French help. Israel has kept a policy of ambiguity, neither confirming nor denying that it has nuclear weapons. On March 21 an Iranian missile fell on a residential neighborhood of this city.

There are precedents for military attacks on operating nuclear facilities, such as Israel’s bombing of Iraq’s Osirak reactor in 1981 and of Syria’s al-Kibar site in 2007. But this was the first day two countries aimed at each other’s nuclear-related sites on the same day.

What Is Fact and What Is Fiction

The Natanz strike (March 21) — Real events

The United States hit the Natanz nuclear facility with bunker busters. Iran said the enrichment center had come under air attack, and the IAEA urged restraint to avoid the risk of a nuclear accident. The scene at the site in this episode is the author’s invention.

Dimona and Arad (March 21) — Real events

Iranian missiles collapsed a building in Dimona, wounding 64 to more than 78, and wounded 116 in Arad. Iran said it had aimed at the Negev nuclear research center. Galit Amir is a fictional character.

The power plant ultimatum — Real events / March 21–23

On March 21 President Trump said that if the strait was not opened within 48 hours he would strike Iran’s power plants, and on March 23 he delayed it five days, citing “productive” talks. Iran denied there were talks. On March 30 he would bring up power plants again, along with oil wells, Kharg Island, and desalination plants.

Fordow’s 5.2 seconds — Fictional

The Fordow signal and its change are the invention of this novel. There is no public information about Fordow’s operations during the 2026 war.

Civilian infrastructure and the law of war — Legal concept

Power facilities may be targeted only when they make an effective contribution to military action, and even then an attack is prohibited if the expected civilian harm is excessive in relation to the military advantage (the principles of distinction and proportionality). Whether aiming at all of a country’s power plants meets this standard was argued over throughout this war.

Mohammad Bagher Zolghadr — Real person / Iran

A hard-line figure from the Revolutionary Guards. On March 24 he was named secretary of the Supreme National Security Council to replace Larijani.

The Ford and Souda Bay — Real events · Fictional

After the fire the Ford received initial repairs and resupply at Souda Bay, Crete. The call between the captain and the reporter, and the “thirty seconds” in sick bay during the fire, are the author’s invention.

March 25. Five days’ grace.

The deadline closes in again. Washington says Iran is begging to negotiate, and Tehran says there are no negotiations. Between them a single sheet of paper passes from country to country.
