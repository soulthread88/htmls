The Adriatic Sea — bridge of the U.S. aircraft carrier USS Gerald R. Ford (CVN-78), about to enter port at Split, Croatia
March 28, 2026 (Sat.) · morning, local time — War Day 29
Scene 1 / 7

Captain Marcus Webb was driving his ship with his back to the war.

Northwest. The Adriatic. A shipyard in Croatia. Five days of repairs. A hundred thousand tons dragged a long wake, and the wake pointed back toward the war. Twenty-five years in the Navy. He had never sailed with his back to a war.

At the top of the morning message board was a new name.

On March 28 the Houthis in Yemen fired a ballistic missile toward Beersheba in southern Israel. It came right after a speech by the Houthi military spokesman. Hours later a Houthi cruise missile was intercepted, and a drone flying from Yemen was shot down near Eilat. Twenty-nine days into the war, the Houthis had come into it. The same day 2,500 troops of the U.S. 31st Marine Expeditionary Unit arrived in the Middle East, and U.S. Central Command said it had struck more than 11,000 targets inside Iran since the war began.

The Houthis. In early March he had been in the Red Sea with this ship. The coast of Yemen to the south had been quiet then. It was so quiet he had asked the officer of the deck about it twice. Now he knew. They had been waiting. What they had been waiting for, he did not know. All he was sure of was that the side that had waited twenty-nine days had moved. To them, this war had only now become a war that would go long.

Eleven thousand. He looked at the number. Nearly four hundred a day. He knew by heart how many of them his own aircraft had taken. Thirteen days’ worth. After that the fire in the laundry room had ended his war.

Eleven thousand destroyed. The strait was closed. Eleven thousand destroyed. A new enemy had come in. Eleven thousand destroyed. Twenty-five hundred more Marines had come. There is no connection between the number of targets and the end of a war. He had learned that as a junior officer in another war. That war had put out numbers every day too. So had the jungle war half a century ago, the old hands said.

The reporter in Bahrain had not yet put out those six pages. He did not push. He had handed over the words. The words he had handed over were not his anymore.

When a war goes long, the map gets wider. At first it was a map of two countries. Now one sheet runs from the Red Sea to the Adriatic.

Langley, Virginia, United States — CIA Iran Operations Center, branch chief’s office
March 28, 2026 (Sat.) · 7:30 a.m. EDT — War Day 29
Scene 2 / 7

The branch chief read the cable three times and took off his glasses.

“Start with the possibility it’s bait.”

Leila Shirazi had spent the night getting that answer ready.

“Three possibilities. One, bait to make us hit Fordow. If we hit it, they can declare something, using the attack on a nuclear site as a pretext. Two, bait to confirm what sources we have. They throw out the date March 1 and watch how we react. Three, it’s real.”

“You think it’s three.”

“Bait is made of what the other side wants to hear. Nobody in this building wants to hear that Fordow is alive. And the sentence. ‘I do not know why.’ Counterintelligence never writes I don’t know. Operational documents are written in the voice of someone who knows everything.”

The branch chief looked out the window. On a Saturday morning the parking lot was half full. A building that had gone twenty-nine days without a weekend. In 1953 too this agency had watched Tehran without weekends. Then too the papers had moved first.

On March 28 there were reports that Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian had clashed with Revolutionary Guards commander Ahmad Vahidi over how the war was being conducted. According to the reports, the president warned that without a ceasefire Iran’s economy could collapse within three or four weeks, criticized the Guards’ attacks on neighboring countries, and demanded that executive authority be returned to the civilian government.

“Your assessment that Tehran is split got stronger with this morning’s reports,” the branch chief said. “But we don’t know which side of the split sent this. The side that wants to talk, or the side that wants to shoot.”

“It’s a soldier’s writing. Air defense. Someone who saw the defense order for the Fordow zone with his own eyes.”

“Then he’s on the shooting side.”

“He’s someone on the shooting side who wants to talk,” Leila said. “A man like that is in the most dangerous seat there is.”

The branch chief said nothing for a long while. Then he took a form out of the drawer. Request for Approval to Contact Unverified Source. The top box was empty. At this agency a decision always started in the empty box of a form.

“He said he’d open again in forty-eight hours. Sunday evening, seven forty-four, our time. We send one line then. Just one. No promises, no questions, no word that shows what we know.”

“What line?”

“You write it. In Persian. If he’s a soldier, in words a soldier understands.”

Leila took the form. In the code name box she wrote: ZARRIN. Once she wrote it she understood. It had become one person’s name. Until yesterday it had been a word. From today it was something she had to protect.

A source is not information. A source is a person. From the moment you write a name in the box of a form, that person’s life becomes part of the paperwork. Paperwork can protect that life. It can also sell it.

Tehran — roof of a student dormitory near Sharif University of Technology
March 28, 2026 (Sat.) · 5 p.m. Tehran time — War Day 29
Scene 3 / 7

Soraya watched the smoke to the east.

It was the place hit before dawn. The University of Science and Technology. Her cousin went there. Her cousin was all right. The campus had been empty for a month. What was hit was buildings. Labs. Lecture halls. The room where her cousin had spent three years learning to solder.

There were six of them on the roof. Before the war thirty people used to come up to this roof. Some went home, some went to the border, some just did not come anymore. The six who were left sat around one radio.

The radio read a statement from the Revolutionary Guards. American universities in the region would become targets.

“Universities for universities,” said a fourth-year mechanical engineering student. His face was not smiling. “An eye for an eye, and now a blackboard for a blackboard.”

“The kids at those schools are kids like us,” Soraya said. “In Doha or wherever. Worrying about exams, making their parents worry.”

“Their universities didn’t get hit.”

“Not yet.”

After she said it she looked back toward her own school. Four blocks west. The main gate of Sharif. For a month she had not gone through that gate. The gate was shut. The guard still sat there. When he saw her he raised his hand. It’ll open soon, miss. A month of the same words. In 1980 too the gates of this country’s universities were shut. Then it was not bombs that shut them but the revolution. Then too the guards said it would open soon.

Why are universities hit? She rolled the question around for a long time on the roof. The other side said weapons came out of the labs. It was probably not all a lie. One of her advisers never would say what he was researching. But in the same building she had learned differential equations. Both things were in one building. The bombs could not tell them apart.

The internet had been shut for a month. Only people with permission used it. So they went back to the old ways. Copied lecture notes by hand. Six of them passed one person’s textbook around. The fourth-year mechanical engineering student said: if we forget where we were in the course by the time school opens, that’s them winning. He did not say who he meant. The sky, or the people reading the statements. Both.

A university is not a building. It is one person handing what he knows to another. That can be done on a roof too.

Alborz Mountains, Iran — outside the air defense command post, in front of a vehicle shelter
March 28, 2026 (Sat.) · 9:20 p.m. Tehran time — War Day 29
Scene 4 / 7

It was a number he did not know. He answered on the first ring.

“Baba.”

Colonel Darius Sepahvand put his back against the concrete wall of the shelter. The strength went out of his legs. A voice after fourteen days.

“Where are you?”

“Karaj. A friend’s house. It’s my friend’s phone. Mine broke the first week.” Narges’s voice was lower than he remembered. “Baba, I can’t talk long. I’m leaving at dawn tomorrow.”

“Where to?”

“Van. Turkey. By car with two friends. Through Khoy to the Razi border. A coworker’s in Istanbul. He says he’ll look for a job for me.”

He could not answer. In his head he drew the road. Karaj to Tabriz. Tabriz to Khoy. Khoy to the border. Fifteen hours. He knew better than anyone what had fallen on that road. For twenty-nine days he had watched the sky over it on a screen.

“Go by day,” he said. “At night don’t stop and don’t drive. Don’t stay long near bridges or gas stations. Don’t follow behind military trucks.”

“I know.”

“At the border they’ll ask your name. When you say the family name—”

“I won’t give them your name,” she said. It was not an accusation. It was a fact. “Baba. Come with us.”

The wind blew. Below the mountain, half of Tehran was dark.

“I have work to do here.”

“Last time I asked you. Who you’re protecting. You didn’t answer.”

“I think I know now,” he said. “That’s why I can’t go.”

The other end of the line was quiet. His daughter had understood something. What she understood, he did not ask. Not asking was how he protected her.

“When you get to Van, send one line. Not to this number. To the number your mother used.”

“You still keep that number alive?”

“Eight years now I’ve paid the bill.”

Narges laughed. A laugh with crying in it. Then the line cut off. Whether she hung up or the line dropped, he could not tell.

He stood there holding the phone. With this, the things he had left in this country as hostages were one fewer. He was surprised the thought came into his head. He had never thought of his daughter as a hostage. But half the reason he had kept his mouth shut for thirty-one years was that she was inside this country. Power took hold of your family before it took hold of a gun. Every soldier in this country knew it.

The one who leaves is not running away. The one who stays is not a fool. There is no higher or lower between them. There is only a choice. And that choice is not only one’s own.

Tehran — Imam Khomeini Hospital, makeshift classroom in the basement shelter
March 29, 2026 (Sun.) · 9:30 a.m. Tehran time — War Day 30
Scene 5 / 7

It was the thirtieth day.

Professor Azad Moradi did not tell the children that number. The children did not count days. The children counted meals. This morning it was bread and two dates.

The old man’s radio next to him talked about Islamabad.

On March 29 Pakistan hosted the foreign ministers of Turkey, Egypt, and Saudi Arabia in Islamabad to discuss ways to end the war. Pakistan’s foreign minister, Ishaq Dar, said the four countries had discussed how to bring the war to an early and permanent end, and that Pakistan was ready to host meaningful talks between the United States and Iran within days. Reuters reported that the first discussions centered on how to reopen passage through the Strait of Hormuz. The same day the speaker of Iran’s parliament, Mohammad Bagher Qalibaf, rejected negotiations with the United States and said Iran could not be made to submit.

Nariman opened the atlas. The child was used to finding the names from the news on the map now. Pakistan. Turkey. Egypt. Saudi Arabia. Her fingers touched four places. In the middle of the four fingers was Iran.

“Why are those countries getting together?”

“To stop the war.”

“Can they stop it?”

This time Azad decided to answer. For four days he had not been able to answer. Four days of losing to a child was too long for a philosopher.

“The people fighting have a hard time being first to say let’s stop. If you say it first, it feels like you lost. So someone who isn’t fighting stands in the middle and says it for them. Carries this side’s words to that side, and that side’s words to this side.”

“That happened in my class. When Amir and Hossein had a fight, I was in the middle carrying what they said.”

“And did it stop?”

“It took three days. But in the middle I told a lie once. Hossein didn’t say sorry, but I said he did.”

Azad laughed. For the first time in thirty days he laughed out loud. The old man next to him turned to look.

“That’s not a lie, Nariman. That’s diplomacy.”

Late in the morning Maryam came down. After thirty-two hours. She held her daughter and stayed like that a long time. Then she sat next to Azad and spoke low. The power had gone out twice last night. The operating rooms had held on with the generator. There were six days of generator fuel left.

On March 29 the power was cut in parts of Tehran and Alborz Province, including Karaj. Debris had damaged power equipment. The same day explosions were reported in several places in Tehran, including Evin, and in one village an airstrike killed six people and brought down five houses.

Azad nodded. Evin. The neighborhood where his home was. His study. Half a century of books. He did not ask. Maryam did not say. Books were books. Right now there was a child next to him.

The words let’s stop come last out of the mouths of people who are fighting. That is why the world needs people who stand in the middle.

West Azerbaijan Province, Iran — Razi border checkpoint, across from Kapıköy on the Turkish side
March 29, 2026 (Sun.) · 4:40 p.m. Tehran time — War Day 30
Scene 6 / 7

The mountains squeezed the road from both sides.

Narges had been in the back seat for close to twelve hours. They left Karaj at five in the morning. Qazvin. Zanjan. Tabriz. One checkpoint outside Tabriz. Two hours in Khoy to get gas. The line at the gas station ran to the edge of town. As her father had said, they did not stop near bridges.

Iran’s airspace had been closed since the war began. The Kapıköy border crossing in Van Province in eastern Turkey had become one of the few ways Iranians could get out into the world. The Guardian reported that between March 3 and 30 about 64,000 Iranians entered Turkey and more than 48,000 Iranians went back into Iran. Many people were not leaving. They were going back and forth.

The line was not long. That was strange. A month into the war. The middle-aged woman ahead of her explained. People with money to get out had gotten out the first week, and people without it couldn’t. The ones coming out now were in-between people like them. The woman said she was buying medicine in Van and going back. Her mother’s blood pressure pills. There were none in Tehran. Even at the border, the war sorted people by class.

Going back. Narges turned the words over. The woman said it as if it were obvious. Because her home was there.

The official at the window was young. He asked as he turned the pages of her passport.

“Sepahvand. Father’s occupation.”

She took one beat. She had an answer she had been getting ready for twelve hours.

“Civil servant. Retired.”

The official looked at his screen. He looked a long time. She tried to keep her breathing even. Twice in the fall of 2022 she had sat in front of a screen like this. That time it was about her hair. That time it was her father’s rank that got her out. Today the same rank could hold her. The same rank had once been a door, and once could be bars.

The stamp came down.

Two hundred meters from the Iranian gate to the Turkish gate. She walked with one bag on her shoulder. A laptop. Three changes of clothes. One photo of her mother. She tried not to look back. She looked back. Mountains. A flag. People in line. The woman going for medicine waved.

As she went through the Turkish gate her phone went off all at once. A month of notifications. Hundreds. She did not open one. She dialed one number. Her mother’s, who had died eight years ago. A text, one line.

Arrived.

She thought a little, then sent one more line.

Whatever it is you’re protecting, Baba, come when you’re done protecting it.

A border is not a line drawn on the ground. It is the place where a person looks back.

Langley, Virginia, United States — CIA Iran Operations Center / signals room, Alborz air defense command post, Iran
March 29, 2026 (Sun.) · 7:44 p.m. EDT / 3:14 a.m. March 30 in Tehran — War Day 30
Scene 7 / 7 — Final

Leila Shirazi rewrote one line thirty times.

No promises could go in. No questions. No word that showed what they knew. Keep all those rules and almost nothing was left. In the end she picked a soldier’s words. Her grandfather had been an artillery sergeant in the Shah’s army. He had gone through the year the king left and the army changed in that uniform. There was one thing he had taught his granddaughter, as a game. What a sentry says to someone coming at him in the dark.

Halt. Who goes there? Show your light.

At seven forty-four in the evening, Islamabad station put that one line on the Quetta line.

On the other side of the mountain Darius Sepahvand was sitting at the console. Tonight’s duty officer was the same second lieutenant. The fuel in the generator room would be down to three days. He had told him to go count it.

Letters came up on the screen. He read them and stopped breathing. It was Persian. Not a translation machine’s Persian. The old army’s words. The challenge his father’s generation used. The person sitting on the other end of the line had learned this country’s language on a grandmother’s knee. Someone who had left the country was calling to him in the country’s old words.

Show your light.

It meant prove it. He had expected that. For two days he had thought about what to show. Documents would not do. Documents can be forged, and real documents become a road to finding him. Of all he had, there was one thing the other side could see with its own eyes, that would hurt no one, and that in this country only he could do.

Air defense uses decoys. To protect the real radars, they set transmitters on empty ridgelines that put out fake emissions. No people. Only a generator and an antenna and a timer. Deciding when to turn those decoys on and off was this command post’s job. The man who signed that schedule was him.

He typed.

Coordinates 35 degrees 41 minutes north, 51 degrees 52 minutes east. Today at 06:00 exactly, the search radar at this position comes on. It goes off after one hundred twenty-seven seconds. For six hours before and after, this position is silent. That is me.

One hundred twenty-seven. The number he chose. The last three digits of his brother’s service number. The number of a brother who never came back from the Iran-Iraq War went up into the sky of another war.

SENT 03:19:42.

Those three lines came up on the screen at Langley at four minutes past eight. Leila put the coordinates on the map. East of Tehran, an empty ridgeline on the road to Damavand. She picked up the phone. Sam Farrell. Senior satellite analyst. It was Sunday night.

“Sam. I need an electronic intelligence collection window two and a half hours from now. These coordinates. Three minutes, starting six o’clock sharp Tehran time.”

“What are we looking for?”

“A light,” she said. “Somebody says he’ll show us his light.”

There is only one way to trust each other in the dark. One side shows where it is first. The side that shows itself always stakes more.

Background Analysis
The Thirtieth Day — The War Widens, the Table Draws Near

On March 28 and 29 the war moved in two directions. In one, it widened. The Houthis in Yemen fired missiles at Israel for the first time. Iran hit Salalah port in southern Oman, Kuwait International Airport, and the aluminum smelter in Abu Dhabi, and claimed to have targeted a Ukrainian counter-drone equipment warehouse in Dubai. In Iraq’s Kurdistan Region, the homes of regional president Nechirvan Barzani and Masoud Barzani were attacked by drones. 2,500 U.S. Marines arrived, and Britain sent the landing ship RFA Lyme Bay, carrying mine-hunting drones, to Hormuz.

In the other, the table drew near. Pakistan gathered the foreign ministers of Turkey, Egypt, and Saudi Arabia in Islamabad and said it would host talks between the United States and Iran. According to Reuters, these countries passed to Washington ideas such as a Suez Canal–style toll system and a consortium to manage the flow of oil through the strait. Iran allowed twenty more Pakistani-flagged ships through. Inside Tehran there were reports that the president had faced off with the Revolutionary Guards commander, and the speaker of parliament said he rejected negotiations.

A widening war and a table drawing near are not a contradiction. When the end of a war starts to come into view, both sides try to add to what they hold before they sit down at the table. That is why the days just before a ceasefire are the most dangerous. The actual ceasefire would be announced nine days later, on April 7.

At six o’clock sharp Tehran time, on an empty ridgeline on the road to Damavand, a radar comes on. One hundred twenty-seven seconds. The same day Washington says it is talking with a new and more reasonable regime, and writes that without a deal it will wipe out the power plants, the oil wells, and the desalination plants. B-52s enter Iranian skies for the first time. And in the signals room in the Alborz, the man who reads the counter sees that the number has gone up by two.
