Episode 10, “Blood of Allies”

Scene 1. If the IRGC navy commander is gone, does the Strait of Hormuz open?

Bahrain — Mina Salman Port, the coalition liaison cell at the headquarters of the U.S. Fifth Fleet

March 26, 2026 (Thu.) · 10:20 a.m. Bahrain time — War Day 27

One line on the status board changed.

Lieutenant Kim Junho had been watching that line for twenty-seven days. IRGCN CDR — TANGSIRI, A. The Revolutionary Guards navy commander. The man who sorted the ships that could pass through the strait from the ships that would be hit. This morning the box next to that name said KIA presumed.

On March 26 the Israeli military said Revolutionary Guards navy commander Alireza Tangsiri had been killed in an airstrike in Bandar Abbas, and the Revolutionary Guards confirmed it. The same day Iranian media reported that a residential area of Shiraz had been hit and two teenagers killed. In Abu Dhabi two civilians were killed by debris from an intercepted Iranian missile. Lebanon’s Health Ministry said the death toll since Israel’s invasion had risen to 1,116, and in southern Lebanon two Golani Brigade soldiers were killed fighting Hezbollah.

At the next desk Lieutenant Commander Mark Henderson gave a short whistle.

“The man holding the strait’s key ring is gone. Isn’t that good news for your ships?”

Kim Junho did not answer right away. For twenty-seven days he had been working this question out his own way. If the man stopping the ships disappears, do the ships get out? He thought of the Vincennes on this sea in 1988. The one who is frightened shoots first.

“I don’t know, sir,” he said. “He was the man who picked which ships got through. Ships like India’s, from countries he could talk to. When somebody called, he was the one who picked up. Now who picks up that phone?”

“His replacement, I guess.”

“It’ll take days just to name a replacement. And he’ll be a man who saw how the last one died. For a man like that to pick up the phone and say go ahead—is that going to be easy or hard?”

Henderson took a sip of coffee. He knew this young Korean officer had changed in three weeks. The man whose hands had shaken at the sirens now answered questions with numbers.

On the morning call one captain had said he had five days of water left. The desalinator was broken and the part was in Dubai. Dubai was a hundred and twenty nautical miles away. The name of the man who could have let him across those hundred and twenty miles had just been erased from the status board. All the ships inside the strait were tied to that one line.

It is easy to believe that if you kill the gatekeeper the gate will open. But the only one who knew where the key was hanging was the gatekeeper.

Scene 2. Why did the CIA analyst warn about the word “begging”?

Langley, Virginia — CIA Iran Operations Center

March 26, 2026 (Thu.) · 4:30 a.m. EDT / noon in Tehran — War Day 27

Leila Shirazi finished her analysis of the five conditions and called her aunt.

Four thirty in the morning. Noon in Tehran. The line went through on the third try.

“Leila jan.”

“Auntie. Are you all right?”

“I’m all right. The power came on this morning. I’m cooking rice. Last night the sounds were close, but it wasn’t our street.”

Leila tried to choose her words and could not. The cables she had written. The numbers in the annex. The minutes from three months ago. What piece of this war they had made, she could not tell her aunt. In 1953 too, this agency’s papers had overturned the government in Tehran. The old people of Tehran had not forgotten that year. Knowing she could not say it, she called anyway.

“Auntie. I’m sorry.”

“For what?”

Leila did not answer. Her aunt asked again. Sorry for what?

“Just because.”

Her aunt was quiet for a moment. A pot lid rattled.

“Just because isn’t just because, Leila.”

Leila hung up first. She could feel her aunt was about to say something more. It was not that she did not want to hear it. If she heard any more she would come apart.

When she got back to her desk there was a new post up.

On March 26 President Trump claimed Iran’s leaders were “begging” for a peace deal. He said the Iranian government’s statements denying it were false, and suggested Iran’s negotiators could not come forward publicly because they were afraid of their own people. The day before, Iran had rejected the American fifteen points and put forward five conditions.

Begging. She tried putting the word into Persian. How it would be read on Tehran’s broadcasts. How it would sound in the ears of the people who wrote the five conditions. If someone had been walking toward the table, he would stop in front of that word. The side that is told it is begging fires something to show it is not begging.

She wrote two readings into the cable.

(A) Tehran has given up on diplomacy, and the five conditions were stalling. (B) Inside Tehran, those who want to talk and those who want to fire are split. Yesterday’s paper and today’s missiles did not come from the same hand. This analyst assesses (B). Basis: signs that the selective-passage channel is being maintained even after the navy commander’s removal. Warning: insulting public language weakens the side in (B) that wants to talk.

As she wrote the last sentence she knew who it was aimed at. Analysts do not grade a president’s words. Somewhere up the chain of approval someone might strike that line. She did not strike it. The sentences she did not delete were adding up.

To believe in diplomacy in the middle of a war is to stand in front of a fire holding a sheet of paper. The paper can burn at any moment. But someone has to hold it for the fire to turn another way.

Scene 3. What are the scatterable mines at Kafari waiting for?

Fars Province, Iran — Kafari, a village south of Shiraz

March 26, 2026 (Thu.) · early morning, Tehran time — War Day 27

They came down in the night.

Hasan was sixty-three. All his life he had grown pomegranates and wheat in this village. After the dawn prayer, on his way out to the fields, he saw something he had never seen before. One by the road. One by the ditch. One under the fig tree. Like flat cans. Grass-colored, with something like a fin on one side.

In the Iran-Iraq War he had been on the southern front. He had learned one thing there. Anything that falls from the sky and does not go off has not finished its work. He stopped where he was. And he shouted. Don’t come out. Don’t touch anything. The shout he had heard in the trenches now came out of his own throat.

The investigative group Bellingcat said that before dawn on March 26 U.S. forces appeared to have scattered antitank mines over the village of Kafari near Shiraz. Experts identified the objects in the photos as BLU-91/B mines from the GATOR system. Among the parties to this war only the United States had this system, and it was the first combat use of scatterable antitank mines by the United States since the 1991 Gulf War. About two kilometers from where the mines fell was a missile base south of Shiraz. A reporter who visited the site said he saw the place where a thirty-one-year-old father had been killed after picking up a mine that had fallen next to his car. These mines go off easily when moved.

Not everyone heard his shout. There was a dull sound at the edge of the village. Hasan did not run toward it. He had learned in that war, too, that you do not run. He walked looking at his feet. One step. Another step. Thirty meters took five minutes.

A car was standing there. The driver’s door was open. It was the car of the young father next door. On the back seat was a child’s school bag. The schools were closed, but the bag was in the car. Hasan could not take his eyes off that bag.

There were no missiles in this village. The missiles were over that hill. Somebody on a map had drawn a line between the hill and the highway. To keep the trucks from getting through, they decided to scatter these things along that line. That there was a pomegranate orchard on that line, and a child’s way to school, was not on the map.

Hasan stood in the middle of the road until the sun came up. To turn back anyone who came out. All morning he stood there like a flag. That was all the mine clearing a sixty-three-year-old farmer could do. Behind him the whole village held its breath.

A bomb kills people the day it falls. A mine waits where it is after the war is over. Until the pomegranates ripen. Until the child goes back to school.

Scene 4. What did the Iranian colonel decide after reading the order to recruit twelve-year-olds?

Alborz Mountains, Iran — north of Tehran, Iranian Air Defense Force command post

March 26, 2026 (Thu.) · afternoon, Tehran time — War Day 27

Colonel Darius Sepahvand had not slept at all last night.

In the morning there was a one-line text on his phone. From a number he did not know. Baba, I’m okay. That was all. Where she was, who she was with, whether she had water. Nothing. He read the one line a hundred times. After a hundred times it was still one line.

And at noon an order came down.

On March 26 Rahim Nadali of the Revolutionary Guards announced a program called “For Iran.” It would allow recruitment from the age of twelve for security-related duties with the Revolutionary Guards. Earlier, among those killed in a drone attack on a Tehran checkpoint on March 11, there had been an eleven-year-old boy, and Amnesty International called the mobilization of children a war crime.

Twelve.

He put the order down on the desk. It was not his hand that was shaking. It was the paper. When Narges was twelve he used to drive her to school. Before she got out of the car she would try on her father’s uniform cap. The cap was big. It came down over her eyes. She laughed. What he had spent thirty-one years trying to protect was not a country that put that cap on a child and stood her at a checkpoint.

In 1987 his older brother died. On the southern front. His brother was seventeen. A volunteer. He went with a plastic key around his neck. The key to paradise, they said. Thirteen-year-old Darius watched their mother get the key back and put it in a drawer. Eight years later, at twenty-one, he put on the uniform. The uniform his brother should have worn. Swearing he would build a country where no seventeen-year-old ever died like that again. In that war his brother was not the only boy with a key like that around his neck.

For thirty-one years he had never once taken out that vow and looked at it. Today he took it out for the first time. When he looked, the vow had died long ago. Without his knowing.

Darius made his decision.

How he would carry it out he did not know yet. But the decision was made. Thirty-one years were over. He had two things. A head that knew this command post’s communications network. And the memory of a cable that had passed across the command network the night Natanz was hit. The coordinates of the Fordow air-defense zone. The order to protect that mountain. Three lines saying what was spinning under it, and how much.

Who to send those three lines to, he did not know. But he knew that the people who could end this war did not know those three lines. The orders said to protect that mountain. The country he had meant to protect was outside it.

Getting out of an organization takes longer than getting in. When you go in, the door is open. When you go out, first you have to find where the door is.

Scene 5. Why couldn’t the old professor answer the child who said, “If it’s complicated, then just don’t do it”?

Tehran — Imam Khomeini Hospital, makeshift classroom in the basement shelter

March 26, 2026 (Thu.) · 6:10 p.m. Tehran time — War Day 27

Nariman brought a map.

It was the atlas she had used at school. One of the few things saved from the house that fell. The cover was swollen with water. The child opened the map of the Middle East and put her finger on a place across the sea.

“Here. Abu Dhabi. They said two people died here today. They got hit by missile pieces.”

Professor Azad Moradi picked up his magnifying glass. A city across the Gulf. He had been there once when he was young. A conference. Back then it was nothing but sand and a small harbor.

“Who shot that missile?”

Azad stopped for a moment.

“Iran shot it. That’s what the news said.”

“We did? Why did we?”

We. To a seven-year-old the country was we. What the country did, we did. Azad stayed a long time in front of that word. For seventy-two years he had used it carefully. He had seen the “we” they shouted together in the square in 1979, and who it had subtracted within a few years. He knew too well who this country’s “we” put in and who it left out.

“Because there are American soldiers there. America hits us, so we hit the places where American soldiers are.”

“But the people who died weren’t American soldiers.”

“……No.”

“Then why?”

“In a war the reasons are complicated, Nariman.”

“If it’s complicated, then just don’t do it.”

Azad could not answer. Yesterday it had been the same. It was not that the child was especially clever. Grown-up logic had too many things in it that could not be explained to a child.

He looked up at the small ventilation window of the basement. Evening light came in between the bars. Somewhere up there, in the Alborz, there would still be snow. Spring was coming. Even in a war. That was the logic of nature. Simpler than the logic of people, and older.

The child said, “If it’s complicated, then just don’t do it.” For the second time a philosopher of seventy-two could not answer. A seven-year-old’s logic beat the grown-ups’ war.

Scene 6. Why is the blood of allies never written into a treaty?

The Ionian Sea — the captain’s cabin of the U.S. aircraft carrier USS Gerald R. Ford (CVN-78), bound for Croatia after leaving Souda Bay, Crete

March 27, 2026 (Fri.) · afternoon, local time — War Day 28

Captain Marcus Webb read the casualty report twice.

On March 27 Iranian missiles and drones struck Prince Sultan Air Base in Saudi Arabia. Fifteen U.S. troops were wounded, five of them critically. Several aerial refueling tankers and one E-3 airborne early warning and control aircraft were damaged. In an earlier attack that same week, fourteen U.S. troops were also reported wounded. The same day Kuwait’s Shuwaikh and Mubarak Al-Kabeer ports were hit by drones and missiles, and the UAE intercepted six ballistic missiles and nine drones. Iranian cluster munitions fell on six sites in central Israel, and one person was killed in Ramat Gan.

Fifteen. Five critical. He knew that base. Desert south of Riyadh. The tankers that fueled his aircraft flew from there. For thirteen days those people had passed fuel to his pilots in the sky. He did not know their faces. He knew their voices on the radio. A boom operator with a Texas accent. When the refueling was done he always said the same thing. Fly safe, Navy.

Whether that man was among the fifteen, he had no way to know.

His ship had left the pier at Souda Bay and was heading for the Adriatic. A shipyard in Croatia. Second round of repairs. Five days, they said. The war was in its twenty-eighth day, and he had been outside it for fifteen. His forty-five hundred sailors were alive. The fifteen at Prince Sultan were bleeding. What made the difference was not his command. It was a dryer exhaust duct.

Allies. He chewed on the word. At a base in Saudi Arabia American soldiers had bled. In Abu Dhabi a worker—Filipino or Indian, he could not tell—had been killed by debris. In Lebanon Israeli soldiers had died. Kuwait’s ports had burned. Two countries started this war, and the blood was running in ten. After the Khobar Towers bombing in 1996, the Air Force moved its people deep into that desert. They believed far away meant safe. That was what an alliance came down to. Lending your land to someone else’s war, and paying the rent in your own people’s blood.

He picked up the phone. The Bahrain number. It rang.

“This is Webb,” he said. “Last time I said ‘not yet.’ I’m going to move that up some. Leave out the name—‘a senior U.S. Navy officer.’ Are you ready to take this down?”

On the other end he heard Sarah Callahan open her notebook.

The blood of allies is not written in the documents. The treaties write the obligations, and the blood soaks into a desert runway.

Scene 7. Where do betrayal and loyalty part ways?

Alborz Mountains, Iran — Air Defense Force command post, communications room

March 27, 2026 (Fri.) · 3 a.m. Tehran time — before dawn, War Day 28

There was only one duty officer in the signals room.

Captain Farhan Moradi. Thirty-seven. Signals officer for this command post. Darius Sepahvand had had him for six years. A man of few words, quick hands, who never forgot a frequency once he heard it. Sometimes he talked about his uncle, a philosophy professor. His uncle hated soldiers, so they argued every holiday.

“Colonel, at this hour.”

“I can’t sleep,” Darius said. “Let’s have some tea.”

He pulled over a chair and sat down. The light from the console turned both their faces blue. He knew how far this console reached. The inside of the military network. And, very rarely, the outside. There was one line they had used before the war for joint anti-smuggling work on the Pakistani border. A line that ran through a relay station in Quetta to Islamabad. For twenty-eight days no one had used it. No one had cut it, either.

“Farhan,” he said. “How is your uncle these days?”

“He tells the children old stories in a hospital basement, they say.” Farhan laughed. “My mother says at seventy-two he’s finally doing something useful.”

Darius did not laugh. He looked into his tea glass.

“Did you see the order that came down today? Twelve.”

Farhan’s laugh stopped. He did not answer. He answered by not answering.

Darius said nothing more. Tonight this was as far as it went. He knew where the door was, and he knew who was sitting in front of it. Would he draw this young officer in? Or wait for a night when he was away from his post? That was for tomorrow. Draw in one man and that man’s whole family stands in front of the same door.

He finished his tea and stood up. In the doorway Farhan spoke.

“Colonel. My uncle used to say something. What’s frightening about soldiers isn’t that they have guns. It’s that they hand their thinking over to someone else. I hated that.” He turned his eyes back to the console. “These days I’m not so sure.”

Betrayal and loyalty begin with the same sentence. “I am trying to protect this country.” It is the next sentence where they part.

At the same hour it was eight thirty in the morning in Seoul. For the first time Kang Yujin brought it up with her lab director, Park Sejun. A model reacting to a variable she had not put in. Park Sejun was seven years her senior. He was the only person at this institute she showed her code to. He looked at the screen a long time and said, “Interesting. Don’t delete the logs.” Then he added, “Who have you told?” Nobody, she said. He nodded. Only much later would she remember that the nod had gone on a little long. Behind that long nod, what was already moving?

How did the CIA read Iran’s rejection? — Two Readings

Reading (A): Tehran has given up on diplomacy and the five conditions are stalling. Reading (B): those who want to talk and those who want to fire are separate, with different decision-makers.

The fictional analyst Leila Shirazi assesses (B). Her basis: signs that the selective-passage channel persists even after the navy commander’s death; the president’s March 6–7 apology coexisting with continued attacks; and a new Supreme National Security Council secretary appointed after Larijani’s death.

Her warning: insulting public language risks weakening the negotiation-minded faction in (B). Keep the Islamabad channel. This assessment is a character’s view.

Why does the blood flow on other people’s land? — The Geography of This War

There are three parties to this war. The United States, Israel, Iran. But in twenty-eight days missiles and drones have fallen on more than ten countries. Iran has hit bases, ports, airports, and refineries in the Gulf states where U.S. forces are stationed. Bahrain, Kuwait, Qatar, the UAE, Saudi Arabia, Oman, Jordan, Iraq. On March 11 the Security Council adopted Resolution 2817 condemning Iran’s attacks on its Gulf neighbors and Jordan, 13 in favor, 2 abstaining (China and Russia).

The European allies bled too. In mid-March a drone attack hit the peshmerga base at Makhmur in Iraq, wounding six French soldiers, and Arnaud Frion of the 7th Mountain Infantry Battalion was killed. Off Cyprus Greek fighters shot down an Iranian drone, and there were NATO interceptions in the skies over Turkey. But the collective defense of Article 5 of the North Atlantic Treaty applies only to attacks in the area defined by Article 6—Europe, North America, and so on. Soldiers wounded at bases in the Middle East were outside that article.

For the countries of the Gulf this was not a war they chose. On March 6 and 7 Iran’s president apologized to the neighboring countries on state television. After that the missiles still fell on the same countries. Apology and attack crossed paths in the same month. In this war the words ally and neighbor were paying the highest price.

What Is Fact and What Is Fiction

Alireza Tangsiri — Real person / Iran

Revolutionary Guards navy commander from 2018. He had directed the closure of the Strait of Hormuz. The Israeli military said he was killed in an airstrike in Bandar Abbas on March 26, and the Revolutionary Guards confirmed it.

The Kafari mines — Real events / March 26

Bellingcat assessed that U.S. forces appeared to have scattered BLU-91/B antitank mines from the GATOR scatterable mine system over the village of Kafari near Shiraz. Iranian state television, the Tasnim news agency, and a Canadian reporter who visited the site posted photos and video. The farmer Hasan is the author’s invention; about the resident who died, only reported facts are given.

“For Iran” — Real events / March 26

A program announced by Revolutionary Guards official Rahim Nadali allowing recruitment for security-related duties from the age of twelve.

Prince Sultan Air Base — Real events / March 27

A base south of Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, where U.S. Air Force tankers and early warning aircraft were stationed. The March 27 attack wounded 15 U.S. troops. On March 13 the Wall Street Journal had reported that five tankers were damaged in an attack earlier that week.

“Begging” — Real statement / March 26

President Trump claimed Iran’s leaders were “begging” for a peace deal. Leila’s assessment is a character’s view.

The key to paradise — History / Iran-Iraq War

The story that Iranian boy volunteers in the 1980–88 war went to the front with plastic keys around their necks is widely told, but how much of it actually happened is disputed among researchers. Darius’s brother is a fictional character.

Farhan Moradi — Fictional character

A 37-year-old signals captain. Nephew of Professor Azad Moradi. Handles communications at Darius’s command post.

Articles 5 and 6 of the North Atlantic Treaty — International treaty

Article 5 collective defense has been invoked only once, after the September 11 attacks in 2001. Article 6 limits its scope to the territory of member states in Europe and North America, islands in the North Atlantic north of the Tropic of Cancer, and vessels and aircraft there.
