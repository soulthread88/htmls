Scene 1 / 5

The lieutenant colonel came alone. Driving his own car. As if he were walking in, again.

Naser Taheri walked once around the collapsed main building and came down. Three flights of stairs. There was concrete dust on his boots. He took in the low ceiling underground, the three screens, the people sitting shoulder to shoulder. Then he sat across from the colonel’s place, on an upturned ammunition crate. Two months ago this man had sat with a file on his knees. Today his hands were empty.

Darius Sepahvand didn’t send for tea. There were no teacups down here. The ones upstairs had all broken. The saucers too.

“Is this an investigation?”

“A confirmation. The damage reports from the command posts hit on the night of June 10 have been collected at the General Staff. Someone put them in a table, and when you put things in a table, some boxes stand out. Not many places had the main building destroyed and the whole watch come out unhurt.” Taheri didn’t take out a notebook. “I came before anyone looks at that box twice.”

Darius waited. Waiting was the thing he had done most in three months.

“At 1500 on June 9 the attaché from the Pakistani embassy paid a call. Reconfirmation of ceasefire monitoring. Thirty minutes. At 2000 that day the night watch came down here. Five hours apart.”

“As the log says. There was an engagement the night before. The first in sixty-two days. My battery fired two missiles and survived, and I thought about what would come next. A command post gets hit in the first hour. I have taught that for thirty-one years.”

“I know. That reason stands. Whoever reads it.” Taheri rested both hands on his knees. “What did the attaché ask?”

“Three questions on monitoring procedure. The state of the battery, whether the rules of engagement had changed, the time of firing on the night of the 8th.”

“And drank tea.”

“Picked it up and put it down.”

The two men looked at each other. A drop of water fell from a pipe in the ceiling. The people underground were each looking at their own screens. Pretending to. In this country people knew well how to pretend not to listen.

Taheri looked away first. It was the first time the hunter had turned his eyes away first.

“In May, in Evin, I made a drawer. To keep the question of the seven seconds in. One I decided not to open while I’m alive. Today I need one more drawer.” He spoke low. Low enough that the man beside them wouldn’t hear. “My report will say this. Commander’s survivability measure following the June 8 engagement. Justified. Unrelated to the foreign attaché’s visit. If this report is in the file first, a second report doesn’t get written. That’s why I came alone.”

“Why go that far?”

“Because twelve people lived. I’ve spent nineteen years in counterintelligence. Of those, there aren’t many days I can say people lived because of my report.” He paused. “But Colonel. I’m not a man with many drawers. Two is the end. A third I can’t shut for you.”

“There won’t be a third.”

It was true. Eight days ago he had given the attaché a last farewell to carry. Farewell, honest soldier. He didn’t know how far those words had gone. If they had arrived, the person on the other side would know what they meant. That it was what you say to a sentry going off watch. The farewell said it was the end. He meant to keep to the end.

Taheri nodded. Then he talked about something else. In a lower voice.

“I went around through the rooms next door. Mess, entry, dispatch. Before dawn on April 7, nine names drew night rations at the General Staff Signals Directorate. Of those, three had passes for the room with Terminal 3. One of the three put in a request for discharge the day before yesterday. The day after the deal was announced.”

“The name?”

“I won’t tell you. It’s better for my work if you don’t know, Colonel. But please tell Professor Moradi this. The paper wasn’t written for nothing. It may take three more months, or three years. Peacetime work is slow. But peacetime work doesn’t forget.”

There were footsteps on the stairs. Someone running down. Second Lieutenant Sohrab Jahangiri. The bandage was still on his forehead. He saw the lieutenant colonel’s rank and hesitated, but what he had brought was bigger.

“Colonel. They signed. Not Friday — today. Their president did it first, in France, and our president did too. It was on the broadcast.”

On Wednesday, June 17, after attending the Group of Seven summit, President Trump signed the memorandum of understanding just before a dinner hosted by the French president at the Palace of Versailles. As he signed he said, “This was not easy,” and leaving the palace he told reporters, “It’s signed… I signed it in Versailles… Just signed it.” President Pezeshkian then signed in Tehran. Iranian state television aired footage of him holding up the signed document. That night Iran’s foreign ministry spokesman Baghaei said the memorandum had been finalized by the presidents’ signatures and was in effect, and that Friday’s ceremony in Switzerland would not take place.

It went quiet underground. Then someone let out a long breath, and the sergeant who had grumbled the first night covered his face with both hands. His shoulders heaved. Nobody looked at him. They were all busy keeping their own faces. In the summer of 1988 this country’s soldiers had cried like that too. Where nobody looked.

Darius stayed seated. He was doing arithmetic. Arithmetic he could have chosen not to do. Seventeen of his men. One signals officer. His daughter’s two lines. His brother’s photograph. And two pieces of paper the size of a palm, no longer in his hands. On the first night of peace he counted what he had lost and what he had kept, side by side.

“Is it over?” Sohrab asked.

“Sixty days. That’s what the paper says, I hear. They talk again for sixty days.”

“And after that?”

“After that, the people after that will know.” He stood up. His knees were stiff. “Tonight’s watch is as usual. The morning report too. A paper getting signed doesn’t empty the sky.”

Taheri stood too. He put on his cap and said,

“Counterintelligence is a peacetime profession, Colonel. During a war everybody looks ahead and nobody looks to the side. From tomorrow we look to the side. Who survived and why, who did what where. People like me get busy.” He took a step toward the stairs, then looked back. “So from tomorrow, keep your log even better. The way you have up to today.” After the revolution, after the war, this country had always begun to look to the side like that.

“Counterintelligence is a peacetime profession. During a war everybody looks ahead and nobody looks to the side. From tomorrow we look to the side.”

Scene 2 / 5

Kang Yujin read the news on the subway. The people on their way to work were looking at the same screen.

Signed. Not Friday but Wednesday night. Not Switzerland but France. She rode one stop past hers with the phone in her hand. Getting off and walking back, what she thought about wasn’t the war. It was the line her machine had put out at 23:50 last night. LIKELY (0.86) — signature event, 06-19, Switzerland.

There was no one in the office. Without even putting down her bag she opened the log. A four-month habit.

There were four lines stamped in the early morning.

signature event: realized 06-17. forecast: 06-19. date mismatch (−2d).

location: Versailles, FR. forecast: Switzerland. mismatch.

basis class "public statements / multiple sources": realization 0/1.

note: no precursor in ingest. event preceded forecast window.

She looked a long time at the fourth line. No precursor in ingest. The machine had read what everyone in the world read and believed what everyone in the world believed. Friday, by the lake. And it was wrong along with everyone in the world. That the two presidents would move it up two days had not been written down first in any newspaper or any analyst’s document. A few people decided it in some room, and only after they decided did the world know.

A laugh came out of her. In the empty office, out loud. The first sound she had made like that in four months.

Four months ago she would have read these lines as a failure. The model was wrong. The weights needed adjusting. Now she read them differently. There had been a spring when this machine got things right two seconds ahead of people. She never did find out where those two seconds came from, and she had written in item four of the handover memo that she didn’t know. This morning the machine had read the same things as people and been wrong in the same place. That wasn’t a malfunction. It was where it belonged. History’s decisions were still made by a few people in some room. The machine wasn’t in that room.

For the first time, something had come from the far end of the channel. After four months. By the road they said ran one way. For the first time the wall spoke.

It was a technical liaison form. There was no name in the sender field, only a title. Channel operations. It was Priya Narayan. Three lines.

1. Of your requests recorded in the minutes of May 13, the first (feedback of execution records) has not been approved. The reason cannot be disclosed.

2. However, please be advised that as of June 15 one field has been added to our receiving form: “Action taken on the basis of this output (Y/N).” These values will be tallied once a week and sent to you. Case-by-case content will not be included.

3. Your output on yesterday’s signing (0.86) was not used on our side as the basis for any action. For your information. — Personally: I can’t put “thanks for being wrong” on a form, it seems.

Kang Yujin read it three times. The last line four times.

Yes, no. Once a week. No case-by-case content. A tenth of what she had wanted. Not enough to break the loop. But the other side had admitted on a form that the loop existed. A field that counted whether someone had acted on this machine’s words or not. Four months ago no one had thought to count such a thing. Big systems always confessed one field at a time.

She opened the handover memo. Eleven pages. She deleted the last sentence of item two, as of June 16 there has been no reply, and rewrote it.

On June 18 the receiving side agreed to send a weekly tally of “action taken (Y/N).” Case-by-case execution records have still not been received. If the “Y” count in this tally rises, the operator should suspect the loop.

Today was Thursday. Ten o’clock at the library. The Beacon System of the Joseon Navy had been on that shelf for two months. A book nobody borrowed. Today she meant to give Yoon Jaeha the handover memo. No phone, no laptop, on paper. The most important document about the newest machine, in the oldest way.

Before she left she looked once more at the model’s line for today. Then she wrote in the operator-note field. The field she had first used on the night of April 25.

Operator note 06-18: Model was wrong with everyone else. Keep it that way. — Y.K.

The machine read the same things as people and was wrong in the same place. That wasn’t a malfunction. It was where it belonged.

Scene 3 / 5

The wall screen in the operations center was the same screen the dots had crossed seven days ago. The same screen showed the bombing and the signing, one after the other.

Today it showed a palace. A gilded room. The President holding a pen, and the Secretary of State standing behind him. Leila Shirazi watched it from her place. Many people were standing to watch. Someone started to clap, found he was alone, and stopped. A hundred and seven years ago a treaty that ended one war in the same palace became the seed of the next war. She had learned that in college.

That day the United States and Iran released the text of the fourteen points. The memorandum, written in English and Persian, was signed electronically by the two presidents, and was regarded as the first agreement signed jointly by the presidents of the United States and Iran since the founding of the Islamic Republic in 1979. At a press conference President Trump said he would bomb Iran again if it violated the deal, and also said that Iran not being allowed ballistic missiles was a little unfair. Iran’s chief negotiator, Parliament Speaker Qalibaf, said on state television that through negotiation Iran had gained many times over everything it had sought to gain by military action. The G7 leaders welcomed the deal and called for an immediate ceasefire in Lebanon. Brent crude futures fell below $80 a barrel, the lowest since the war began.

Sam Farrell came over with two coffees and set one on her desk.

“Not easy, he says.”

“I heard.”

“How about you? Was it easy?”

Instead of answering she pointed at another screen. Persian. The foreign ministry spokesman’s words had come up as a wire flash.

“Now is the time to test the implementation of the deal. They chose the word test. Not keep, not enjoy. And the speaker of their parliament called it a record of America’s failure, and our President said he’d bomb them if they break it.” She picked up her coffee. “Only a paper both sides can say they won gets signed. That isn’t a bad thing. If fewer people die that way.”

“Spoken like an analyst giving a toast.”

“It’s not a toast. It’s an assessment.”

Sam laughed. Then he lowered his voice.

“You see what came down from seven? Friday turned into Wednesday, so they say your thing got moved up too.”

“Tomorrow morning, they said.”

She said that and took her eyes off the screen. Nine tomorrow morning. 7E-41. Put him to sleep, task him, end it, bring him out. One of the four would come down written on paper. Her opinion was one, and that room’s only opinion was that one. But above that room there was another room. In this building the decision was always one floor up.

She knew this morning’s four lines by heart. Eastern Sector 3. No unidentified tracks. No engagement. Seventh day clearing main building debris; duties continue at alternate command post. Prepared by Jahangiri. Confirmed by Sepahvand. Seventh day. Those people had been living three floors down for seven days. With three screens.

On the screen the President was leaving the palace. Reporters shouted. He turned and said something. A caption came up. Signed it. Just now.

Of the fourteen points, none was about people. She had thought that in the elevator yesterday. Today she thought a little differently. The points about people weren’t in the memorandum. They were in drawers. In some drawer in this building, and probably in some drawer in a building over there too. The end of a war also meant those drawers would begin to open. That was what happened to the drawers of East Berlin when the Cold War ended.

She took out the notebook. The one she had switched to on April 21, when she stopped typing and moved to paper. On the last page she wrote one line.

June 17. Signed. He’s alive. That is as far as I know. Fight tomorrow so that it’s enough.

Only a paper both sides can say they won gets signed. That isn’t a bad thing. If fewer people die that way.

Scene 4 / 5

Someone in the liaison cell whistled. Henderson. The sound of a big country’s officer letting out relief.

Lieutenant Kim Junho didn’t whistle. He was copying points four and five of the just-released text into his notebook. Not yesterday’s leaked version but the one the government released. Word by word. A small country’s officer read a big country’s documents one word at a time.

Point four of the released memorandum said the United States would begin lifting the naval blockade immediately upon signing and end it completely within 30 days. Point five said that upon signing Iran would provide for the safe passage of merchant ships between the Persian Gulf and the Sea of Oman without fees for 60 days only, and that merchant traffic would begin immediately but settle in within 30 days, since technical and military obstacles and mines had to be cleared. Iran was to work out the future management of the strait and maritime services in consultation with Oman. That night Iran’s foreign ministry spokesman said Iran would finalize a new management regime for the strait together with Oman and would collect service fees there.

Begin immediately. He underlined the phrase twice. It hadn’t been in yesterday’s version. Thirty days was the date of the end; the beginning was tonight. On paper.

His phone buzzed. Seoul. First Secretary Han Jiwon of the foreign ministry. Someone who had spoken only by text for three months was calling for the first time.

“Lieutenant. This is Han Jiwon. First time hearing your voice.”

“Yes. First time.”

“I’ll keep it short. Starting tomorrow morning our embassy in Tehran begins consulting with their port authority on transit order. Through the same channel we opened for the Universal Winner. We need your list. Draft, cargo, drinking water left, crew size, current anchorage for all twenty-three ships. Just as you’ve been writing them down for three months.”

“Who decides the order?”

“They set the routes, and we propose the order. We’d like you to write the draft of that proposal. That’s what headquarters decided. You’re the only one who knows those ships one by one.”

Kim Junho looked down at his notebook. Twenty-three lines. Three months of handwriting layered on every line. Three days of water. Two days of water. One crewman appendicitis, helicopter medevac. Daughter, Suneung. Two periods. It was a record that wasn’t in any government file. The way one lieutenant had remembered people for three months.

“I’ll send it before tonight is out.”

“Thank you. And Lieutenant.” The first secretary’s voice wavered a little. “Your report of March 15. The one saying to make a consultation channel with Iran the priority. When I read it, I wondered if this person was really twenty-nine. That channel is how we got here today.”

After he hung up he unfolded Henderson’s chart.

Setting an order looked a lot like putting a price on people. He hated that. So he decided to rank by circumstance, not by price. The ship with the least water first. Then ships with sick crew. Then ships with shallow drafts that could get through the narrow channel. The chemical tanker with a twelve-meter draft was last. Its captain knew that. Yesterday he had sent this. Lieutenant. I know we’re last. That’s all right. Only confirm for me that there is a last.. Two periods.

Kim Junho chose the ship to put on the first line of the list. Two days of water. Twenty-two crew. From where that ship lay at anchor to the narrow channel south of Larak Island, he drew one line in pencil. On top of the lines Henderson had drawn for four months. For the first time, with his own hand.

No ship had passed along that line yet. None might pass along it tomorrow either. The drones that went up every night might go up tonight, and the warships on the blockade line hadn’t gotten their orders yet. He knew all that. The signing was not the end but the beginning of the order.

Still, at the end of the line he wrote no date and no question mark. Only the name of the ship.

Henderson looked over his shoulder.

“First line.”

“First line, sir.”

“What are you going to write on the last line? When the twenty-third ship gets out.”

Kim Junho thought about it. That dawn in April he had said he couldn’t close the notebook. Not until his ships were floating in the Gulf of Oman.

“I won’t write anything, sir. I’ll close it.”

At the end of the line he wrote no date and no question mark. Only the name of the ship.

Scene 5 / 5 — Final

The paper had been given his city’s name. The mailman’s city.

The Islamabad Memorandum of Understanding. Assistant Secretary Tariq Mehmood watched the name go by in a television caption in his office. In the corridor people were hugging each other. He had never counted how many cables had gone between the two capitals in three and a half months. The prime minister’s name and the field marshal’s name would go into the history books. His name wouldn’t. He didn’t mind that in the least.

What he minded was something else. Not the name. The end.

There was a note on his desk. Something the attaché at the embassy in Tehran had passed on orally last week, and he had written down. One line in English.

Farewell, honest soldier.

He had taken the survey course in English literature in Lahore. He knew the line. What you say to a sentry going off watch. And after it comes a question. Who hath relieved you?

The colonel hadn’t said the second half. Tariq thought he knew why. He wasn’t going to ask. Who would sit in the place the person on the other side left. And he was saying don’t write again. A farewell from someone who knew it was the end.

The attaché had passed on one more thing. Something he learned after June 11. Under the sheet where he had written it out in Persian, Tariq added his own words, as he had the last time.

From the one who passed it on: He read the paper and that evening took all his people down below. Two days later the top came down. Twelve lived. He is alive, and his chair is three floors down.

And he wrote one more line. This time not the words of the one who passed it on, but his own.

From the mailman: The pretext for courtesy calls ends tonight. The attaché who monitored the ceasefire now has to monitor the peace, and peace has no saucers. This is the last pouch.

He put down his pen. Thirty years of mail had ended.

On the night of April 5, a sheet of paper with a blank operator field came out of the printer on this floor. Give them a door. He never did find out who printed it. After that he had carried people’s words. Two going, one coming. Now one more coming. None of those four had an operator field. Every hand that passed them on knew whose words they were.

Whether the sheet the machine put out had opened a door, he didn’t know. That one of the four messages passed by human hands had saved twelve people, he did know. Of everything about this war, that arithmetic was the one thing he knew for certain, after thirty years as a diplomat. History would remember the memorandum’s name. It would not remember the papers in the pouch.

He folded the paper and wrapped it in foil. From a cigarette pack. Friday’s pouch went to Washington. The attaché there would have lunch with someone. At a place where the lamb was good.

Someone in the corridor laughed loudly. Tariq waited for the sound to fade. Then, low, in a room with no one in it, he asked the second half the colonel hadn’t.

“Who hath relieved you?”

There was no one to answer. He turned off the light. In the dark the foil gleamed once.

Whether the sheet the machine put out had opened a door, he didn’t know. That a word passed by a human hand had saved twelve people, he did know.

Background Analysis
Versailles — The Signing That Came Two Days Early

The Islamabad Memorandum of Understanding took shape with the electronic signatures of both sides’ representatives on June 14, and the formal signing ceremony was to be held in Switzerland on June 19. But on Wednesday, June 17, President Trump, attending the Group of Seven summit (Évian-les-Bains, France), signed it just before a dinner at the Palace of Versailles, and President Pezeshkian followed by signing in Tehran. Iran’s foreign ministry said the memorandum took effect that day and the ceremony in Switzerland would not be held. The same day the two countries released the text of the fourteen points.

Even on the day of the signing, the words diverged. President Trump said he would bomb again if Iran violated it, and Iran’s foreign ministry spokesman said Iran would watch U.S. implementation with zero tolerance. The spokesman also said missiles were not up for negotiation and that Iran would not send highly enriched uranium out of the country. Israel was not a party to the signing, and in southern Lebanon airstrikes and shelling went on that day too. The Israeli military said five soldiers were wounded in a Hezbollah drone attack.

Reuters reported that by the time of the signing more than 7,000 people had been killed in this war, mainly in Iran and Lebanon. The Iranian government put its own death toll at 3,528, and the rights group HRANA counted 3,684. The memorandum was not a document that ended the war. It was a framework for negotiating a final agreement within 60 days, and those 60 days would not run their full course. The underground in the Alborz, the log in Seoul, Langley, the chart in Bahrain and the note in Islamabad in this episode are all the author’s invention. That the South Korean government consulted with Iran on transit order is a premise based on the consultation channel the foreign ministry described at the first transit on May 20; the phone call and list on this day are fictional.
