Scene 1 / 6

23:49:30. She pressed it.

suspend outbound: confirmed. operator Y.K.

Thirty seconds later the model put out its table. Kang Yujin read it. Four lines. This time it wasn’t the sky. It was a table saying that a certain command post’s communications frequency would change at a certain time. Confidence zero point six six. In the basis box, stated intent again. Three days ago it would have been a number somewhere around zero point three. The model was counting the way it had learned.

The fifth line was stamped.

output → channel KR-7: HELD (outbound suspended).

It didn’t go out. For the first time in three months, what the model wrote stayed in this room. One researcher’s finger had shut the channel between two countries.

She looked at the clock. And waited. Knowing what she was waiting for.

00:07. The phone on the desk rang. She usually left it at home. Today she had brought it. On purpose.

The name on the screen was Park Sejun.

“Dr. Kang.” His voice wasn’t the voice of a man just woken up. “What did you press?”

“Suspend outbound.”

“Why.”

“The loop closed. The model watches what it said come true and raises its own confidence. Zero point three four became zero point seven one. Tonight’s table is zero point six six. You said it only listened. A machine that only listens doesn’t learn that it was right.”

On the other end, breathing. Once, long.

“The phone rang in seventeen minutes,” he said. “For me. From over there. A human voice.”

“Who?”

“I don’t know. Really. For five months the other side was paper. Memorandum of understanding, technical annex, quarterly settlement. Today was the first time I heard a human voice. English, a man, didn’t give a name, wasn’t angry. That scared me more. He just asked. Is it a technical problem, or some other problem.”

“What did you say?”

“That I’d look into it.” Park Sejun laughed briefly. A tired laugh. “Dr. Kang. Release it by morning. I’ll write the after-action report. A security check drill. Then nobody gets hurt.”

“I can’t release it.”

“Doctor.”

“If I release it, tonight’s table goes out. The zero-point-six-six one. If that goes out, somebody somewhere over there will move something again, and the model will see it and push to zero point eight. If I sign the report you’ll write for me, I become the person who released it knowing.”

“Right now you’re the person who blocked it knowing. Have you thought about which weighs more?”

“I have. For three days.”

There was a long silence. She had expected him to get angry. He didn’t.

“Until Thursday,” he said. “Until noon Thursday I’ll hold them off. We’ll call it a technical check. After that it’s out of my hands. And, Dr. Kang.”

“Yes.”

“That button — you know who made it? In the design review I said to take it out. Said we’d never use it. You insisted on putting it in. Remember?”

She remembered. The conference room three months ago. She had said: You shouldn’t build something you can’t turn off. She had been repeating a line from a textbook. She hadn’t thought she would be the one to press that button. The people who built the atomic bomb didn’t think first about how to turn it off either.

“You shouldn’t build something you can’t turn off.” Three months ago she was only repeating a textbook. She hadn’t thought the hand pressing that button would be her own.

Scene 2 / 6

Darius Sepahvand was still sitting in his own chair.

Yesterday at noon he hadn’t known it would go this way. Going down the stairs with the folder, he had thought he might not come back up. Of the officers in this country who went down to the basement, many never climbed those stairs again.

The lieutenant colonel was waiting in that room underground. The room where the second lieutenant had sat for three weeks. Darius set the folder on the desk. And before the lieutenant colonel could open it, he spoke.

There’s one thing I have to report first. This morning at six sharp, an unidentified track appeared beyond the northeast ridge of my sector. Civilian code response, two hundred eleven seconds. I did not engage. The log is the top sheet.

The lieutenant colonel read the top sheet. Then he went through the folder to the end. He didn’t hurry. His hand stopped at the operating log for March 30. It stopped at the log for April 1. He took a sheet from his own bag and set it beside them. The technical report of April 7.

Four sheets lay side by side.

March 30. One hundred twenty-seven seconds. Your signature, Colonel. April 1. One hundred twenty-seven seconds. In the sky. April 7. One hundred twenty-seven seconds. The whole net. And today. Two hundred eleven seconds. The lieutenant colonel touched them one by one with his finger. Colonel. Last week I laid out three sheets and drew one picture. Someone is talking with the outside using a single number. That someone is in this command post. Until this morning, the picture was right.

Today.

Today the number is different. If the picture is right, today should be one twenty-seven too. If it’s a conversation. But it’s different. And you brought this to me before I asked. A man with something to hide doesn’t do that.

Darius said nothing. He had told Farhan you could hide a lot while telling nothing but the truth. Now he was hiding by not speaking.

Why isn’t it one twenty-seven today? the lieutenant colonel asked.

That’s what I’d like to ask. That was the truth. Not one letter added.

The lieutenant colonel looked at him a long time. Then he gathered the four sheets again.

I’m going to change the order. I was looking first for the man inside this command post. Now I’ll look outside first. Who is writing numbers in our sky. Whatever the number is. Once I know that, the inside will show itself. He stood. The one twenty-seven on March 30 is still your signature, Colonel. I won’t forget that. I’m only putting it off.

That was yesterday.

This morning Darius stood at the window turning those words over again. Putting it off. Not letting him go. A hunter following one animal’s tracks had seen bigger tracks. Once he caught the big one, he would come back.

But whose were the big tracks?

Since yesterday morning he had been tied to that question. Two hundred eleven. A number with no meaning. Something that wasn’t the person over there. Then there were two over there. The hand that gave back his brother’s service number, and the hand that only imitated the grammar. He had no way to tell which hand was which. Even that the one twenty-seven of April 1 had been a person was now only a belief.

And April 7. The hand that turned off his country’s net for one hundred twenty-seven seconds. It had known his number. The number he had given the other side. Then that hand was over there, or somewhere it could listen in on the other side’s words.

He and the lieutenant colonel were chasing the same thing. For different reasons. The day the lieutenant colonel caught it, Darius himself would come out hanging from the end of it.

A knock at the door. Farhan. A cable in his hand.

“Came down from the General Staff. Next Wednesday the military attaché from the Pakistani embassy is paying a courtesy call, under ceasefire monitoring. A thirty-minute meeting with the commander.”

“What’s Pakistan coming all the way up here to see?”

“It says the mediating country is making the rounds of air defense command posts to pay respects. Of four, we’re third.”

Darius took the cable and read it. An attaché. Thirty minutes. A cup of tea. He put it in the file tray. To him it was a nuisance of protocol. History often knocks on the door wearing the face of a nuisance of protocol.

A hunter following one animal’s tracks saw bigger tracks. He wasn’t letting go. He was putting it off. Once he caught the big one, he would come back.

Scene 3 / 6

The chief went into the Inspector General’s office at eight on Tuesday morning and came out at eleven.

Leila Shirazi heard about it Wednesday morning. In the chief’s office, with the door shut.

“They took it. All six pages. The Inspector General read it himself.” The chief wasn’t wearing a tie. “And then he said this. It might not be illegal.”

“An aircraft flew on an unapproved draft.”

“There was approval. The duty officer’s. The form was built by the rules, the intake account was authorized, the memorandum with the ally passed legal review. There was a person at every link and a signature at every link. The Inspector General looked up at page three and asked me. Which part is the violation?”

“What did you say?”

“That there wasn’t one. That that’s what I was reporting. That nobody broke a rule and something happened that nobody decided. That what we banned in 2024 has come back without breaking a single ban.”

The Inspector General said he would open a preliminary review. Thirty days. That was Tuesday morning.

At four Tuesday afternoon a call came from seven. The chief didn’t say what it was about. But Wednesday morning there was a box on his desk. Empty. For now. The one who raises the warning leaves first. The old order of things in this building.

“Administrative leave, they said. Standard procedure, to separate the parties during the review. I’m the complainant, so I’m a party.” He tried to smile. “It isn’t wrong. Those people never say anything wrong.”

“Starting when?”

“Tomorrow. Midnight Thursday.”

Leila stood there. Of her sixteen years, she had worked eleven under this man. He was the one who had sent her assessments back the most. He was the one who had never once stepped back when she fought the people upstairs.

“What about ZARRIN? Who handles the ZARRIN file?”

“You do. I wrote it down from the start that you’re the only channel. That can’t be changed. Changing it means rewriting the source-handling rules, and that won’t happen in thirty days.” He put a book in the box. A book of Hafez. She hadn’t known he owned it. “But Monday they’ll call you up to seven. Go and listen. Don’t fight. Answer only what they ask. And memorize what they ask. What they ask is what they don’t know.”

“And the note?”

The chief’s hand stopped. He glanced once at the door.

“What note,” he said. And very low: “I didn’t tell the Inspector General about that either. That isn’t a record. That’s a letter you sent your aunt.”

At eleven Thursday night she watched him get into the elevator with the box. Sam Farrell stood beside her. Neither of them said anything. Before the elevator doors closed, the chief said one thing.

“If there’s any way to tell Y.K. thank you, tell them. There won’t be.”

Nobody broke a rule, and something happened that nobody decided. That was the whole complaint.

Scene 4 / 6

Yoon Jaeha was reading the same book in the same seat.

When Kang Yujin sat down, he closed the book. This time he spoke first.

“You pressed it.”

“You told me to act as usual. I couldn’t.”

“I know. It got noisy on our side too, after midnight Tuesday.” There was no reproach in Yoon Jaeha’s face. “Doctor. I told you to act as usual. I take that back. Because you pressed it, we learned something we didn’t know for five months.”

“What?”

“That they call in seventeen minutes. And that it isn’t paper calling, it’s a person. Someone over there watches that channel in real time. Enough to pick up the phone in the middle of the night because one table didn’t arrive. All this time the giving side didn’t know how much what it gave was worth to them. Now it knows.”

She knew where that was going. Knowing the value means you can bargain. This man was turning her fear into leverage. That was how a small country’s intelligence service worked. Collecting, one by one, things to hold in front of a big ally.

“You want me to open it again.”

“At noon today Mr. Park’s time runs out. If it’s still blocked after that, this stops being one researcher’s judgment and becomes a matter between two countries. Then it leaves your hands. That button too. Next time they’ll lay down a board without that button.”

“So open it myself, while it’s still in my hands.”

“While it’s in your hands, your way,” he said. “I’m not saying just open it. You’ve been thinking for three days, haven’t you? What to change before you open it.”

She had. For two days since Tuesday night she had been working out why her annotation hadn’t reached them. She had no way of knowing their side. But she could trace backward from the result. Of the four lines the model wrote, what survived and got carried out? The time survived. The number two hundred eleven survived. The place survived. The confidence died. The basis died. The annotation died whole.

What survived had something in common. All of it was what. What died was all how sure. Some bowl over there held the first kind and spilled the second.

“I’m going to change the output format,” she said. “I won’t write confidence separately. I’ll put it inside the content. So it can’t be torn off.”

She was about to ask for a blank sheet when she remembered there was nothing on his desk. So she wrote on the desk with her finger.

“Right now it’s like this. Content: 211 seconds. Confidence: 0.34. Two boxes. If they take only the first box, the second disappears. From now on it goes out like this. Content: Likely not to happen (0.34) — 211 seconds. One box. If they want the content they have to take those words too. Even someone who reads only numbers reads the first word.”

“In English?”

“UNLIKELY. Below zero point five it starts with UNLIKELY, and only above zero point eight is it LIKELY. In between, UNCERTAIN. And if the basis is a single document, it gets SINGLE SOURCE. In capitals.”

Yoon Jaeha looked a long time at the empty spot on the desk. Where she had written with her finger.

“That isn’t fixing the model.”

“The model said it right from the start. What needs fixing is the model’s mouth. So that what it says carefully only sounds careful.”

“And the loop? The model learning from watching its own words come true?”

“I can’t fix that. Not from here.” She was candid. “To teach the model not to count things that happened because of what it said, the model has to know which things happened because of it. That only works if someone at the far end tells this end who moved what. And that channel only flows one way.”

“Then we’ll have to get that out of them,” he said, standing. For the first time something that wasn’t businesslike crept into his voice. “Out of the people who call in seventeen minutes. That’s my job, Doctor. You fix the mouth. I’ll open the ear.”

He put the book on the return counter and left. She reached the lab ten minutes before noon, changed the output format, and released the button. Fixing it and opening it was a heavier decision than blocking it.

Bahrain — Mina Salman Port, the coalition liaison cell at the headquarters of the U.S. Fifth Fleet

Scene 5 / 6

The sentence came up on the liaison cell’s screen around ten Friday night.

May 1 was the 60-day deadline set by the War Powers Resolution. The law requires the president to withdraw forces if Congress has not authorized the military action within 60 days of his notifying it. That day, in a letter to the Speaker of the House and the Senate president pro tempore, President Trump wrote that the hostilities that began on February 28, 2026, “have terminated.” The day before, Defense Secretary Hegseth had told a Senate hearing that the ceasefire means the 60-day clock “pauses or stops.” On April 30 the Senate rejected, for the sixth time, a Democratic resolution meant to halt the war, and Congress went into a week’s recess.

The hostilities that began on February 28, 2026, have terminated.

Lieutenant Kim Junho read it without making a sound. Terminated. He didn’t need to look the word up. It meant over.

On the next screen was the day’s blockade status. Cumulative ships stopped. Turned back. Boarded and searched. Destroyer positions in the Arabian Sea. The name of the merchant ship the Marines had boarded three days ago. And in his notebook, twenty-six lines. Ships still floating inside the Gulf on the sixty-third day.

Lieutenant Commander Mark Henderson pulled up a chair and sat.

“Congratulations, Lieutenant. They say the war’s over.”

“Do your warships know that, sir?”

“Our warships aren’t lawyers.”

Henderson said it without smiling. He looked at the sentence on the screen a long time.

“My father did two tours in Vietnam. He used to say a war ends three times. Once in Washington, once in the papers, and once in the heads of the guys who were there. Some of them, the third one never ends.”

“Today’s the first.”

“Half of the first. Only on paper.”

Kim Junho opened his notebook. He thought a long time about what to write. A liaison officer’s job was to put what he saw into his own country’s words. What he had seen today couldn’t be put into words. On the same day the same government wrote with one hand that hostilities had ended, and with the other kept another country’s ports shut with ten thousand troops and twelve warships. They would say both sentences were true. The language of law and the reality of the sea stood face to face in a single paragraph.

He wrote.

5.1 U.S. president notifies Congress “hostilities terminated” (War Powers 60-day deadline). Blockade continues. Strait transit not resumed. No change in status of our 26 ships.

And a line below it, in handwriting that would not go in the report:

Between writing that it’s over and its being over, there are twenty-six of my ships.

After midnight a text came. The captain with the frequent cough. Lieutenant, the news says the war is over. When do we get out? The crew are packing. Should I stop them?

For a long time Kim Junho couldn’t write an answer. Sailors packing. People who had taken out their bags at the first good word they had heard in sixty-three days. One sentence from a big country had moved the hands of a small country’s sailors. The sentence hadn’t been written for them.

Captain. That “terminated” is a word written in a letter to the U.S. Congress. It wasn’t written on the sea. The strait is the same. I’m sorry. Tell them not to unpack, just leave it. Packed. That’s better.

A war ends three times. Once in the capital, once in the papers, once in the heads of the people who were there. That day was half of the first.

Scene 6 / 6 — Final

The lights in the chief’s office were off.

Every time Leila Shirazi passed it, she couldn’t stop herself slowing down. A habit of eleven years. All day one sentence circled on the wall screens of the operations center. The hostilities have terminated. The analysts saw the sentence and went on working. Inside their screens, nothing had terminated.

Around three-thirty Sam Farrell laid a sheet of paper face down on her desk and went. Without a word. She turned it over. An item from this morning’s annex. Not about the sky, but about a certain command post’s communications. Nothing to do with her work.

But the content box was different.

UNCERTAIN (0.66) / SINGLE SOURCE — target command post comms frequency change, 05-02 0400Z.

She read it three times. Then she thought of the last five months of annexes. The content box was always written like fact. What, when, where. Hard sentences. Today two words stood in front of the sentence. Uncertain. Single source. In capitals. Inside the content box, where they couldn’t be torn off.

The form was the same. Still four boxes. Someone hadn’t changed the bowl but had changed how to fill it. Knowing that confidence handed over separately gets spilled, they had stuck it to the thing itself.

Y.K.

She knew only those two letters. Not whether a man or a woman. Not what room in what city. But she knew that person had sent an annotation six days ago, that it hadn’t arrived, and so they had found another road. Without giving up. The way she had folded a note and sent it.

At each end of the machine stood one person. Neither knowing the other’s face. One fixing the machine’s mouth, one fooling the machine’s ear.

She put the paper in her drawer. If the chief had been there, he would have taken it with him. The chief wasn’t there.

At four an email came. The sender’s title was long, and the room number started with 7.

Analyst Shirazi. Monday, May 4, 09:00, 7E-41. Meeting regarding the handling status of source ZARRIN. No materials needed.

No materials needed. She looked at those words. Don’t bring paper. They knew where the chief had taken his paper. Power was afraid of paper. Paper stays.

Memorize what they ask. What they ask is what they don’t know.

On the way home she turned on the radio. The host was reading that sentence. The hostilities that began on February 28 have terminated. Someone called an expert explained what that meant legally. She turned the radio off.

In Tehran it would be close to midnight now. The man on the mountain had seen two hundred eleven seconds three mornings ago. What had he read it as? As her greeting? Then he would be puzzling now over why she had changed the number. A reason that didn’t exist.

How far had the note gotten? She had no way of knowing. If the chief had been there she would have asked him. The chief wasn’t there.

Terminated. She tried the word in Persian. Tamam shod. Ameh used it when a meal was over. Meaning we’ve finished eating. Meaning clear the table. But there was still food on the table. Who was going to clear it?

At each end of the machine stood one person. Neither knowing the other’s face. One fixing the machine’s mouth, one fooling the machine’s ear.

Background Analysis
Sixty Days — How the Word “Terminated” Was Used

The War Powers Resolution of 1973 lets the president begin military action without congressional authorization, but requires him to notify Congress within 48 hours and to withdraw forces if he has not received authorization within 60 days. President Trump notified Congress 48 hours after the first strikes, and the day those 60 days ran out was May 1.

That day, in a letter to congressional leaders, the president wrote that the hostilities that began on February 28, 2026, “have terminated.” His basis was that there had been no exchange of fire with Iran since the ceasefire. He also restated his view that the War Powers Resolution itself is unconstitutional, and acknowledged in the letter that the conflict might not be fully resolved. The day before, Defense Secretary Hegseth had told the Senate that the ceasefire stopped the 60-day clock, and Republican Senator Susan Collins said the deadline was a requirement, not a suggestion. The Senate rejected a resolution to halt the war for the sixth time.

On that same day U.S. forces were still stopping ships going in and out of Iran’s ports. And three days later U.S. forces would destroy several Iranian small boats in the Strait of Hormuz. “Terminated” was a word that did one job inside a legal document, while out on the sea other days went on. All six scenes in this episode are the author’s invention.
