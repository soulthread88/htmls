Tehran — Imam Khomeini Hospital, emergency ward
March 8, 2026 (Sun.) · 7:40 a.m. (Tehran local time) — War Day 9
Scene 1 / 8

The sun came up. It did not get light.

Maryam came off the night shift and stood at the ward window. Seven-forty. The sky over Tehran was the color of ink. It was not cloud. Cloud does not run that low, or that oily. The streetlights were still on. Cars crawled along with their headlights burning.

Before dawn on March 8, the Israeli Air Force bombed Iran’s oil facilities for the first time since the start of the war. Four people were killed, and black smoke rising from the storage tanks covered the sky over the capital. Along with fuel shortages came fears of contaminated water and soil and of respiratory harm. The same day, U.S. and Israeli forces said they had struck the headquarters of the Revolutionary Guards’ Aerospace Force and 50 ammunition storage sites.

The emergency room had been full since four in the morning. Not with people hurt by shrapnel. With people coughing. Children with asthma, old people with weak lungs, taxi drivers with red swollen eyes. By midnight half the oxygen tanks were already empty. For nine days Maryam had learned burns and fractures and crush injuries. Today she learned breath. The sound people make when they cannot breathe. In nursing school she had learned about Sardasht. 1987, the day gas came down from the sky and a whole border town coughed at once. Then it had been a page in a textbook. Today it was the whole ward.

She went up to the third floor of the nurses’ dormitory. Nine days ago the house on Golestan Street had come down. Since then mother and daughter had lived in a double room the hospital gave them. Her husband had been called up by his unit before dawn that day and had gone out and not come back. A man who wore the uniform of the Revolutionary Guards. Five days ago there had been one call. He was alive. He did not say where he was. She did not think past that. She had no time to think. Right now she was grateful for that.

Nariman had her chin on the windowsill, looking out. Seven years old. In the gap where her front tooth had been, a new one was coming up white.

“Maman, it’s morning. Why is it night?”

Maryam stuffed a wet towel into the crack of the window. It was a towel she had already stuffed there once. It had turned gray.

“Because oil is burning,” she said. She had no strength left to lie. “A very big oil tank is burning. It’ll take a few days.”

“So it’s night for a few days?”

“It’ll be dark for a few days. You can’t open the window. Promise.”

Nariman nodded. Then with her finger she drew a sun on the fogged glass. One circle, eight lines. Seen from outside it was a sun turned backward. Maryam did not wipe it off. It was the brightest sun in Tehran that morning.

A bomb hurts only the people it hits. Smoke picks everyone who breathes.

Langley, Virginia — CIA Iran Operations Center
March 8, 2026 (Sun.) · 8:18 a.m. EDT / 3:48 p.m. in Tehran — War Day 9
Scene 2 / 8

Dr. Leila Shirazi looked from one screen to the other.

The left screen was Tehran. In the satellite image the capital was half wiped out. Black smoke cut across the city from southwest to northeast. The Evin district sat on the edge of the band. Where her aunt Fereshte lived. Seventy. A woman who made tea with rose petals. The phones had been dead for nine days.

The right screen was Fordow. The signal was at yesterday’s height. 5.7 seconds. It did not waver.

Overnight the clocks had gone forward an hour. Daylight saving time. The difference between Washington and Tehran had shrunk from eight and a half hours to seven and a half. She held on to that small fact for a moment. The two countries had come an hour closer. The distance on the screen had not shrunk at all.

Sam Farrell came over with two cups of coffee.

“Heard it from the liaison office,” he said, setting down a cup. “Tel Aviv asked about your Fordow report. They want the raw data too.”

“Did they get it?”

“Who knows. That gets decided upstairs.” He pointed his chin at the left screen. “But look at that. They didn’t hit the mountain. They hit the oil.”

Leila chewed on that a long time. Her report had pointed at a mountain. The bombs had fallen on storage tanks. Instead of breaking through what could not be broken through, they had chosen to choke the country. From military targets to economic ones. The grammar of the war had changed overnight. The report spoke of a mountain and the decision chose oil.

“It’s a choice,” she said.

“Whose?”

“Israel’s. And if we didn’t stop them—ours.”

Sam did not answer. He was a satellite analyst. He could estimate how much oil was burning from the width and length of the smoke. He had learned it from satellite pictures of the Kuwaiti oil-well fires in 1991. The smoke in the textbook had been over someone else’s desert. The number he came up with now was a number he did not want to say out loud.

Leila put the cursor on the Evin coordinates. She took the resolution all the way up. Roofs. Courtyards. The shadow of the smoke. She looked for a courtyard with a rose vine. The satellite did not show roses.

The target list carries the names of buildings. The names of the people who live downwind are not.

Red Sea — bridge of the U.S. aircraft carrier USS Gerald R. Ford (CVN-78)
March 9, 2026 (Mon.) · 2:40 a.m. (local time) — War Day 10, before dawn
Scene 3 / 8

Captain Marcus Webb watched from the bridge as the map grew.

Ten days ago only two countries had been colored on this map. Iran and Israel. Now the color had spread. Lebanon. Iraq. Kuwait. Bahrain. Qatar. The United Arab Emirates. Saudi Arabia. Jordan. Oman. Off Cyprus, Greek fighters had shot down an Iranian drone. In the sky over Turkey a NATO interceptor had caught a ballistic missile. A drone had come down on an airport terminal in Azerbaijan. Off Sri Lanka a sunken frigate lay on the bottom.

Webb had served twenty-five years. He had gone through the Iraq war as an ensign. He had passed through every crisis since on a deck. No manual covered a war that spread this far in ten days. A manual is the record of the last war.

On March 8, U.S. Central Command announced that an Iranian attack had killed a seventh U.S. service member. The same day in Kuwait City, a drone attack set fire to a high-rise owned by the Public Institution for Social Security, and two Kuwaiti officers died in the line of duty. The Revolutionary Guards claimed to have struck Tel Aviv, Beersheba, and Muwaffaq Salti Air Base in Jordan. The United States was sending a third aircraft carrier, USS George H.W. Bush, to the Middle East.

Sarah Callahan’s spot was empty. The place twelve feet from the bulkhead. She had left by helicopter two days ago. Before she went she gave him a page torn from her notebook. The number of the Bahrain bureau. Under it one line. The days your hands are still are the ones that scare me most, Captain.

He kept the page in his breast pocket. He did not take it out to look at it. It was enough that it was there.

The officer of the deck brought a new cable. Hormuz. Marine insurance premiums had jumped from four to six times normal in a week. The ships trapped inside the Gulf did not move. Webb had learned the 1987 Tanker War from a textbook. That year the Navy had escorted tankers flying the Stars and Stripes into the strait. Now his ship was the American carrier farthest from it.

On a map a war spreads as color. On the sea it comes as distance—distance you cannot close to help.

Alborz Mountains, Iran — north of Tehran, Iranian Air Defense Force command post
March 9, 2026 (Mon.) · Afternoon (Tehran local time) — War Day 10
Scene 4 / 8

The name was announced.

Colonel Darius Sepahvand stood in front of the same television as eight days ago. The black band across the screen was still there. The anchor read the decision of the Assembly of Experts. The third Supreme Leader of the Islamic Republic. Mojtaba Khamenei. The dead leader’s second son.

On March 9, the Assembly of Experts announced that it had elected Mojtaba Khamenei the new Supreme Leader. Born in 1969, he was fifty-six years old. He had never held elected office or any official post. The new leader did not appear on screen. His first statement would be made public three days later, on March 12, read aloud by a state television announcer. On that same March 9, in Isfahan, the Safavid dynasty palaces Chehel Sotoun and Ali Qapu were badly damaged by bombing nearby.

Somebody in the command post shouted Allahu akbar. A few others joined in. Darius moved his lips too. Whether a sound came out, he did not know himself.

For eight days he had lived as a body without a head. No orders came. He decided for himself. Turn on the radars he had left, or turn them off? Turn them on, and missiles came inside an hour. Turn them off, and he could not see the sky. He turned them on and off by turns. He lost eleven men. He kept nineteen alive. He could not report that arithmetic to anyone. There was nowhere to report it.

Now there was a head. He found he felt no relief.

In 1995 he had enlisted with the first generation raised after the revolution. He had learned something then. This country was founded by driving out a king. It had shed blood to put an end to sons taking their fathers’ places. For thirty-one years he had taught that sentence to recruits. The name on the screen today stood on the other side of that sentence.

The signals officer came over. It was the first cable from the new chain of command. It was short. All units hold present positions. The strait stays closed.

“Will you reply, Colonel?”

“Acknowledge receipt,” Darius said. “Send only that.”

News of his daughter Narges had come three days ago. A call a neighbor had made for her. She was alive. The western sky was black. When was her father coming? He had not been able to answer the last question. He still had no answer. The new cable had no answer either.

The body had a new head. Which way was that head looking? Nobody had seen yet.

Bahrain — Mina Salman Port, the basement shelter at the headquarters of the U.S. Fifth Fleet
March 9, 2026 (Mon.) · 10:05 p.m. (Bahrain local time) — War Day 10, night
Scene 5 / 8

When the siren went off, Sarah Callahan had been on the ground in Bahrain for thirty hours.

She followed the others down the stairs. Concrete walls, fluorescent lights, folding chairs. About thirty people sat along the walls. A strange uniform sat down next to her. Not American camouflage. A Korean flag on the shoulder.

“Korean navy?” she asked.

“Lieutenant Kim Junho. Liaison officer.” His English was exact and a little slow. “You’re Ms. Callahan from the AP. I saw you on the list.”

There was a dull sound in the distance. One. A little closer, another. Dust came down from the ceiling. Kim Junho’s hand drew in once on his knee and opened again. Sarah saw it. And acted as if she had not.

On the night of March 9, an explosion near Manama started a fire close to a refining facility and injured at least 32 Bahraini civilians. Four were seriously hurt. An analysis reviewed by Reuters found it likely that a U.S.-operated Patriot interceptor was involved, in the course of shooting down an Iranian drone in the air. The same day, Bahrain’s state oil company, BAPCO, declared force majeure on its oil exports after its only refinery came under Iranian attack.

“What does a liaison officer do?” Sarah asked. In a shelter, asking was the only thing a reporter could do.

“I count ships,” he said. “Of the ships trapped inside the Gulf, the ones owned by Korean companies and the ones with Korean crews. Where they’ve anchored, how many days of water and food they have left. Every morning I send it to Seoul.”

“How many ships?”

“I can’t tell you that.” He looked sorry. “But—I know every one of them by name. I know the captains’ voices too. I talk to them every day on the sat phone. Yesterday one of them said: Lieutenant, we’re not targets. We’re more like hostages.”

Sarah did not take out her notebook. If she did, the young officer would shut up. She memorized the sentence. Not targets. Hostages. All the sailors tied up in the Gulf were in that short line.

“Aren’t you scared?” she asked.

Kim thought about it for a moment. It was not the face of a man choosing a lie. It was the face of a man choosing the exact word.

“I am scared. But in the morning, once I begin calling the ships by name, it gets better. A person who has something to count can endure.”

The all-clear sounded. People got to their feet. Climbing the stairs, Sarah thought about it. The captain of the Ford had endured with still hands. This lieutenant endured by counting. And she—she was enduring by memorizing sentences.

In a war everyone picks one thing to hold on to. That thing is their war.

Bahrain — Mina Salman Port, the quarters area at the headquarters of the U.S. Fifth Fleet
March 10, 2026 (Tue.) · 11:40 p.m. (Bahrain local time) — War Day 11, night
Scene 6 / 8

Lieutenant Kim Junho was writing a letter.

He could have made a short call. But what he wanted to say was hard to say on the phone. He had called his mother five days ago. Tonight he wrote to his father. With his father, writing had always been easier than talking. It had been that way since he was small. His father was a quiet man. So was the son. Between two quiet men, words always came out too few or too many.

Dear Father,

I’m in Bahrain now. It is day eleven of the war. If anything has changed since I came, it’s that now I know the smell of this port, and I know how many steps it is to the stairs when the sirens go. That isn’t everything. But it is everything I know for sure right now.

I haven’t fired a shot yet. For somebody like me this war is a war of screens and numbers and reports. But there are people on the other side of the screens. Today a report came in that a big intersection in Tehran was bombed and a lot of civilians died. The UN announced that seven hundred thousand people have left their homes in Lebanon. When I read those numbers I try not to forget that numbers are people. Some days that’s hard.

Today the U.S. military announced it hit several Iranian boats that were trying to lay mines in the strait. Nobody can say for sure whether mines have already been laid. The ships whose names I know are floating on that water. The captains ask when they can get out. I tell them I don’t know. I never knew how hard it is to say I don’t know.

There’s something you said to me the day I was commissioned. A soldier is a man who follows orders, but if he loses the strength to think about whether the orders are right, he is no longer a soldier. These days I learn that again every day. To keep the strength to think, you can’t look away. That’s what I’m doing here.

Please stay well. Give my regards to Mother, too.

Your son, Junho

He folded the letter and put it in an envelope. He could have sent it by email. He wrote it by hand anyway. A handwritten letter has something an email does not. The shake in the writing. The bleed of the ink. That was the temperature in Bahrain at 11:40 that night. There was no telling when military mail would run again. Still, someday his father would recognize that shake. On the ships tied up in the Gulf, someone was surely folding the same kind of envelope tonight.

We usually understand war as arrows on a map. But what lives through a war—always—is the letter in the envelope.

Tehran — residential streets south of Sharif University of Technology
March 11, 2026 (Wed.) · 4:20 p.m. (Tehran local time) — War Day 12 · Six days before Chaharshanbe Suri
Scene 7 / 8

Soraya Tahir was hauling a broken door.

Firewood. Door frames, chair legs, fruit crates from bombed buildings. She stacked it in the basement storeroom of the boardinghouse with three friends. Six days from now was Chaharshanbe Suri. The night before the last Wednesday of the year. The night you light fires in every alley and jump over them. My yellow to you, your red to me. A three-thousand-year-old charm: give your sickness and bad luck to the fire and take the fire’s strength.

“We’re doing it this year too?” a friend asked. “There’s plenty of fire falling out of the sky already.”

“That’s why we’re doing it,” Soraya said. “Because that’s their fire, and this is ours.”

There was a checkpoint at the end of the alley. It had gone up three days ago. A few sandbags, oil drums, a flag. Four people standing there. Two of them were boys. Their rifles were too long for their bodies. One of them, rifle on his shoulder, took sunflower seeds out of his pocket and cracked them.

On March 11, Israeli drones struck checkpoints inside Tehran, killing at least ten people. Several members of the Basij were among them. One of the dead was eleven-year-old Alireza Jafari. Citing the boy’s death, Amnesty International said the Iranian authorities’ systematic mobilization of children that age was a war crime. The same day, gas stations and fuel depots in Tehran also came under air attack.

Soraya set down the door and looked at the boy. The boy looked at her too. It was not the look you give a woman without a hijab. It was the look you give a big sister carrying a big door. He spat out a husk and shyly shifted the rifle on his shoulder.

She could not hate that boy. That made her angry. The people who dragged her off in 2022 were adults. They were easy to hate. This boy looked about twelve. Somebody had put a gun in his hands and stood him next to an oil drum. The schools taught that the boy Basijis of the Iran-Iraq war were martyrs. Children who went across the minefields first. The children in the textbooks were standing at the end of the alley now. A machine looking down from the sky would not tell a child from a man.

“Hey,” she called before she knew it. “Don’t stand there. Go home.”

The kid pretended not to hear. The man next to him glared her way. Her friend pulled Soraya by the arm.

That night word passed from mouth to mouth that other checkpoints in the city had been hit. Nobody knew which. Soraya walked to the end of the alley. The sandbags were still there. The people were not. Sunflower husks were scattered on the ground.

To have an enemy you cannot hate. That was the heaviest thing this war had given her.

Tehran — University of Tehran, Department of Philosophy office, Building 16
March 11, 2026 (Wed.) · 9:30 p.m. (Tehran local time) — War Day 12, night
Scene 8 / 8 — Final

Professor Azad Moradi’s notebook was finished. The last page and the inside of the back cover were both full. One notebook in fifty-three years.

He took a bundle of lecture notes out of the drawer. The lecture he had meant to give on the morning of February 28. Kant’s Perpetual Peace. The lecture never happened. The lecture-hall door had been locked ever since. The backs of the notes were blank. The back of a lecture on peace. There was no better paper for writing down a war.

Soot had settled on the windowsill. The rain three days ago had come down black. An oily film covered every leaf of the plane trees in the courtyard. He ran his finger along the sill. His fingertip came away black. What had burned was oil. What came off on his finger was this city’s spring.

He picked up his pen and wrote on the back of the notes.

March 11, 2026. Day twelve of the war. Two days ago the new leader’s name was announced. The son. At twenty-six I watched a king driven out. We believed then that we were making a country where a son would never again take his father’s place. In forty-seven years we have come back to the same place. Only this time a turban instead of a crown.

On the same day, they say, Chehel Sotoun and Ali Qapu in Isfahan were damaged. At nineteen I first saw the twenty columns of Chehel Sotoun reflected in its pool. Count the reflections too and there are forty, the guide said. A palace made of half reality and half shadow. That is exactly what this country is like. The bombs broke the half that is real. The half that is shadow is still inside us.

Today I saw the children standing at the checkpoints. They are my grandchildren’s age. As a man who teaches, I must answer for what this country has taught its children. I cannot answer. Not even after close to half a century of teaching.

He put down the pen. There was an explosion far off. Closer than that, on some rooftop, someone began to sing. The spring song from five days ago.

He was born in 1953. The year Mossadegh was thrown out. The coup that restored the Pahlavi throne. A coup America and Britain had a hand in. A child born that year had turned seventy-two and was watching another foreign intervention. History was a circle—but not exactly the same circle. It was a spiral. It came round past the same place, each time at a slightly different height. Whether you could call that progress—he did not know. But he could tell it was a spiral.

And on the spiral, spring came every year. In 1953, in 1979, in 2009, in 2022. And in 2026—spring was coming. Into the gunfire. Into the explosions. Into the black smoke. Spring did not ask permission. Spring just came.

That was the last thing Azad Moradi learned after close to half a century of teaching philosophy. Truth does not come from teaching—it comes from living. The students singing on rooftops and stacking firewood in alleys knew that truth better than anyone.

At the same moment it was three in the morning on March 12 in Seoul. Kang Yujin had not gone home. She downloaded the public ship-position data and fed it to her model. It was not work. She was just curious. Whether the predictor she had built could tell how the hundreds of dots stopped inside the Gulf would move tomorrow. The model gave its answer. Not moving. She looked at those two words for a long time. Why was the machine so sure? What could the people inside those dots be sure of tomorrow?

War Situation
War Day 12 — What Has Changed and What Has Not

What has changed is the target. When Israel bombed Iran’s oil facilities for the first time on March 8, the war moved from a phase of destroying military installations to a phase of taking aim at a country’s livelihood. Iran answered in the same grammar. On March 9 the refinery in Bahrain was hit, and on March 10 the Abu Dhabi National Oil Co. (ADNOC) shut down the Ruwais refinery after a drone attack set it on fire. On March 11 the fuel tanks at Oman’s Salalah port were hit and port operations were suspended.

What has not changed is the strait. On March 10 the U.S. military said it had struck several Iranian minelayers. On March 11 the bulk carrier Mayuree Naree, just out of the strait, was hit by two missiles and caught fire, and the Star Gwyneth and the One Majesty were also hit nearby. Marine insurance rates jumped in a week to four to six times what they had been.

The Lebanon front was turning into a war of its own. UNHCR announced that by March 10 about 700,000 people in Lebanon had left their homes. And Iran had a new Supreme Leader. What he wanted, no one knew until his first statement was read aloud on March 12.

Author’s Note
The Strike on the Oil Facilities (March 8)Real event
The Israeli Air Force bombed Iran’s oil facilities for the first time since the start of the war. Four people were killed and the sky over Tehran was covered in black smoke; concerns were raised about fuel shortages, contamination of water and soil, and respiratory harm. This episode’s title, “Israel’s Choice,” refers to that shift in targets. The passage in which the Israeli side inquires about Leila’s Fordow report is the author’s invention.
The Election of Mojtaba Khamenei (March 9)Real event
On March 9 the Assembly of Experts elected Mojtaba Khamenei, the second son of Ali Khamenei, as the new Supreme Leader. The first statement issued in his name was read by an announcer on state television on March 12. The “first message” Darius receives in the story is the author’s invention.
Chehel Sotoun and Ali QapuReal heritage site / Isfahan
Palaces of the seventeenth-century Safavid dynasty. Chehel Sotoun (چهل ستون) means “Forty Columns”; the name comes from its twenty columns, which become forty when reflected in the pool in front. Both palace museums were badly damaged in the March 9 airstrikes on Isfahan.
The Manama Explosion (March 9)Real event / Bahrain
An explosion and fire close to a refining facility near Manama injured at least 32 people. An analysis reviewed by Reuters found it likely that a U.S.-operated Patriot interceptor, which shot down an Iranian drone, was involved.
The Children at the Checkpoints (March 11)Real event
Israeli drone strikes on checkpoints in Tehran killed at least ten people, among them eleven-year-old Alireza Jafari. Amnesty International characterized the Iranian authorities’ systematic mobilization of children as a war crime. The child Soraya meets is a character invented by the author and is not a portrayal of an actual victim.
Chaharshanbe SuriReal holiday / Iranian culture
“Red Wednesday.” A custom of Zoroastrian origin: on the eve of the last Wednesday of the Persian calendar year, people light fires and jump over them to burn away the year’s bad luck. In 2026 it fell on the night of Tuesday, March 17.
The Merchant Ships in the StraitReal event
On March 10 the U.S. military announced that it had struck several Iranian minelayers. On March 11 the bulk carrier Mayuree Naree was hit by two missiles just after passing through the strait, and the Star Gwyneth and the One Majesty were damaged. The premise that Kim Junho keeps a tally of Korean-linked ships, and the captain’s remark, are the author’s invention.
1953History / Iran
The year Prime Minister Mohammad Mosaddegh was overthrown in a coup in which the U.S. CIA and British intelligence had a hand. In the story, Azad Moradi is a character born that year.
Daylight Saving TimeTime notation
The United States began daylight saving time on Sunday, March 8, 2026. From this episode on, Eastern time is EDT (UTC−4), and the difference from Tehran is 7 hours 30 minutes.
